Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) Lester B. Pearson; (b) the Avro CF-105 Arrow; (c) the Multiculturalism Policy 1971.
Answer Key

(a) Pearson — Canadian diplomat, Nobel Peace Prize 1957 for UNEF at Suez; Liberal PM 1963–68 (Pearson Pennant flag 1965, CPP 1965, Medicare 1966, Centennial 1967). (b) Avro Arrow — Mach-2 Canadian-built interceptor cancelled Feb 1959 (Black Friday); destruction of prototypes politically controversial. (c) Multiculturalism Policy 1971 — Trudeau's HoC statement creating federal policy of cultural pluralism within bilingualism; codified in 1988 Act.

2
[2]
The 1967 Immigration Act introduced what selection framework, and what was its main effect?
Answer Key

A. The points system replaced explicit racial preference (in place since 1947) with race-neutral selection. Top source countries shifted to include India, the Philippines, Hong Kong, Jamaica, Trinidad, Portugal by 1981.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) NORAD; (b) Hydro-Québec under Lévesque; (c) Calder v. BC 1973.
Answer Key

(a) NORAD — North American Air Defense Command 1957–58, US-Canadian bilateral; HQ at Cheyenne Mountain; Canadian deputy commander. (b) Hydro-Québec — Crown corporation expanded 1962 under René Lévesque to nationalise all private hydroelectric companies; campaign slogan "Maîtres chez nous" (Masters in our own house). (c) Calder v. BC 1973 — Supreme Court of Canada decision in which 6 of 7 judges recognised Aboriginal title existed in principle after the Royal Proclamation 1763; triggered modern land-claims process.

4
[3]
Briefly identify three pieces of social legislation enacted between 1944 and 1966 that became the postwar Canadian welfare state.
Answer Key

(1) Family Allowance 1944 (first universal cash transfer). (2) Unemployment Insurance Act 1940 (operational 1942; expanded postwar). (3) Old Age Security 1951 (universal pension). (4) Canada/Quebec Pension Plan 1965. (5) Medical Care Act 1966 (universal physician-services coverage). (6) Canada Assistance Plan 1966 (federal-provincial cost-shared social assistance). Any three with explanation.

5
[2]
Who was Harold Cardinal, and what is his connection to the 1969 White Paper?
Answer Key

Harold Cardinal (Cree, Alberta), founder of the Indian Association of Alberta, authored The Unjust Society (1969) and Citizens Plus (the "Red Paper," 1970) in direct response to Trudeau's White Paper. He argued treaties were nation-to-nation agreements with constitutional standing, not colonial paternalism. His leadership, with the National Indian Brotherhood, forced Trudeau to formally withdraw the White Paper in 1971.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"Just watch me." — Pierre Trudeau, 13 October 1970, when asked how far he would go in the October Crisis.
Apply the historical thinking concept of cause-and-consequence: in 5–6 sentences, evaluate the 16 October 1970 invocation of the War Measures Act. Was it necessary? Identify two arguments for and two against.
Answer Key

For: (1) Cross and Laporte were both being held; the FLQ had committed previous lethal violence (estimated six deaths from 200+ bombings since 1963); (2) Quebec municipal and provincial authorities formally requested federal action; (3) public opinion supported the WMA at the time — 87% English-Canadian, 86% Quebec. Against: (1) the FLQ was small (estimated 35–40 active members), and 497 detentions without charge largely caught artists and intellectuals with no FLQ links — Tommy Douglas called it "using a sledgehammer to crack a peanut"; (2) Laporte's murder was not prevented by the WMA; the FLQ likely collapsed through internal causes; (3) civil-liberties precedent costs were significant — the 1988 Emergencies Act with parliamentary review replaced the WMA, in part because of this episode. A Level-4 response weighs both and notes the legal-architecture lesson. Marks: 1 each for two arguments-for, two arguments-against, 1 for analytical framing.

7
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of continuity-and-change: in 4–5 sentences, trace how Canadian immigration policy changed from 1923 (the Chinese Immigration Act) to 1976 (the comprehensive Immigration Act). Identify the principal change and one continuity.
Answer Key

Principal change: from explicit racial selection (1923 Chinese Immigration Act; 1947 King statement preferring British/French/American/NW European; 1962 removal of racial criteria; 1967 points system) to race-neutral but credentialed selection. The 1976 Immigration Act added family-reunification and refugee streams as distinct categories. Continuity: federal immigration policy has consistently been an instrument of state nation-building, favouring particular skill profiles and adaptability assessments — not "open borders." The points system itself privileges credentialed urban applicants, producing demographic outcomes that are race-neutral in criteria but not race-blind in outcomes (e.g., language-test requirements affect specific source countries differently). A strong answer notes this distinction. Marks: 2 principal change, 1 continuity, 1 conventions.

8
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of historical perspective: in 4 sentences, reconstruct why Pierre Trudeau and Jean Chrétien thought the 1969 White Paper was a progressive measure. What framework did they apply, and why was it inadequate?
Answer Key

Trudeau's framework was civic egalitarianism — drawing on his liberal philosophy that special legal status was the basis of inequality. He read the Indian Act as a discriminatory apparatus enforcing separation; equal citizenship through termination of special status was, in his framework, the rights-respecting choice. The framework was inadequate because it treated treaties as colonial paternalism rather than as nation-to-nation agreements with binding legal force, and it ignored First Nations' own understanding of their constitutional position. The 1971 withdrawal — after First Nations organisations under Cardinal and NIB articulated the rejection — was an example of good-faith course-correction when the misframing was visible. A Level-4 answer respects Trudeau's framework without endorsing it. Marks: 1 framework, 1 logic, 1 inadequacy, 1 course-correction.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (7–8 sentences) on the claim: "Pearson's 1956 Suez intervention reflected genuine 'middle-power' diplomacy, but Canada's actual peacekeeping record since 1956 has not matched the founding ideal." Use specific evidence.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear topic sentence agreeing or qualifying the claim; (2) specific 1956 evidence — Pearson's UN proposal, 2 November 1956 GA approval 57–0, UNEF deployment under Burns; Nobel Prize 1957; (3) "middle power" components — military significance but not great power, no colonial baggage, federal-bilingual diplomatic training, UN-multilateral focus; (4) post-1990 evidence — Rwanda 1994 (Dallaire, UNAMIR, 2,500 troops, inadequate mandate and political backing); Somalia Affair 1992–93; Srebrenica 1995; by 2020, under 100 Canadians on UN missions; (5) shift toward NATO commitments (Afghanistan, Latvia) over UN blue helmets; (6) analytical close: the founding moment was real; the long retreat is also real; both are part of the honest story. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 1956 evidence, 2 later evidence, 1 conventions.

10
[6]
Write a coherent 8–10 sentence answer on the claim: "The 1962–1967 transformation of Canadian immigration policy was the most consequential federal policy change of the postwar era." Address both immigration shift and one other major change (e.g., Medicare, multiculturalism, the Quiet Revolution) and rank them.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) discussion of immigration shift — 1962 race removed from regulations, 1967 points system, demographic consequences (top source countries shift from UK/Italy 1961 to India/Philippines/Hong Kong/Jamaica by 1981; Toronto foreign-born ~25% in 1961 to ~50% by 2001); (2) treatment of an alternative candidate — Medicare 1966 (universal-physician services from Saskatchewan model), the Quiet Revolution (Hydro-Québec, Education Ministry, Pension Plan), the 1971 Multiculturalism Policy; (3) a defensible ranking — strong arguments exist for either order, and the response should justify its choice; (4) consideration of how the changes interacted (e.g., points-system immigration combined with multiculturalism policy produced distinctively Canadian patterns of integration). A Level-4 response makes a defensible ranking. Marks: 1 thesis/ranking, 2 immigration evidence, 2 alternative-candidate evidence, 1 interaction-analysis.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
In 2024, Statistics Canada reported that newcomers (those who immigrated in the past 5 years) accounted for over 4% of the Canadian population — the highest share since the early 20th century. The federal Immigration Levels Plan for 2024–26 targeted 485,000 new permanent residents annually.
Apply your understanding of the 1967 points system to current immigration debate: in 5–6 sentences, identify (a) one continuity between 1967 and today; (b) one significant change; (c) one challenge the current system faces that the 1967 system did not.
Answer Key

(a) Continuity: race-neutral selection by credentialed criteria; federal control over levels and selection; strong economic-class component. (b) Change: the integration of family-reunification and refugee streams (1976 onward), the introduction of the Express Entry system (2015), the expansion of Provincial Nominee Programmes (1990s–2020s) shifting selection partly to provinces, the targeting of specific labour-market needs. (c) Current challenge that 1967 did not face: very high recent volumes have created housing-supply pressure that 1967 did not have; international-student-credential issues (post-2010 expansion); the post-pandemic affordability crisis is partly attributed to demographic absorption capacity. A Level-4 response engages the substance, not the politics. Marks: 1 continuity, 1 change, 2 current-challenge analysis, 1 conventions.

12
[4]
Apply your understanding of the FLQ Crisis to a current Canadian civil-liberties debate (e.g., the 2022 Emergencies Act invocation, Bill 21 in Quebec, online-harms legislation). In 4–5 sentences, identify one lesson from 1970 that is or should be applied today.
Answer Key

Example lessons: (1) the 1970 detention of 497 people, the vast majority not FLQ-affiliated, demonstrated that emergency powers tend to be over-used against political dissent broadly; current frameworks should narrow targeting and require independent oversight. (2) The 1970 invocation produced lasting trust damage between Quebec and the federal government; current emergency action should be paired with explicit civil-liberties protections. (3) The 1988 Emergencies Act replaced the WMA partly because of 1970 lessons, adding parliamentary review and the Public Order Emergency Commission framework — a continuity that should be preserved and strengthened. A strong answer engages a specific contemporary issue with a clear 1970 lesson. Marks: 1 specific current issue, 2 specific 1970 lesson, 1 conventions.

13
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: Tommy Douglas and the CCF designed Saskatchewan's 1962 Medicare programme. Canadian Medicare turned 60 in 2026. In 3–4 sentences, identify one strength and one weakness of the current Canadian Medicare system that you have observed or read about.
Answer Key

Possible strengths: universal coverage at point of care; relatively low per-capita health-spending compared to U.S.; equity of basic access. Possible weaknesses: long wait times for specialists and elective surgeries; uneven access to family physicians (about 17% of Canadians without a regular GP, 2024); long-term-care system stresses exposed by COVID-19; pharmacare gaps. Strong responses make a specific observation. Marks: 1 strength, 1 weakness, 1 conventions.
