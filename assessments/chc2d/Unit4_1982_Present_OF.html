Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]

[3]
Identify each: (a) the Kitchen Accord (November 1981); (b) the notwithstanding clause (s.33); (c) the 1995 Clarity Act.
Answer Key

(a) Kitchen Accord — overnight November 1981 agreement between federal Justice Minister Chrétien and provincial AGs Romanow (SK) and McMurtry (ON) that secured nine-province agreement on patriation; Quebec was excluded (Lévesque's "Night of the Long Knives"). (b) Notwithstanding clause — section 33; allows legislature to enact legislation despite Charter conflict with sections 2 or 7–15, renewable every 5 years. (c) Clarity Act 2000 — federal statute defining requirements for any future Quebec secession referendum (clear question, clear majority, negotiation).

2
[2]
The Meech Lake Accord (1987) proposed which five elements?
Answer Key

A. The five Quebec demands articulated by Premier Robert Bourassa, secured by Mulroney's negotiation with all 10 premiers in April 1987. Triple-E Senate and Indigenous self-government were 1992 Charlottetown components, not 1987 Meech.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) the Nisga'a Final Agreement 2000; (b) the 94 Calls to Action; (c) Operation Athena.
Answer Key

(a) Nisga'a Final Agreement 2000 — first modern BC treaty; created Nisga'a Lisims Government over 1,930 km² in Nass Valley; $190M compensation; ended Indian Act application to the Nisga'a Nation. (b) 94 Calls to Action — TRC's December 2015 recommendations across child welfare, education, language, health, justice, reconciliation, business, sports, media, museums, missing children, and Vatican apology. ~13 fully implemented as of 2024. (c) Operation Athena — Canadian Forces operation in Afghanistan 2003–2014 (combat to 2011, training to 2014).

4
[3]
Briefly identify three major Indigenous-rights legal or political moments between 1982 and 2024.
Answer Key

(1) Section 35 of Constitution Act 1982 — entrenches "existing aboriginal and treaty rights." (2) Sparrow v. The Queen 1990 — section 35 rights can be limited only with justified infringement; Crown has fiduciary duty. (3) Oka Crisis 1990. (4) RCAP 1991–96. (5) Delgamuukw 1997 — oral histories admissible; aboriginal title is sui generis. (6) Nisga'a Final Agreement 2000. (7) Harper apology 2008. (8) Tsilhqot'in 2014 — first declaration of aboriginal title at common law. (9) TRC Final Report 2015. (10) UNDRIP Act 2021. Any three with brief explanation.

5
[2]
Why did Prime Minister Chrétien decline to join the 2003 U.S.-led invasion of Iraq?
Answer Key

Chrétien's 17 March 2003 announcement cited: (1) lack of UN Security Council authorisation; (2) unproven weapons-of-mass-destruction claims; (3) failure to meet international-law threshold for war. The decision aligned with Pearsonian multilateralism. Retrospective vindication: Iraqi WMD did not exist; the war's costs were vast for all parties. Canada had been a full participant in NATO's Afghanistan operation since 2001, which had UN Security Council Resolution 1373 support and Article 5 invocation — the distinction was legal authority, not blanket opposition to U.S. operations.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"We have not the right to remain insulated from the imperatives of action." — Lucien Bouchard, addressing Quebeckers, October 1995.
Apply the historical thinking concept of cause-and-consequence: in 5–6 sentences, explain why the 1995 Quebec referendum produced a 50.58%–49.42% result. Identify two short-term and two long-term causes.
Answer Key

Short-term causes: (1) Bouchard's September–October campaign mobilisation and the late designation as chief sovereignty-negotiator galvanised the Yes; (2) federal complacency in early campaign followed by the late Unity Rally response (27 October). Long-term causes: (1) Quebec's 1982 non-signature creating a persistent grievance; (2) the failed Meech (1990) and Charlottetown (1992) reform attempts; (3) Bloc Québécois 1993 federal-election success (54 seats, Official Opposition); (4) PQ provincial-election win 1994 under Parizeau. Result: 50.58% No, 49.42% Yes — margin of 54,288 votes. Consequences: Clarity Act 2000; declining sovereigntist support through 2000s and 2010s; Quebec nation-recognition motion 2006. Marks: 2 short-term causes, 2 long-term causes, 1 analytical synthesis.

7
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of continuity-and-change: in 4–5 sentences, trace how Canadian Indigenous-rights jurisprudence has evolved from section 35 of the Constitution Act 1982 to the 2014 Tsilhqot'in decision. Identify one continuity and one major change.
Answer Key

Section 35 (1982) entrenched "existing aboriginal and treaty rights" but left their substance undefined. The change: court decisions filled in the substance — Sparrow (1990) established fiduciary duty and justified-infringement test; Vander Peet (1996) added the "integral to distinctive culture" test; Delgamuukw (1997) admitted oral histories and characterised aboriginal title as sui generis; Tsilhqot'in (2014) was the first common-law declaration of aboriginal title (1,750 km² in BC's Chilcotin region). The continuity: the section 35 framework — rights-recognition through litigation and case-by-case definition — has remained the principal mechanism; the policy framework (UNDRIP 2021, TRC implementation, modern treaties) has supplemented but not replaced it. A Level-4 response notes both the legal progress and the slow pace. Marks: 1 continuity, 2 change examples, 1 synthesis.

Marks: 1 specific Call, 1 current state, 1 responsibility, 1 conventions.

Write one analytical paragraph (7–8 sentences) on the claim: "The 1982 entrenchment of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms is the most consequential constitutional change in Canadian history." Use specific evidence including at least one Charter case.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear topic sentence agreeing, qualifying, or contesting the claim; (2) specific Charter cases — Oakes 1986 (section 1 reasonable-limits test), Morgentaler 1988 (abortion law struck down), Schachter 1992 (remedies doctrine), Vriend 1998 (sexual orientation as analogous ground under s.15), Reference re Same-Sex Marriage 2004 (constitutional foundation for marriage redefinition); (3) consideration of section 33 use — Quebec Bills 21 and 96, Saskatchewan and Ontario use 2017+; (4) comparison with other candidates — Confederation 1867, the 1949 Newfoundland union, the 1965 flag, the 1965 CPP, the 1966 Medicare framework; (5) a defensible position. A Level-4 response holds the claim against alternatives. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 Charter case evidence, 2 alternative-comparison, 1 conventions.

10
[6]
Write a coherent 8–10 sentence answer on the claim: "The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) has done more for reconciliation than the Constitution Act 1982 section 35 did in the previous quarter-century." Use specific evidence.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear position; (2) TRC evidence — 6,750+ statements, 94 Calls to Action, the residential-schools record made public, 2008 federal apology, 2022 Pope Francis apology at Maskwacis; (3) section 35 evidence — Sparrow 1990, Delgamuukw 1997, Tsilhqot'in 2014, modern treaties (Nisga'a 2000, Tla'amin 2016), Indigenous self-government recognition in principle; (4) honest weighing — TRC has shifted public conversation but Calls implementation is slow (~13/94 by 2024); section 35 has provided the legal architecture but reservation conditions remain unchanged; (5) a synthesis: the two work together, the question is overstated. A Level-4 response rejects either-or framing and articulates the relationship. Marks: 1 position, 3 TRC evidence, 2 section 35 evidence, 1 synthesis.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
In 2022, the federal government invoked the Emergencies Act for the first time in peacetime in response to the Freedom Convoy occupation of Ottawa and border crossings (14–23 February). The Rouleau Commission's 2024 report concluded the invocation was legally justified.
Apply your understanding of the FLQ Crisis (1970), Japanese-Canadian internment (1942), and the WWI internment (1914–17) to the 2022 invocation: in 5–6 sentences, identify two key differences in the legal framework available in 2022, and one continuing concern.
Answer Key

Differences: (1) 1914 and 1942 internments used the broad War Measures Act with virtually no parliamentary oversight; the 1988 Emergencies Act replaced the WMA with mandatory parliamentary review (within 30 sitting days), automatic expiry, judicial review by the Federal Court, and a mandatory independent commission of inquiry (the Rouleau Commission). (2) Charter protections (Charter sections 2, 7, 9, 15) constrain emergency-power use, and the Federal Court reviewed the 2022 invocation in 2024 (initially finding it unjustified; the federal government appealed). (3) The 2022 measures lasted nine days, not nine years as in coastal Japanese-Canadian exclusion. Continuing concern: emergency powers, however reformed, tend to broaden against political dissent (Trudeau's 2022 invocation targeted bank accounts of supporters without judicial warrant — a power without 1942 precedent). A Level-4 response shows the institutional learning across a century without dismissing remaining concerns. Marks: 2 differences, 1 continuing concern, 2 historical synthesis.

12
[4]
Apply your understanding of CUSFTA (1989) and NAFTA (1994) to the 2020 USMCA and the 2025 Trump-era tariff threats: in 4–5 sentences, identify one continuing principle and one significant change in Canada-US economic relations.
Answer Key

Continuing principle: bilateral integration through tariff reduction and standardised rules of origin has been the structural goal since 1989; trade flows (~$735B in 2017) confirm the integration. Significant change: the 1989 framework presumed continued US adherence to liberal trade rules; 2017–onward Trump-era tariff threats showed those rules can be revisited by either side, weakening the certainty Canadian exporters had counted on. USMCA's sunset clause (16 years, 6-year review) is itself a structural acknowledgement that trade arrangements may be re-opened. A Level-4 response identifies the structural shift toward uncertainty. Marks: 1 continuing principle, 2 significant change, 1 conventions.
