Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Name and briefly define the six historical thinking concepts (Seixas).
Answer Key

(1) Historical significance — criteria for what made an event/person matter (result-in-change, revealing, resonant). (2) Cause and consequence — identifying multiple, ranked causes and distinguishing foreseen/unforeseen consequences. (3) Continuity and change — what has changed, what has stayed the same, at what pace. (4) Historical perspective — reconstructing how past actors understood their world without anachronism. (5) Evidence — using primary and secondary sources to support claims, with attention to origin and context. (6) Ethical dimensions — making fair judgments about past conduct and present responsibilities.

2
[2]
What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source?
Answer Key

A. Primary sources are produced during the period being studied; secondary sources are later analyses based on primary evidence. Source classification is relative to the question — a 1980 textbook can be a primary source about 1980 historiography even as it is a secondary source about 1917 events.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) OPVL; (b) Triangulation; (c) Presentism.
Answer Key

(a) OPVL — Origin (who produced this when and where?), Purpose (why?), Value (what does it tell us?), Limitations (what does it not tell us, and what could mislead?). (b) Triangulation — corroborating a claim across multiple independent sources to raise confidence in the claim. (c) Presentism — applying contemporary values, knowledge, or expectations to past actors as though they shared our framework; a methodological error opposite to historical perspective.

4
[3]
List three components of a strong historical question (as opposed to a closed yes/no question).
Answer Key

(1) Open-ended: invites investigation rather than a one-word answer. (2) Specific and bounded: identifies a time period, group, event, or geography (e.g., "shift in public opinion between 1970 and 1980" rather than "what did people think"). (3) Defensible by evidence: can be supported or contested with sources rather than depending only on personal preference. (4) Invites application of historical-thinking concepts (significance, cause-and-consequence, etc.). (5) Identifies multiple perspectives. Any three.

5
[2]
Explain in 2 sentences why historians treat omissions in primary sources as themselves evidence.
Answer Key

What a source does not say is often as informative as what it says. F. C. Blair's correspondence on Jewish immigration is sparse on moral reasoning — the omission reveals the assumed legitimacy of the policy. Mackenzie King's 1937 diary on Hitler captures appeasement framing precisely by what he does not note. Reading absences requires attention; this is what source-criticism distinguishes from naive textual reading.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"It is not the strongest of the species that survives, nor the most intelligent, but the one most responsive to change." — Frequently misattributed to Charles Darwin (the actual source is Leon Megginson, 1963).
Apply Caulfield's SIFT method to this quotation. In 5–6 sentences, explain how a historian or careful reader would: (a) Stop; (b) Investigate the source; (c) Find better coverage; (d) Trace claims to original context. What does this case study reveal about citation in the social-media era?
Answer Key

(a) Stop: pause before resharing or citing. (b) Investigate source: the quotation is widely attributed to Darwin on the internet but does not appear in any verifiable Darwin source. (c) Find better coverage: search "Darwin survival fittest most responsive" → multiple fact-checking sites (Snopes, Quote Investigator) document the misattribution. (d) Trace: the actual source is Leon Megginson, "Lessons from Europe for American Business," Southwestern Social Science Quarterly (1963), pp. 3–13 — a paraphrase, not a quotation, of Darwin. What this reveals: false attribution lends authority to claims; verification takes ten minutes; citation integrity matters because the actual claim (a 1963 management-theory paraphrase) is materially different from the Darwinian framing (1859 natural-selection theory). The historian's discipline of source-trace is the antidote. Marks: 1 each of S, I, F, T; 1 for synthesis.

7
[4]
Two historians take opposite positions on Vimy Ridge's "birth of a nation" reading. Pierre Berton's Vimy (1986) emphasises the nation-building narrative; Tim Cook's Vimy: The Battle and the Legend (2017) treats the narrative as a postwar construction. Apply the historical thinking concept of historical significance: in 4–5 sentences, explain how the two historians can examine the same evidence and reach different syntheses. What does this reveal about historical interpretation?
Answer Key

Both historians have access to the same primary evidence — war diaries, Currie's after-action reports, casualty figures, 1936 dedication speeches. They reach different syntheses because they ask different questions and apply different criteria of significance. Berton asks "what does Vimy mean to Canadian identity?" — a meaning-making question that foregrounds significance-as-resonance. Cook asks "how was the Vimy-as-nation-birth narrative constructed?" — a historiographical question that foregrounds significance-as-cultural-construction. Neither reading is wrong; they answer different questions. This reveals that historiography is contestation among honest readings, not a single correct answer. A Level-4 response shows the discipline accepting multiple defensible interpretations. Marks: 1 different questions, 1 different criteria, 1 neither wrong, 1 historiographical implication.

8
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of ethical dimensions: in 4 sentences, explain why "fair historical judgment" is a discipline rather than a personal opinion. Use one example from Units 1–4 (e.g., Japanese-Canadian internment, residential schools, the MS St. Louis, the FLQ Crisis).
Answer Key

"Fair judgment" requires structured criteria: (1) the moral resources available to actors at the time; (2) whether the harm was foreseeable; (3) whether alternatives existed within political constraints; (4) what responsibilities the judgment creates today. Applied to Japanese-Canadian internment: 1942 actors had RCMP intelligence finding no security threat, contemporary civil-liberties critics, and the alternative of selective rather than blanket internment. The harm was foreseeable; the moral resources were available; alternative existed. The fair judgment is that internment was wrong by 1942 standards, not just hindsight — which is consistent with the 1988 redress and the 1988 Emergencies Act replacing the WMA. A Level-4 response shows the discipline of structured judgment. Marks: 1 criteria, 2 example application, 1 synthesis.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write a 7–8 sentence analytical paragraph on the following claim: "The skills of historical source-criticism are the same skills needed for media literacy in 2026." Use specific examples from both historical sources (from any unit) and contemporary media.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear topic sentence supporting, qualifying, or contesting the claim; (2) historical source-criticism examples — OPVL applied to Borden's 1917 speech, F. C. Blair's correspondence, the 1942 P.C. 1486 Order-in-Council; (3) contemporary media examples — applying SIFT to a viral claim, lateral reading on a TikTok or news article; (4) recognition that the discipline (origin, purpose, value, limitations) is the same; the velocity and scale are different; (5) consideration of new contemporary challenges (deep fakes, generative AI, algorithmic curation) that historians did not encounter; (6) a synthesis that the historian's discipline is necessary but may require updating. A strong response affirms the continuity and identifies the specific contemporary additions. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 historical examples, 2 contemporary examples, 1 conventions.

10
[6]
Write a coherent 8–10 sentence response on the following inquiry question: "How has Canadian public memory of one event from Units 1–4 changed between the event itself and 2026?" Choose the event (e.g., Vimy Ridge, the conscription crisis, Japanese-Canadian internment, the 1995 referendum). Use specific evidence about both moments.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a specific event chosen with clear scope; (2) primary-source evidence for the event itself (a contemporary newspaper, government document, photograph); (3) primary-source evidence for 2026 public memory (a museum exhibition, a school curriculum, a documentary, a commemorative artefact); (4) honest description of change — what is commemorated, what is omitted, what is contested; (5) application of historical-thinking concepts (continuity-and-change is most relevant; historical-perspective and ethical-dimensions also apply); (6) a synthesis explaining why the change occurred. Example for Japanese-Canadian internment: 1942 newspapers framed it as wartime security; 1988 redress reframed it as racial injustice; 2018+ school curricula treat it as a foundational civil-liberties case study. Marks: 1 event/scope, 2 1942 sources, 2 2026 evidence, 1 synthesis.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
Apply the historical inquiry process to a current Canadian issue of your choice (climate policy, reconciliation progress, healthcare wait times, immigration debate, electoral reform, etc.). In 5–6 sentences, work through the five steps (formulate, gather, interpret, evaluate, communicate) for one specific aspect of the issue.
Answer Key

Look for the five steps applied: (1) Formulate — a specific, defensible question (e.g., "How has the federal carbon-pricing program changed between 2018 and 2025?"); (2) Gather — naming sources to consult (StatsCan, federal Environment Canada, peer-reviewed climate-economics literature, Supreme Court 2021 References decision); (3) Interpret — identifying perspectives (provincial Alberta-Saskatchewan vs federal vs environmental NGO vs business sector); (4) Evaluate — applying historical thinking (continuity-and-change in environmental policy since Kyoto 1997 → Paris 2015 → Net-Zero 2021); (5) Communicate — naming the format (essay, podcast, infographic) and the audience. A Level-4 response treats a contemporary issue with historical-thinking discipline. Marks: 1 per step.

12
[4]
Apply the historical-thinking concepts to your own family or community history. In 4–5 sentences, identify one event in your family or community that you would investigate, name two primary sources you could gather, and identify which historical-thinking concept would most help your analysis.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a specific event chosen with appropriate scope; (2) two specific primary sources (family photographs, oral history interviews, family letters, census records via Library and Archives Canada, school records, immigration files via the Canadian Museum of History at Pier 21); (3) a justified choice of historical-thinking concept; (4) personal engagement that does not collapse into nostalgia. Marks: 1 specific event, 2 primary sources, 1 concept choice.

STSE / Real-World Application: Identify three specific careers or post-secondary pathways in which the skills you have built in CHC2D (source-criticism, evidence-based argument, ethical judgment, communication) would transfer.
Answer Key

Examples: (1) journalism — investigative reporting requires source-criticism, evidence-triangulation, ethical judgment about anonymous sources and public interest. (2) law — building a legal argument, evaluating witness credibility, applying precedent. (3) policy analysis — assessing whether a proposal is consistent with evidence and prior outcomes. (4) digital marketing — understanding audience perspective, evaluating campaign messaging. (5) medicine — evidence-based diagnosis, ethical patient consent. (6) software engineering — applying primary documentation, evaluating tradeoffs, communicating technical decisions. (7) Indigenous-relations work in business or government — ethical dimensions, historical-perspective, understanding treaty law. Any three careers with brief justification. Marks: 1 per career.
