Topic 4.1 — Patriation and the Charter
Question 1
Patriation (Constitution Act, 1982) means:
Solution: Patriation ended the BNA Act's status as a UK statute and added the Charter (sections 1–34) plus section 35 (Aboriginal rights). The Constitution Act, 1982 took effect 17 April 1982. Quebec has never signed.
Question 2
Section 33 of the Charter (the "notwithstanding clause"):
Solution: The notwithstanding clause was Romanow's concession at the Kitchen Accord (Nov 1981). For decades it was rarely used; since ~2017 use has accelerated (Saskatchewan, Quebec Bills 21 and 96, Ontario), prompting debate about whether the clause's exceptional use has been normalised.
Topic 4.2 — Meech, Charlottetown, 1995 Referendum
Question 3
The Meech Lake Accord (1987–90) collapsed because:
Solution: Both Harper's procedural refusal in the Manitoba Legislature and Wells's stance in Newfoundland prevented unanimous provincial ratification by the June 1990 deadline. Harper's eagle feather is preserved in the Canadian Museum of History. The Bloc Québécois was founded immediately afterward.
Question 4
The 1995 Quebec referendum on sovereignty-association produced:
Solution: The closest separation result in Canadian history. Parizeau's concession speech reference to "money and the ethnic vote" precipitated his resignation; Lucien Bouchard succeeded him. The Clarity Act (2000) defined requirements for any future Quebec secession referendum.
Topic 4.3 — Oka, Nisga'a, and Reconciliation
Question 5
The Oka Crisis (11 July – 26 September 1990):
Solution: Oka was the most televised Indigenous confrontation in Canadian history to that point. SQ Corporal Marcel Lemay was killed in the 11 July assault on the Mohawk barricade. The Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (RCAP) was established in response; its 4,000-page 1996 report largely went unimplemented.
Question 6
The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015):
Solution: The TRC was established under the 2007 Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (~$5 billion compensation). Harper's 2008 House of Commons apology preceded it; Pope Francis's 2022 apology at Maskwacis answered TRC Call to Action #58. As of 2024, ~13 of the 94 Calls are fully implemented.
Topic 4.4–4.5 — NAFTA and Post-9/11
Question 7
The 1988 federal election was effectively a referendum on:
Solution: Turner's "you've sold us out" attack in the leaders' debate made CUSFTA the defining issue. Mulroney's PC majority was reduced (169→ majority) but maintained; CUSFTA took effect 1 Jan 1989. NAFTA followed 1 Jan 1994; USMCA replaced it 1 July 2020.
Question 8
Canadian forces' main combat deployment after 9/11 was to:
Solution: ~40,000 Canadians served in Afghanistan over the 2001–2014 deployment. 158 military died; the combat mission ended July 2011 and the training mission March 2014. Canada did not join the 2003 Iraq invasion — Chrétien's House of Commons announcement of 17 March 2003 cited the lack of UN authorisation.
Question 9
The Maher Arar case (2002–06):
Solution: Maher Arar, a dual Canadian-Syrian citizen, was detained in New York in 2002, rendered to Syria, and tortured. The 2006 O'Connor Inquiry exposed the case; Canada apologised and paid $10.5M settlement. The case exposed the civil-liberties costs of post-9/11 security cooperation.
Question 10
Short answer: In 3 sentences, explain why the Constitution Act, 1982 had both an immediate effect (the Charter operating from April 1982) and a continuing political effect (the Quebec non-signature). What does the dual reading reveal about Canadian federalism?
Sample: The Charter became the operating constitutional law in April 1982 — Charter jurisprudence (Oakes 1986, Morgentaler 1988, Vriend 1998) has since shaped every area of Canadian law. The Quebec non-signature, however, remains an unresolved political fact: Quebec has never formally consented to the Constitution Act, 1982, which is why Meech (1990) and Charlottetown (1992) attempted to bring Quebec in, and why the 1995 referendum and 2006 nation-recognition motion remain part of the same long conversation. Canadian federalism therefore lives a dual reality: the Charter is operative law everywhere, but Quebec's standing within the federation is, structurally, still pending.
