Topic 1.1–1.2 — The Great War and Vimy
Question 1
When Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914, Canada was:
Solution: Canada was automatically at war when Britain declared war. The Statute of Westminster came in 1931, so by 1939 Canada chose to declare war. In 1914 the BNA Act governed, and Canadian foreign policy was made in London.
Question 2
The Battle of Vimy Ridge (9–12 April 1917):
Solution: The four Canadian divisions fought together at Vimy under British Lt-Gen Julian Byng's command. Canadian casualties were 10,602 in four days, including 3,598 dead. The war continued for another 19 months.
Question 3
"In all earnestness and sincerity I plead with you in the name of all that is dear to a Canadian … to give the man at the front a fighting chance."
This 1917 appeal by Prime Minister Robert Borden was part of his case for:
Solution: Borden's 1917 plea was for the Military Service Act, which received Royal Assent on 29 August 1917 — the source of the conscription crisis. The League of Nations followed Versailles in 1919; foreign-policy independence came with the Statute of Westminster in 1931.
Topic 1.3 — The Conscription Crisis
Question 4
The Wartime Elections Act (1917):
Solution: The Wartime Elections Act enfranchised soldiers' female relatives to support conscription, while removing the vote from naturalised citizens from enemy countries. Full federal female enfranchisement came in 1918; Status Indians received the federal vote only in 1960.
Question 5
Henri Bourassa, editor of Le Devoir, opposed conscription on the grounds that:
Solution: Bourassa's argument was structural: Canada was being asked to fight a British imperial war for a state whose Anglo-Protestant majority had already attacked French-language rights (Regulation 17, 1912). This was not pro-German sentiment but a critique of imperial loyalty.
Topic 1.4–1.5 — Halifax Explosion and Women's Suffrage
Question 6
The Halifax Explosion of 6 December 1917 resulted when:
Solution: The SS Mont-Blanc, carrying TNT, picric acid, benzol, and guncotton, collided with the empty SS Imo. The resulting explosion equalled about 2.9 kilotonnes of TNT — the largest human-made explosion before Hiroshima. About 1,782 died instantly; 600 were blinded by flying glass.
Question 7
In the 1929 Persons Case (Edwards v. AG Canada), the JCPC's "living tree" doctrine established that:
Solution: Lord Sankey's landmark judgment used the "living tree" metaphor to interpret "persons" in the BNA Act to include women, by then a socially-evolved meaning. The doctrine later supported Charter interpretations extending rights to LGBTQ Canadians and others.
Topic 1.6 — Winnipeg General Strike
Question 8
"Bloody Saturday" — 21 June 1919 — refers to:
Solution: On Bloody Saturday, the RNWMP charged a peaceful crowd on Main Street; two strikers — Mike Sokolowski and Steve Schezerbanowicz — were killed. The strike was broken three days later. Federal Cabinet had also amended the Immigration Act to deport British-born "agitators" without trial.
Question 9
The Winnipeg General Strike's most significant long-term political consequence was:
Solution: The strike collapsed without immediate gains, but Woodsworth went to Parliament in 1921 and founded the CCF in 1932 — the direct ancestor of today's NDP (1961). The institutional consequence of 1919 unfolded over decades.
Question 10
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain how the conscription crisis of 1917 changed Canadian federal politics in Quebec for decades afterward.
Sample: The Union Government's 1917 election crushed the Quebec-based Laurier Liberals (62 of 82 anti-conscription seats were in Quebec) and tied the federal Conservative party to Anglo-imperial loyalism. Quebec voted Liberal in nearly every federal election from 1917 to 1984 — six and a half decades of alignment shaped by the conscription betrayal. The Quebec memory of forced military service became a touchstone for the 1942 plebiscite, the FLQ era, and the modern PQ.
