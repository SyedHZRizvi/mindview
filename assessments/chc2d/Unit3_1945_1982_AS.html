Topic 3.1–3.2 — Postwar Boom and Cold War
Question 1
Tommy Douglas is most often associated with which Canadian institution?
Solution: Douglas was CCF premier of Saskatchewan 1944–61 and introduced provincial medicare in 1962; the federal Medical Care Act 1966 used Saskatchewan as its model. He became first leader of the federal NDP in 1961.
Question 2
The Avro CF-105 Arrow was cancelled by Diefenbaker's government on 20 February 1959 (Black Friday) because:
Solution: Multiple factors converged. The cancellation was likely justifiable; the destruction order — destroying completed aircraft and tooling — was the political mistake. 14,000 engineers were laid off; many went to NASA's Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo programmes.
Topic 3.3 — Pearson and Peacekeeping
Question 3
"We are now living in a world where conflict between great powers cannot be allowed to continue."
Lester B. Pearson received the Nobel Peace Prize on 11 December 1957 for proposing what at the UN?
Solution: Pearson's UNEF proposal (approved by the General Assembly 57–0 with 19 abstentions on 2 November 1956) became the model for modern UN peacekeeping. Pearson later became Liberal PM 1963–68.
Topic 3.4 — Quiet Revolution and FLQ
Question 4
The Quiet Revolution (Révolution tranquille) under Premier Jean Lesage (1960–66) included:
Solution: The Quiet Revolution was the rapid modernisation of Quebec society — Lesage's campaign slogan was "Il faut que ça change" / "Things must change." René Lévesque championed Hydro-Québec's expansion under the slogan "Maîtres chez nous."
Question 5
The October Crisis of 1970:
Solution: The FLQ Liberation Cell kidnapped Cross on 5 October; the Chenier Cell kidnapped Laporte on 10 October. The WMA was invoked on 16 October at Quebec's request; 497 were detained without charge. Laporte was murdered 17 October. Cross was released 3 December in exchange for safe passage to Cuba.
Topic 3.5–3.6 — Immigration, Multiculturalism, Indigenous Resistance
Question 6
The 1967 Immigration Act under Pearson:
Solution: The 1967 points system replaced explicit racial preference (in place since 1947) with race-neutral selection criteria. The 1962 regulations had removed the racial preference in principle; 1967 implemented the points system in detail. Immigration from Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, and Latin America rose rapidly afterward.
Question 7
On 8 October 1971, Pierre Trudeau announced in the House of Commons:
Solution: The Multiculturalism Policy was codified in the 1988 Canadian Multiculturalism Act. Section 27 of the Charter (1982) directs the Charter to be interpreted "in a manner consistent with the preservation and enhancement of the multicultural heritage of Canadians."
Question 8
The 1969 White Paper (Trudeau-Chrétien) proposed to:
Solution: Trudeau's White Paper framed civic egalitarianism — equal citizenship through termination of special status. Harold Cardinal's Citizens Plus (the "Red Paper," 1970) rejected the framing; the National Indian Brotherhood organised the response; Trudeau withdrew the White Paper in 1971. This episode shaped subsequent Indigenous-federal policymaking.
Question 9
Calder v. British Columbia (1973):
Solution: Calder split the Supreme Court 3–3 on whether the Nisga'a held Aboriginal title; the seventh judge ruled on procedure. But six of seven judges recognised Aboriginal title existed in principle. Trudeau remarked, "perhaps you have more legal rights than we thought you had." Calder triggered the 1975 James Bay Agreement and the modern land-claims process.
Question 10
Short answer: In 3 sentences, explain the difference between bilingualism (Trudeau's 1969 Official Languages Act) and multiculturalism (Trudeau's 1971 policy). Why has Quebec preferred "interculturalism" instead?
Sample: Bilingualism makes French and English equal at the federal level — a two-language framework grounded in the historic British-French duality. Multiculturalism (1971) treats all cultural communities as having recognition within the bilingual framework — a pluralism inside the language duality. Quebec governments have preferred "interculturalism" because they argue cultural pluralism within French as the common public language is more compatible with Quebec's national-language project, whereas full multiculturalism could erode French primacy. The Quebec Bill 21 (2019) on religious symbols and Bill 96 (2022) on language reflect this ongoing tension.
