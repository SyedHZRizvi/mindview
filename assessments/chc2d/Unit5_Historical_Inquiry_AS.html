Topic 5.1–5.2 — Inquiry Process and Six Concepts
Question 1
The Ontario historical-inquiry process consists of five steps. Which is the correct order?
Solution: The five steps move from question to conclusion. The process is recursive — historians often return to earlier steps when new evidence reshapes the question.
Question 2
Peter Seixas (Historical Thinking Project) named six historical thinking concepts. Which list is correct?
Solution: The six concepts are operations a historian performs on evidence, not topics. They are codified in the Ontario CHC2D curriculum (2018).
Question 3
"Presentism" is the methodological error of:
Solution: Presentism is a failure of historical perspective. The opposite error — anachronism — applies later concepts retroactively. Both undercut understanding of how past actors understood their world.
Topic 5.3–5.4 — Primary, Secondary, Historiography
Question 4
A 2024 newspaper article analysing the 1970 October Crisis is:
Solution: Source classification is relative to the question. The 2024 article cannot be primary evidence about 1970 because it post-dates the events; it can be primary evidence about 2024 public-memory dynamics. Many sources are simultaneously primary for one question and secondary for another.
Question 5
"Vimy Ridge was the birth of a nation." — Brigadier-General A. E. Ross, 1936 dedication speech.
A 2017 historian writing about Vimy who quotes Ross's 1936 speech is using:
Solution: Ross's 1936 speech is primary evidence about how the Vimy myth was being constructed at the unveiling of the memorial — not primary evidence about the 1917 battle itself. The distinction matters: a historian using Ross's speech as evidence about 1917 would be confusing the source's reach.
Question 6
Historiography is:
Solution: Historiography studies how interpretations of past events have changed across time and across schools of thought. Canadian historiography includes Donald Creighton's mid-century political-economy, the 1960s–80s social-history turn, the 1980s–2000s women's, ethnic, and Indigenous histories, and the recent transnational and environmental turn.
Topic 5.5–5.6 — Media Literacy and Citation
Question 7
Mike Caulfield's "SIFT" method for evaluating online claims stands for:
Solution: SIFT is essentially the historian's source-criticism procedure applied at social-media velocity. Lateral reading — checking claims across multiple independent sources — is the core operation.
Question 8
In academic history writing, the citation style most commonly used in Canadian peer-reviewed journals is:
Solution: Chicago Notes-Bibliography is the dominant Canadian historical-journal style (e.g., Canadian Historical Review). MLA is more common in English/literature; APA in social sciences. Your teacher will specify which style to use in CHC2D work.
Question 9
Paraphrased material in an essay still requires:
Solution: Citation tracks the source of ideas, not just the source of words. Paraphrasing without citation is plagiarism even if the words are different.
Question 10
Short answer: In 3 sentences, explain how the historian's source-criticism procedure (OPVL: Origin, Purpose, Value, Limitations) transfers to evaluating a TikTok claim about a current Canadian news story.
Sample: The same four questions apply: Origin — who posted this, what's their track record, can the account be verified? Purpose — what does the post want viewers to do (share, donate, vote, get angry)? Value — what useful information is in the post, even if biased? Limitations — what does the post leave out, what context is missing, and what would a fuller picture include? Applying these four to any social-media post produces faster and better critical reading than scrolling on; this is exactly what historians do when evaluating any source.
