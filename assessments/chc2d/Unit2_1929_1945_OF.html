Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) R. B. Bennett; (b) the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan; (c) the Wartime Prices and Trade Board.
Answer Key

(a) R. B. Bennett — Conservative PM 1930–35; relief camps; "Bennett-burghs"; the failed Canadian New Deal of 1935. (b) BCATP — Commonwealth pilot-training programme based in Canada 1940–45; trained 131,000 aircrew at 231 sites; Canada was "the aerodrome of democracy." (c) WPTB — federal wage/price-control agency under Donald Gordon; held wartime inflation to 2.8% annually 1942–45; became template for postwar Keynesian economic management.

2
[2]
The 1942 plebiscite asked Canadians to release Mackenzie King from his 1939 "no overseas conscription" promise. The national result was:
Answer Key

A. 64% Yes nationally, 71.2% Quebec No — exposing the WWI-era English-French divide on overseas conscription. King famously declared "conscription if necessary, but not necessarily conscription"; he did not send conscripts overseas until November 1944, and only ~16,000 actually went.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) the On-to-Ottawa Trek; (b) Order-in-Council P.C. 1486 (1942); (c) the Hunger Winter.
Answer Key

(a) On-to-Ottawa Trek — 1935 protest by relief-camp men who boarded freight trains east; stopped at Regina; the Regina Riot of 1 July killed two. (b) P.C. 1486 — federal order 24 Feb 1942 under the War Measures Act ordering removal of all Japanese Canadians from the 100-mile Pacific coastal zone. (c) Hunger Winter — Dutch famine 1944–45 under late German occupation; 20,000 Dutch died of starvation; First Canadian Army liberated the western Netherlands while Operation Manna airdropped food.

4
[3]
Briefly identify three social programmes created or expanded between 1939 and 1945 that became part of the postwar Canadian welfare state.
Answer Key

(1) Unemployment Insurance 1940 — federal scheme, operational 1942; expanded postwar. (2) Family Allowance 1944 — Canada's first universal cash transfer to mothers; predecessor of later child benefits. (3) Federal-provincial wartime daycare (1942) — Dominion-Provincial Wartime Day Nurseries Agreement; closed after war but precursor of later daycare policy. (4) Wartime Prices and Trade Board mechanisms became postwar monetary-policy templates. Any three with brief explanation.

5
[2]
At which D-Day beach did Canadian forces land on 6 June 1944, and what was the strategic significance of the landings overall?
Answer Key

The 3rd Canadian Division landed 14,000 troops on Juno Beach, one of five D-Day beaches (Utah and Omaha American; Gold and Sword British; Juno Canadian). 359 Canadians died; the Canadian assault penetrated furthest inland of any Allied force on the day. D-Day overall (Operation Overlord) opened the Western Front in Europe, beginning the eleven-month campaign that ended in German surrender on 8 May 1945.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"This is the largest peaceful migration ever to have taken place on this continent." — Justice Sidney Smith, on Japanese-Canadian internment, 1942.
Apply the historical thinking concept of ethical dimensions: in 5–6 sentences, evaluate the fairness of the 1942 internment by the moral resources available at the time. Was it a "wartime necessity" or a policy choice reflecting prior racial politics?
Answer Key

A strong response: (1) Names the moral resources available — the RCMP's February 1942 assessment that no security threat existed; the contrast with treatment of German and Italian Canadians (selective internment of suspected fascists, not blanket removal); existing critics on the record (CCJC, some Catholic and United Church voices). (2) Identifies the political context — long-standing B.C. anti-Asian politics (1885 head tax, 1907 Vancouver riot, 1923 Chinese Immigration Act); B.C. business and labour pressure on King's federal government. (3) Concludes that "wartime necessity" was political cover; the decision reflected prior racial politics dressed in emergency language. (4) Notes the postwar treatment (1945 forced choice between "repatriation" to Japan or "dispersal" east of the Rockies; 3,964 deported to Japan; coastal exclusion to 1949) compounded the original injustice. (5) Acknowledges the 1988 Mulroney redress agreement as institutional recognition of this fair judgment. Marks: 1 for moral resources, 1 for context, 1 for fair-judgment formulation, 1 for postwar consequences, 1 for redress framework.

7
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of continuity-and-change: in 4–5 sentences, compare the role of the federal state in the Canadian economy between 1939 and 1944. Identify two specific changes and one continuity.
Answer Key

Changes: (1) Federal spending rose from 4.5% of GDP (1938) to 37% (1944); federal employment from 46,000 to 116,000+. (2) Twenty-eight new Crown corporations (Polymer, Eldorado, Federal Aircraft) directed strategic industries; the federal state became the active director of major sectors of production, not merely a regulator. (3) Wage-price controls under the WPTB held inflation to 2.8%/year against WWI's 17%. Continuity: federal commitment to balanced peacetime budgets and limited social spending was suspended for the war but partially returned — Family Allowance 1944 endured but Crown corps gradually privatised; the welfare state expansion that followed (1951 OAS, 1965 CPP, 1966 Medicare) was the institutional consequence of the wartime build-up. The continuity-and-change pattern: wartime crisis enabled lasting state expansion. Marks: 2 changes, 1 continuity, 1 conventions.

8
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of historical perspective: in 4 sentences, reconstruct how Mackenzie King could read Hitler as "a simple sort of peasant" wanting peace in 1937. What contextual factors made this reading possible without imposing today's hindsight?
Answer Key

(1) King's diary reflects the appeasement framework of late-1930s Anglo-American liberal foreign policy: another European war was a horror to be avoided at almost any cost. (2) The trauma of WWI's casualties (60,661 Canadian war dead 1914–18) made another large-scale European intervention politically and morally unsustainable for King. (3) Intelligence available in 1937 was limited — Krystallnacht (Nov 1938) had not yet occurred; the full architecture of the Final Solution did not begin until 1941. (4) King's diary also reflects personal religious-spiritualist habits and a tendency to romanticise strong leaders; this is not a virtue but is itself the historical-perspective question — how did personal psychology, postwar trauma, and limited intelligence combine to produce a reading that 1945 made unimaginable? A Level-4 answer reconstructs without justifying. Marks: 1 appeasement framework, 1 WWI trauma, 1 limited intelligence, 1 personal psychology.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (7–8 sentences) defending or contesting the claim: "The Dieppe Raid (1942) was a necessary sacrifice that made D-Day's success possible." Use specific evidence.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear topic sentence agreeing, contesting, or qualifying the claim; (2) specific evidence (about 5,000 Canadians involved, 916 dead, 1,946 captured in 9 hours; Canadian casualty rate over 60%); (3) acknowledgment of the "lessons learned" reading — preliminary heavy bombardment, deception, specialised armour (Hobart's Funnies), open beach over port — that did inform D-Day's design; (4) consideration of the counter-argument that the lessons could have been learned at lower cost; (5) attention to political pressure (Mountbatten's Combined Operations was under pressure from the Soviet demand for a "second front"); (6) Tim Cook's recent scholarship questioning the "necessary sacrifice" framing. Strong responses do not give a one-sided answer; they qualify. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

10
[6]
Write a coherent 8–10 sentence answer evaluating the claim that Canadian wartime women's labour was "the largest single advance in Canadian women's employment to that date, but largely temporary." Cite specific evidence about the scale of women's wartime work AND about the postwar rollback.
Answer Key

Scale: women's non-agricultural employment rose from 638,000 (1939) to 1,200,000+ (1944); 50,000 women served in CWAC/WRENS/RCAF-WD; women were over 25% of munitions workers. Federally-funded daycare programmes (1942 Dominion-Provincial Day Nurseries Agreement) supported employment. Limits: women's wages were 70¢/hour against men's $1.00 for identical work; daycare programmes closed after 1945; dismissal of married women from federal civil-service jobs resumed (formally repealed 1955). Postwar: federal Family Allowance was paid to mothers, recognising the unpaid domestic work; but the male-breadwinner model returned. Long-term: the wartime expansion was real but the institutional follow-through was limited; the next major advance in women's labour-force participation waited until the 1960s. A Level-4 response balances the advance with the rollback. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 scale evidence, 2 rollback evidence, 1 long-term framing.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
In 2018, Prime Minister Justin Trudeau formally apologised on behalf of the Government of Canada for the 1939 refusal of the MS St. Louis.
Apply the historical-thinking concept of ethical dimensions: in 5–6 sentences, evaluate whether the 2018 apology was appropriate. Address (a) whether the harm was foreseeable in 1939; (b) what responsibilities the apology creates in 2018; (c) what should follow.
Answer Key

(a) Foreseeable in 1939: Krystallnacht (Nov 1938) had occurred; the Évian Conference (July 1938) had failed; the antisemitic statutes were public — the Holocaust as systematic mass murder was not yet underway, but the refugee crisis was visible, and refusal predictably meant return to escalating persecution. (b) Responsibilities created: institutional reckoning with antisemitism in 1930s–40s immigration policy; commitment to refugee admission today; commemoration (National Holocaust Monument 2017); education (CHC2D 2018 curriculum includes the St. Louis). (c) What should follow: continued openness on contemporary refugee crises; honest treatment of the broader "none is too many" policy that admitted only 5,000 Jewish refugees 1933–48 (fewer per capita than any Allied nation); curriculum and museum inclusion. A strong response treats the apology as a beginning rather than a closure. Marks: 2 foreseeability, 2 present-day responsibilities, 1 next-steps.

12
[4]
Apply your understanding of Japanese-Canadian internment to the 2022 invocation of the Emergencies Act against the Freedom Convoy. In 4–5 sentences, identify one similarity and two differences in how Canadian governments use emergency powers across a century.
Answer Key

Similarity: both invocations transferred extraordinary executive authority to address a perceived public-order crisis; in both cases the political and legal merits remained contested afterward. Differences: (1) 1942 was an executive Order-in-Council under the broad War Measures Act without parliamentary oversight; 2022 required parliamentary review and the Public Order Emergency Commission (Rouleau Report 2024). (2) 1942 targeted a racial/national-origin group (75% Canadian citizens); 2022 targeted a self-organised political protest movement with mixed demographics. (3) 1942 dispossessed property without consent and lasted nearly a decade in coastal exclusion; 2022's measures lasted nine days (14–23 February). The continuity: emergency powers have always been controversial; the change: the legal architecture has moved toward (limited) judicial oversight. Marks: 1 similarity, 1 each difference, 1 framing.

[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: The wartime federal state built much of the institutional architecture that became the postwar welfare state. In 3–4 sentences, identify one current Canadian social programme you have experienced or read about (e.g., the Canada Child Benefit, the Canada Pension Plan, universal healthcare) and trace its institutional ancestry to a 1939–45 programme.
Answer Key

Example responses: (1) The Canada Child Benefit (2016) descends from the 1944 Family Allowance — the same political logic of universal cash support for mothers. (2) Canadian Medicare (1966) was a postwar institutional translation of the wartime federal-provincial cooperation in administering health programmes. (3) The Canada Pension Plan (1965) built on Old Age Security (1951), itself made politically possible by Family Allowance's universalist precedent. Marks: 1 specific current programme, 1 historical ancestor, 1 institutional connection.
