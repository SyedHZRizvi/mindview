Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) Sir Robert Borden; (b) the Canadian Expeditionary Force; (c) the Statute of Westminster.
Answer Key

(a) Sir Robert Borden — Conservative Prime Minister of Canada 1911–20; led Canada through WWI; introduced conscription 1917; secured separate Canadian signature on Versailles 1919 and seat at the League of Nations. (b) Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF) — the army Canada raised for overseas service in WWI; over 619,000 served. (c) Statute of Westminster (1931) — Imperial Act recognising dominions' full sovereignty over their own foreign policy; Canada was no longer automatically at war when Britain was.

2
[2]
The Wartime Elections Act (1917) enfranchised which group of women?
Answer Key

B. Female relatives of soldiers — designed to secure pro-conscription votes. Full federal female enfranchisement (for white women) came in the 1918 Act; Status Indian women only got the federal vote in 1960.

3
[2]
In 2–3 sentences, identify two key features of the "creeping barrage" tactic used at Vimy Ridge.
Answer Key

The creeping barrage was a moving curtain of artillery fire (1) advancing 100 metres every three minutes ahead of attacking infantry, (2) suppressing German defenders in their trenches until just before Canadian troops arrived. Combined with platoon-level small-unit training, it allowed troops to follow close behind the barrage rather than walk into prepared machine-gun fire — a major tactical innovation.

4
[3]
Define each: (a) Henri Bourassa; (b) Nellie McClung; (c) the "living tree" doctrine.
Answer Key

(a) Henri Bourassa — Quebec nationalist, editor of Le Devoir; leading conscription opponent; argued the British Empire was not Quebec's empire. (b) Nellie McClung — Manitoba suffragist, novelist, one of the Famous Five; campaigner for women's vote (1916 Manitoba) and the Persons Case; also championed sterilisation laws. (c) "Living tree" doctrine — Lord Sankey's principle (Persons Case, 1929) that Canadian constitutional language grows with society, not fixed to 1867 meanings.

5
[3]
Identify and briefly describe three events that mark a step toward Canadian autonomy from Britain between 1914 and 1929.
Answer Key

(1) Canadian Corps under Currie at Hundred Days 1918 — Canadian command of Canadian forces. (2) Separate Canadian signature on the Treaty of Versailles, June 1919. (3) Separate Canadian seat at the League of Nations 1919. (4) The 1923 Halibut Treaty — first treaty signed by Canada alone (without UK signature). (5) The 1926 Balfour Declaration and the 1929 King–Byng Affair, both confirming dominion autonomy. (6) The Persons Case 1929 — though this is a citizenship-internal advance, not external autonomy. Any three with explanation.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"Has the time come when we are prepared to face a great possibility of Canadian unity? Are we prepared to accept that this country will be divided into two nationalities — one English-speaking, one French-speaking — and that they will be at war with each other?" — Wilfrid Laurier, 1917
Analyse Laurier's 1917 warning. Apply the historical thinking concept of cause and consequence: name two short-term causes of the conscription crisis and two long-term consequences for Quebec-federal relations. (5 sentences)
Answer Key

Short-term causes: (1) the spring 1917 collapse in voluntary enlistment (under 5,000/month by spring 1917, down from over 30,000/month in 1915); (2) Borden's commitments at the Imperial War Conference May 1917 promising 50,000 reinforcements. Long-term consequences: (1) Quebec voted Liberal federally for most of the next six and a half decades (1917 to ~1984), tying the Conservative party to Anglo-imperial loyalism; (2) Mackenzie King's 1939 "no overseas conscription" promise — explicitly framed in response to the 1917 crisis — shaped his entire WWII political strategy; (3) the 1942 plebiscite (Quebec 71.2% No to releasing King from the promise) demonstrated the durability of the Quebec memory; (4) the 1970 FLQ October Crisis was partly read through the same federal-Quebec grievance lens. Marks: 1 for each cause, 1 for each consequence, 1 for analytical synthesis.

7
[4]
A 1936 photograph shows the unveiling of the Walter Allward Vimy Memorial in France. King Edward VIII speaks at a podium; thousands of Canadian veterans stand at attention.
Apply the historical thinking concept of historical significance to the Vimy memorial. In 4–5 sentences, evaluate whether Vimy Ridge "made Canada a nation." Refer to specific evidence.
Answer Key

A strong answer recognises that significance has multiple criteria: (1) Resulting in change — yes, Canadian battlefield reputation translated into Canadian diplomatic leverage at the Imperial War Conference 1917 and a separate seat at the League of Nations 1919; the strategic outcome of the battle itself was limited. (2) Revealing — Vimy revealed Canadian tactical innovation (creeping barrage, all-arms coordination); but Currie's later Hundred Days operations (Aug–Nov 1918) were militarily more decisive. (3) Resonant — Vimy is heavily commemorated (50-dollar bill, 1936 memorial, 2007 restoration) but the "birth of a nation" framing is a 1920s–80s historiographical construction. A balanced answer concludes: significant for shaping a national myth, less significant tactically, and the significance changes with whose nation is being framed (Indigenous, Black, Quebec experiences differ).

8
[4]
Apply the historical thinking concept of ethical dimensions: in 4 sentences, evaluate the Famous Five. Both championed women's enfranchisement AND championed eugenic sterilisation laws. How can a Canadian today fairly judge both legacies?
Answer Key

Fair historical judgment requires holding both moves together. The Famous Five achieved a constitutional victory (the 1929 JCPC Persons Case) and the "living tree" doctrine has been foundational to subsequent Canadian rights cases. The same women — particularly McClung — also championed the 1928 Alberta Sexual Sterilization Act under which 2,800+ people (disproportionately Indigenous, immigrant, intellectually disabled) were forcibly sterilised before repeal in 1972. The honest judgment: recognise the achievement without erasing the harm; the bronze statues on Parliament Hill (since 2000) and the 50-dollar bill (2004) require contextual interpretation, not simple celebration. Marks: 1 for acknowledging achievement, 1 for acknowledging harm, 1 for the holding-both move, 1 for present-day implication.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) on the claim: "The 1917 Borden government's Wartime Elections Act enfranchised women not on principle but as a tactical measure to secure conscription's electoral survival." Use specific evidence; embed at least one quotation or statistic.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear topic sentence agreeing or disagreeing with the claim; (2) specific evidence including the date (September 1917), the population affected (female relatives of soldiers — not all women), and the symmetrical disenfranchisement of post-1902 immigrants from enemy countries; (3) link to outcome (the Unionist 153-seat victory in December 1917, including 62 of 82 Liberal seats in Quebec); (4) the 1918 Act extended the federal franchise to white women on the same terms as men only after conscription had passed; (5) analytical close — the Borden case was a tactical measure consistent with the war policy, not a principled commitment to equality; full enfranchisement of Status Indian women waited until 1960. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

10
[6]
Write a coherent 8–10 sentence answer comparing the experience of three groups in Canada during WWI: (a) French-Canadian Catholics in Quebec; (b) British-born immigrants in the Prairie wheat economy; (c) Indigenous men who enlisted in the CEF. Identify one common factor and three differences.
Answer Key

Common factor: all three groups were Canadian residents whose relation to the imperial war was mediated by identity and citizenship rather than just geography. Differences: (1) French Canadians had no Anglo-imperial loyalty and faced Regulation 17 hostility; their enlistment rates were under 6%. (2) British-born immigrants enlisted at high rates (over 60% of the early CEF) and were enfranchised under Borden; many also faced suspicion when the Russian Revolution made labour militancy seem dangerous. (3) Indigenous men (over 4,000 served — Henry Norwest, Francis Pegahmagabow at 378 confirmed kills, the highest-credited sniper in the war) volunteered without the federal franchise, often without Indian Act enfranchisement, and were often denied veterans' benefits or returned to reserves under residential-schools-era policies. A strong answer engages historical perspective: each group had different available frames for understanding the same war. Marks: 1 common factor, 1 per group's distinctive features, 2 for analytical synthesis, 1 for evidence/citations.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
In 2017, the Canadian federal government issued a 50-dollar polymer banknote that included a depiction of the Famous Five statue on Parliament Hill. Some Indigenous and feminist scholars criticised the choice; others defended it.
Apply your understanding of the Persons Case (1929) and the Famous Five: in 5–6 sentences, what is one defensible argument for honouring them on the banknote, and one defensible argument against? Take a position.
Answer Key

For honouring: the Persons Case is a constitutional landmark with downstream effect (the "living tree" doctrine has supported LGBTQ rights, disability rights, and Charter expansions); commemorating women's constitutional inclusion is appropriate to a state currency. Against honouring: the Famous Five championed eugenic sterilisation laws that harmed disproportionately Indigenous, immigrant, and disabled people; uncritical commemoration without context obscures the moral complexity. A defensible position: a commemorative banknote could include both context (e.g., a QR code, a paired image of a 1928 sterilisation-law text) or could simply be replaced; the choice depends on whether you read commemoration as "celebration" or as "framework for ongoing conversation." Look for: clear position with evidence; recognition of both sides; specific historical detail. Marks: 1 for case-for, 1 for case-against, 2 for analytical balance, 1 for position-taking.

12
[4]
Apply the historical-thinking concept of continuity-and-change: in 4–5 sentences, identify how the federal government's response to the 2022 Freedom Convoy compares with its response to the 1919 Winnipeg General Strike. What is one continuity, one change, and one significant difference in legal framework?
Answer Key

Continuity: in both cases, federal emergency authority was invoked against domestic protest movements that were framed (rightly or wrongly) as challenges to federal authority and public order. Change: 1919 used the War Measures Act with virtually no parliamentary oversight; 2022 used the Emergencies Act (1988 replacement of the WMA) with mandatory parliamentary review and the Public Order Emergency Commission (Rouleau 2024). Difference in legal framework: 1919 amendments to the Immigration Act permitted deportation of British-born residents without trial — a measure incompatible with the post-1982 Charter framework; the 2022 Emergencies Act invocation required and received judicial review. A Level-4 response treats this as evidence of a real (though imperfect) institutional learning across a century. Marks: 1 continuity, 1 change, 1 legal-framework, 1 conventions.

[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: The Winnipeg General Strike's leaders went to Parliament and helped found the CCF / NDP. In 3–4 sentences, give one example of how this 1919 → 1932 → 1961 chain has shaped a specific 21st-century Canadian policy you have learned about (e.g., Medicare, the EI system, the Canada Pension Plan).
Answer Key

Example responses: (1) Tommy Douglas, premier of Saskatchewan 1944–61 and first NDP leader 1961, designed the 1962 Saskatchewan Medicare programme that became the model for the federal 1966 Medical Care Act; the CCF/NDP tradition runs directly from Woodsworth's 1921 Parliament seat. (2) Family Allowance 1944, Old Age Security 1951, CPP 1965, and Medicare 1966 were all shaped by CCF/NDP pressure on Liberal minority governments. (3) The post-1995 federal program review reforms were resisted partly through NDP pressure on minority parliaments. A strong answer makes a specific institutional connection. Marks: 1 specific policy, 1 historical chain, 1 communication.
