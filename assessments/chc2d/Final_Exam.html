Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[2]B · 1914–29
When Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914, Canada:
Answer Key

A. The Statute of Westminster came in 1931. In 1914 the BNA Act made Canada part of Britain's automatic war declaration.

2
[3]B · 1914–29
Identify three contributors to a "Canadian autonomy" trajectory between 1914 and 1931.
Answer Key

(1) Canadian Corps under Currie at Hundred Days 1918 — Canadian command of Canadian forces. (2) Separate signature on Treaty of Versailles 1919; separate seat at League of Nations. (3) Halibut Treaty 1923 — first treaty signed by Canada alone. (4) Balfour Declaration 1926 — dominion equality. (5) King–Byng Affair 1926 — dominion confidence in own legislature. (6) Statute of Westminster 1931 — formal sovereign foreign policy. Any three.

3
[3]B · 1914–29
Identify: (a) the Persons Case (1929); (b) the Winnipeg General Strike (1919); (c) the Halifax Explosion (1917).
Answer Key

(a) Persons Case — JCPC ruling under Lord Sankey that women were "qualified persons" under the BNA Act; established the "living tree" doctrine. (b) Winnipeg General Strike — 30,000 workers across nearly every sector walked off 15 May–25 June; broken by federal intervention; Bloody Saturday 21 June killed two strikers; strike leaders later founded the CCF. (c) Halifax Explosion — 6 Dec 1917 collision of SS Mont-Blanc and SS Imo; ~1,782 dead, ~9,000 injured, ~600 blinded.

4
[2]C · 1929–45
The MS St. Louis:
Answer Key

B. The 1939 refusal of the St. Louis by Canada (under F.C. Blair's "none is too many" approach) is the most pointed illustration of Canada's antisemitic immigration policy. The 2018 federal apology recognises this.

5
[3]C · 1929–45
Identify: (a) the Dieppe Raid (1942); (b) Order-in-Council P.C. 1486 (1942); (c) Family Allowance (1944).
Answer Key

(a) Dieppe Raid — 19 Aug 1942 amphibious raid on French coast; ~5,000 Canadians involved, 916 dead, 1,946 captured in 9 hours; "lessons learned" informed D-Day. (b) P.C. 1486 — federal order ordering removal of all Japanese Canadians from 100-mile Pacific zone; ~22,000 affected, 75% Canadian citizens. (c) Family Allowance — Canada's first universal cash transfer; "baby bonus" paid to mothers; predecessor of postwar welfare state programmes.

6
[2]D · 1945–82
Tommy Douglas is most associated with:
Answer Key

B. Douglas was CCF premier of Saskatchewan 1944–61. Saskatchewan's 1962 Medicare programme became the model for the federal 1966 Medical Care Act.

7
[3]D · 1945–82
Identify: (a) NATO 1949; (b) the 1967 Immigration Act; (c) the 1969 White Paper.
Answer Key

(a) NATO — North Atlantic Treaty Organization; Canada was a founding member; Pearson contributed Article 2 (the "Canadian article" on economic and cultural cooperation). (b) 1967 Immigration Act — formalised the "points system"; selection by age, education, language, occupational demand regardless of national origin. (c) 1969 White Paper — Trudeau-Chrétien proposal to terminate special status of First Nations; rejected by First Nations; formally withdrawn 1971.

8
[2]D · 1945–82
The Quiet Revolution refers to:
Answer Key

B. "Il faut que ça change" — "Things must change" — was Lesage's 1960 slogan. The QR produced both federalist (Trudeau) and sovereigntist (Lévesque/PQ) currents.

9
[2]E · 1982–Present
The 1995 Quebec referendum result was:
Answer Key

A. The closest separation result in Canadian history. Parizeau's "money and the ethnic vote" speech preceded his resignation; Bouchard succeeded him as PQ leader and premier.

10
[3]A · Inquiry Skills
Name and briefly define the six historical thinking concepts.
Answer Key

(1) Historical significance — three criteria (results-in-change, revealing, resonant). (2) Cause and consequence — multiple ranked causes, foreseen/unforeseen consequences. (3) Continuity and change — comparison across time. (4) Historical perspective — reconstruct past actors' understanding without anachronism. (5) Evidence — sources interpreted in context; triangulation. (6) Ethical dimensions — fair judgment about past conduct and present responsibilities.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
11
[8]B + C · Document Analysis
Document 1: "All citizens are liable to military service for the defence of their country, and I conceive that the battle for Canadian liberty and autonomy is being fought today on the plains of France and of Belgium." — Sir Robert Borden, House of Commons, 18 May 1917.
Document 2: "Conscription if necessary, but not necessarily conscription." — Mackenzie King, on the 1942 plebiscite results (64% Yes nationally, 71.2% No in Quebec).
Compare these two documents. In 7–8 sentences, address: (i) the rhetorical strategy of each PM; (ii) the political context of each statement; (iii) the historical-thinking concept of continuity-and-change in federal-Quebec relations on conscription between 1917 and 1942.
Answer Key

Borden 1917: bold, direct, frames conscription as "autonomy" — appealing to Canadian (English) nationalism while invoking universal citizen-obligation; this was the rhetoric needed to pass the Military Service Act. King 1942: deliberately ambiguous, designed to keep both English (which wanted overseas conscription) and Quebec (which did not) inside his coalition; King had promised "no overseas conscription" in 1939 and the 1942 plebiscite released him from the promise but he delayed action until November 1944. Continuity: federal-Quebec conscription remained the defining English-French test; King's 1942 framing was directly shaped by the 1917 crisis lesson. Change: by 1942 King had constructed the political framework (the 1939 promise + the 1942 plebiscite) that allowed him to ride out the issue without splitting the country as Borden had. Marks: 2 Document 1 analysis, 2 Document 2 analysis, 2 continuity-and-change, 2 synthesis.

12
[8]B–E · Ethical Dimensions
Apply the historical thinking concept of ethical dimensions across the century. In 8–10 sentences, compare the federal-government's treatment of three groups during three different "emergency" moments: (1) Ukrainian Canadians under the 1914 War Measures Act; (2) Japanese Canadians under the 1942 P.C. 1486; (3) Quebec-FLQ political detainees under the 1970 War Measures Act invocation. Identify continuities in legal mechanism, differences in scope, and the long-term institutional consequences (e.g., the 1988 Emergencies Act).
Answer Key

Continuities: all three used the federal WMA mechanism (1914 Act, surviving 1942, surviving 1970); all three operated without individual trials or evidentiary thresholds; all three were politically driven (anti-immigrant pressure 1914 and 1942; anti-FLQ panic 1970) rather than security-driven (RCMP found no Japanese-Canadian threat; the 1970 detainees were largely not FLQ-affiliated). Differences in scope: 1914 internment was ~8,579 (mostly Ukrainian); 1942 was ~22,000 (75% Canadian citizens, with property confiscation); 1970 was 497 detained without charge, briefer in duration. Long-term consequences: 1988 Emergencies Act replaced the WMA with parliamentary review, automatic expiry, judicial review, and mandatory inquiry; same day, the Japanese-Canadian redress agreement was announced. Ethical judgment: in all three cases, the harms were foreseeable, the moral resources were available at the time (sympathetic press, civil-liberties critics, religious leaders), and alternatives existed within political constraints. The federal apologies (Japanese Canadian 1988, "St. Louis" 2018, residential schools 2008) are the institutional acknowledgments of this fair judgment. A Level-4 response holds all three episodes in view. Marks: 2 each for episodes (6); 2 for legal-mechanism continuity and ethical synthesis.

13
[5]A · Source Criticism
A 2024 social-media post claims: "Statistics Canada admitted in a leaked memo that the 2023 census undercounted Indigenous Canadians by 18%."
Apply Caulfield's SIFT method. In 5 sentences, explain how you would evaluate this claim: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims.
Answer Key

Stop: do not reshare without verification. Investigate the source: check the account's track record, public profile, and any verifiable identity claim. Find better coverage: search for "Statistics Canada 2023 census Indigenous undercount" on independent news outlets (CBC, Globe and Mail, APTN), peer-reviewed publications, and the Statistics Canada website itself; legitimate undercount statistics typically come from the National Household Survey methodology documents. Trace claims: the post claims a "leaked memo" — verifiable claims would include a document link, a journalist's authentication, or a parliamentary record. Without these, the claim should be treated as uncorroborated. This is exactly the historian's source-criticism applied to contemporary media. Marks: 1 per SIFT step + 1 synthesis.

14
[4]D + E · Historical Significance
Apply the historical thinking concept of historical significance: in 4–5 sentences, rank the following five events for their long-term significance to Canadian national identity, and justify your ranking: (a) Vimy Ridge 1917; (b) Pearson's 1956 UNEF proposal at Suez; (c) Trudeau's 1971 Multiculturalism Policy; (d) the 1982 Charter of Rights and Freedoms; (e) the 2015 TRC 94 Calls to Action.
Answer Key

Any defensible ranking with clear justification. Strong responses might place: (1) 1982 Charter as most consequential — it has reshaped every area of Canadian law (Oakes, Morgentaler, Vriend) and section 35 transformed Indigenous-rights jurisprudence; (2) 1971 Multiculturalism as second — it changed the cultural framework of Canadian self-understanding and shaped immigration outcomes; (3) 2015 TRC as third — it has shifted public conversation on reconciliation, though implementation is slow; (4) 1956 UNEF as fourth — created the peacekeeping idea, though Canada's actual contribution has declined; (5) Vimy 1917 as fifth — culturally resonant but the "birth of a nation" reading is a postwar construction. Other rankings are defensible. The key is the justification, not the order. Marks: 1 per ranked event with brief justification, 1 for synthesis.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]

[15]B + C + D + E · Major Essay
Major Task — Write a historical-analysis essay (450–600 words) responding to ONE of the following prompts:

Option 1: "Trace the relationship between federal emergency powers and civil liberties in Canada from 1914 to 2022. Has Canadian institutional learning been adequate?"
Option 2: "Evaluate the claim that Canada's twentieth century was 'a slow movement from colony to nation to multicultural state.' Use specific evidence from at least three strands."
Option 3: "Compare and contrast Canada's response to two minority crises: the MS St. Louis (1939) and the Syrian refugee programme (2015–16). What changed, and what stayed the same?"
Option 4: "Evaluate the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–15) as a historical institution. Has it produced meaningful change, or has it become a symbol that displaces action?"

Include: introduction with thesis, 3 body paragraphs (each evidence-supported), conclusion. Embed at least 3 specific historical references with dates and key actors.
Marking Rubric

K/U (3): correct understanding of the relevant historical events and actors. Thinking (4): quality and depth of argument; specific evidence selection; consideration of counter-arguments. Communication (5): clear introduction with thesis, well-organised paragraphs, embedded specific references with dates, conclusion that synthesises rather than merely restates, voice and conventions. Application (3): transfer of historical-thinking concepts; relevance of evidence to claim. Sample first sentences for Option 1: "From the 1914 War Measures Act through the 1988 Emergencies Act to the 2022 invocation of the renamed instrument, the Canadian state has consistently treated emergency powers as a tool of first resort against domestic dissent; the institutional learning has been real but incomplete — internment of Ukrainian Canadians in 1914 and of Japanese Canadians in 1942 produced no immediate reform, but the WMA's replacement in 1988 added parliamentary review, judicial oversight, and the Rouleau Commission framework that operated in 2024."

[8]A + E · Inquiry Application
Major Application Task — Design a historical-inquiry project on a current Canadian issue (e.g., reconciliation progress, climate policy, immigration debate, electoral reform, federal-provincial relations).

Provide:
(a) one open-ended historical-inquiry question (2 marks),
(b) two primary sources you would consult (with provenance — where and how to find them) (2 marks),
(c) two secondary sources you would engage (with citations) (2 marks),
(d) the historical-thinking concept most relevant to your inquiry, with a 2-sentence justification (2 marks).
Marking Rubric

(a) Question must be open-ended, specific, bounded, and defensible by evidence. (b) Primary sources must be specific and the provenance route must be plausible (LAC, archived newspapers, parliamentary record, oral testimony archives). (c) Secondary sources should be from peer-reviewed publications or recognised popular history. (d) The historical-thinking concept choice must match the question; the justification must explain why. Marks as above.

[5]D + E · Continuity-and-Change
Application: Trace one institutional continuity across at least three of the four units (1914–29, 1929–45, 1945–82, 1982–present). In 5–6 sentences, identify the institution (e.g., the War Measures Act / Emergencies Act framework; the Indian Act; the federal-provincial division of powers; Canadian immigration policy), describe one moment from each of three eras, and analyse the long arc.
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) clear institution identified; (2) three distinct era-moments with dates; (3) analysis of continuity (what persists) and change (what shifts); (4) one specific example of structural continuity. Strong responses use the WMA → EA framework (1914 internment, 1942 internment, 1970 FLQ, 2022 Convoy) or the immigration trajectory (1923 Chinese Exclusion, 1947 King statement, 1967 points system, 2024 Levels Plan). Marks: 1 institution, 3 era-moments, 1 long-arc synthesis.

[5]A + B–E · Cross-Strand Synthesis
Application — Cross-Strand: In 6–8 sentences, identify one substantive thematic connection between two strands of CHC2D. For example: the relationship between Indigenous-rights advocacy (Strand D, 1969 White Paper rejection; Strand E, section 35 / TRC) and the historical-thinking concept of ethical dimensions (Strand A). Or: the relationship between Canadian foreign policy (Pearson 1956 / Chrétien 2003) and constitutional autonomy (1931 Statute of Westminster). Use specific evidence from both sides.
Marking Rubric

Strong responses identify a substantive (not superficial) thematic connection, use specific evidence from each of the two strands, and produce a synthesis statement that does work beyond restating the parallel. Examples: (1) the institutional learning across 1914→1942→1970→2022 emergency-powers history applies the ethical-dimensions concept across the entire century; (2) the Pearsonian "middle power" multilateralism enabled by 1931 sovereignty was Canada's contribution to the post-1945 rights order that flowed into the 1982 Charter; (3) the 1971 Multiculturalism Policy and the 1967 points system together produced demographic outcomes that shape the 2026 federal political landscape. Marks: 1 connection-identification, 2 each side's evidence, 1 synthesis.

21
[7]A · Course-Wide Application
Final Synthesis: In 8–10 sentences, articulate the most important insight you take from CHC2D about Canadian history and identity. Draw on at least two of the four chronological strands (B, C, D, E) and on the historical-thinking concepts of Strand A. Use one specific example from a text, document, or event we studied. Identify one way this insight will transfer beyond history class — to civic life, future studies, or current events.
Marking Rubric

K/U (2): accurate use of two strands. Thinking (2): substantive insight, not generic. Communication (2): clear organisation, specific examples, register. Application (1): explicit transfer to a non-classroom context. Strong responses tie the structural insight (e.g., the gap between Canadian self-image and Canadian conduct; the work of memory in producing national identity; the relationship between law and politics in producing rights protections) to a defensible statement about what historical-thinking discipline produces. Examples of strong syntheses: (1) "Canadian institutions have grown more capable of self-correction across the century, but the WMA → EA progression shows the work is incremental, not complete." (2) "The Charter and the TRC together represent two halves of one Canadian project — rights from above (Charter) and recognition from below (TRC) — and neither functions without the other." (3) "The most important lesson of CHC2D is that 'national identity' is not given; it is built and rebuilt continuously, often through painful reckonings, and that the citizens of a state inherit responsibilities for that ongoing construction." Marks: as above.
