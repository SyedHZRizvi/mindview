Topic 2.1 — The Great Depression
Question 1
The Great Depression's effect on Canada was particularly severe because:
Solution: Canada's heavy exposure to commodity exports — wheat especially — meant the global price collapse hit Canada hard. The 1930 Smoot-Hawley US tariff cut Canadian exports in half. Canadian banks did not fail; the federal regulatory system protected them.
Question 2
The On-to-Ottawa Trek of 1935 was:
Solution: About 1,500 men in B.C. relief camps walked off and boarded freight trains east in April 1935; they were stopped at Regina under Bennett's orders. The 1 July Regina Riot killed two (one police officer, one trekker). The trekkers' demands — improved camp wages, the franchise, work programmes — were materially modest.
Topic 2.2 — Road to War
Question 3
Why did Canada wait seven days after Britain's declaration of war on 3 September 1939 to declare war itself?
Solution: Under the Statute of Westminster, Canada had full foreign-policy sovereignty. Parliament debated for a week; Canada declared war on 10 September 1939. The seven-day gap was the visible mark of Canadian independence — and was deliberate.
Question 4
"None is too many." — Director of Immigration Frederick Charles Blair, on Jewish refugees, late 1930s
This phrase, attributed to F.C. Blair, encapsulates the Canadian government's policy that resulted in:
Solution: Canada admitted only about 5,000 Jewish refugees between 1933 and 1948 — fewer per capita than any Allied nation. The MS St. Louis episode of June 1939 is the most pointed illustration; the federal government issued a formal apology in 2018.
Topic 2.3–2.4 — WWII Combat and Home Front
Question 5
The Dieppe Raid (19 August 1942):
Solution: Dieppe was a disastrous raid on the French coast — about 60% of Allied troops became casualties. Of nearly 5,000 Canadians involved, 916 died and 1,946 were captured. The "lessons learned" — preliminary bombardment, deception, specialised armour, open beach over port — shaped D-Day's success.
Question 6
The 1944 Family Allowance was significant because it was:
Solution: The Family Allowance ("baby bonus") was universal — paid to all eligible mothers without means testing — and established the political legitimacy of universal social programmes that paved the way for OAS (1951), CPP (1965), Medicare (1966).
Topic 2.5–2.6 — Internment and Holocaust
Question 7
Japanese-Canadian internment (1942) involved:
Solution: Federal Order-in-Council P.C. 1486 (24 Feb 1942) ordered removal of all persons of Japanese ancestry from a 100-mile coastal zone. The RCMP's own assessment had found no security threat. The Custodian of Enemy Alien Property confiscated property and sold it below market to fund the internment. Coastal exclusion lasted until 1949.
Question 8
The 1988 redress agreement for Japanese-Canadian internment:
Solution: Mulroney's 1988 redress agreement was the model that influenced later apologies (Chinese Head Tax 2006, Komagata Maru 2016, residential schools 2008). The same day, the Emergencies Act replaced the War Measures Act with stricter parliamentary oversight.
Question 9
Canadian troops who entered the Westerbork transit camp in the eastern Netherlands on 12 April 1945:
Solution: Auschwitz-Birkenau was liberated by Soviet forces 27 January 1945; Bergen-Belsen by British forces 15 April 1945. Canadian troops did liberate Westerbork on 12 April 1945 — the transit point for some 107,000 Jews deported east, including Anne Frank and her family.
Question 10
Short answer: In 3 sentences, explain how the 1942 internment of Japanese Canadians used the same legal mechanism (the War Measures Act) as the 1914 internment of Ukrainian Canadians. What does this continuity reveal?
Sample: Both internments used the WMA's emergency executive powers to detain Canadian residents without trial on ancestry grounds — Ukrainians and other "enemy aliens" in 1914, Japanese Canadians in 1942. In both cases, the RCMP's own assessments found no security threat; in both cases, political and economic pressure (Anglo-Canadian Western Canadian business, organised labour) shaped the decision. The continuity reveals that "wartime necessity" can be a moral cover for prior racial politics — the lesson that drove the 1988 reform replacing the WMA with the Emergencies Act.
