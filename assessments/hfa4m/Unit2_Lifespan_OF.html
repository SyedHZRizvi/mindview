Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [12 marks]
1
[3]
List three nutrients with increased needs during pregnancy and explain the function of each.
Answer Key

Folate — prevents neural-tube defects in early embryogenesis (RDA jumps to 600 µg DFE). Iron — supports increased maternal blood volume and fetal iron stores (RDA 27 mg/day). Iodine — supports fetal brain and thyroid development; deficiency can cause cretinism (RDA 220 µg/day). Other acceptable: calcium, vitamin D, DHA, choline, protein.

2
[2]
Why is honey not recommended for infants under 12 months?
Answer Key

B. Infant gut flora is too immature to prevent spore germination → toxin → botulism. Avoid even pasteurised honey before 12 months.

3
[3]
Describe two reasons why peak bone mass accretion in adolescence is critical for lifelong skeletal health.
Answer Key

(1) ~90% of peak bone mass is reached by age 18 — adolescence is the primary window for building bone. (2) Higher peak bone mass delays the threshold at which age-related bone loss leads to osteoporosis. Inadequate calcium, vitamin D, weight-bearing activity, or excess restrictive dieting during adolescence reduces peak bone mass, raising lifetime fracture risk.

4
[2]
Which condition is characterised by restriction of intake, low body weight, and intense fear of weight gain?
Answer Key

A. Anorexia nervosa — restriction → significantly low body weight + intense fear of weight gain + disturbance in body experience. Bulimia involves binges with compensation; BED involves binges without compensation.

5
[2]
Why is older-adult protein recommendation typically 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day rather than the 0.8 g/kg/day RDA for younger adults?
Answer Key

B. Sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss) is slowed by adequate protein + resistance exercise. Anabolic resistance (less protein synthesis per gram) makes higher intake helpful.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Scenario: A 25-year-old woman is planning her first pregnancy in 6 months. She drinks 3 cups of coffee daily, has one glass of wine 2 evenings a week, and eats tuna salad three times a week. Her current diet contains no fortified or supplemental folate. Outline a pre-conception plan with four specific changes and justify each.
Answer Key

(1) Start daily 0.4 mg folic acid supplement now (NTDs are prevented pre-conception, since the neural tube closes by day 28). (2) Reduce caffeine to ≤200–300 mg/day (some evidence higher intake associates with miscarriage and low birth weight). (3) Stop alcohol entirely once attempting to conceive (no safe amount; FASD is preventable only by abstaining). (4) Reduce high-mercury fish (canned albacore/light tuna limited; switch to salmon, sardines, light canned tuna for safer omega-3 intake). Also: ensure adequate iron (consider testing ferritin), vitamin D supplement, balanced diet, daily prenatal multivitamin closer to conception.

7
[4]
An 84-year-old woman lives alone, has dentures, takes 4 medications, and reports a 5 kg weight loss over 6 months. She no longer cooks. Identify two likely contributors to her nutritional risk and propose two interventions.
Answer Key

Contributors: social isolation (eating alone reduces intake); dental issues (chewing difficulty limits foods); polypharmacy (drugs may affect appetite, taste, absorption); reduced cooking capacity; possibly grief/depression; possibly underlying disease. Interventions: (1) Referral to a registered dietitian + medical assessment (MNA or CNST screening). (2) Connect to community resources — Meals on Wheels, community lunch programs, senior centres. (3) Modify-texture nutrient-dense foods, fortified meals, frequent small meals. (4) Medication review with pharmacist for drug-nutrient interactions.

8
[4]
Calculate calcium intake from one day's food: 1 cup milk (300 mg), 1 cup fortified soy milk (300 mg), 1 cup leafy greens (~150 mg), 50 g sardines (200 mg). Compare to the RDA for a 16-year-old.
Answer Key

Total = 300 + 300 + 150 + 200 = 950 mg/day. Adolescent RDA = 1300 mg/day, so this diet provides about 73% of needs. Possible additions to reach 1300 mg: additional dairy or fortified plant milk (300 mg/cup), Greek yogurt (200–250 mg/cup), tofu set with calcium sulfate (~250 mg/100 g), fortified orange juice, or a small supplement under guidance. Vitamin D (sun + supplement) is needed alongside calcium for absorption.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
Write a one-paragraph (~150 words) summary explaining why breastfeeding is recommended exclusively for the first 6 months. Include three benefits and one caveat.
Answer Key

Look for: WHO and Health Canada recommendation for exclusive BF to 6 months; benefits for infant (immune protection — IgA, lactoferrin, HMOs supporting gut microbiome; reduced rates of otitis media, gastroenteritis, SIDS, later obesity); benefits for mother (reduced ovarian and pre-menopausal breast cancer, lactational amenorrhea, bonding); composition adapts (colostrum → mature milk). Caveat: when BF is not possible/contraindicated, commercial iron-fortified formula is the recommended alternative. Vitamin D supplementation (400 IU/day) recommended for all breastfed infants in Canada.

10
[4]
Compare and contrast anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge-eating disorder in a 3-row table. Columns: defining feature, typical body weight, key complications.
Answer Key

AN: restriction of intake, intense fear of weight gain; significantly low body weight; amenorrhea, lanugo, bradycardia, osteoporosis, refeeding-syndrome risk. BN: recurrent binges + compensatory behaviours (purging, fasting, exercise); often normal weight; parotid swelling, dental erosion, oesophageal tears, hypokalemia. BED: recurrent binges without compensation; often overweight/obese; metabolic complications, depression, shame; most common ED.

11
[4]
Describe how nutritional needs change across the lifespan in a paragraph — touching on infancy, adolescence, pregnancy, and older adulthood. Highlight what nutrient changes are most critical at each stage.
Answer Key

Look for clarity of writing, accuracy across stages: infancy (highest per-kg energy, iron-rich complementary foods, vitamin D supplement), adolescence (calcium peak, iron for menstruating individuals, energy and protein for growth spurt), pregnancy (folate, iron, iodine, DHA, modest +kcal), older adulthood (lower energy, same/higher specific nutrients — B12, vitamin D, calcium, protein, fluid).

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[6]
Case Study — Infant Feeding: A 4-month-old breastfed boy is growing well on the WHO charts. The mother asks whether she should start solids early "because he seems hungry." Counsel her, citing current Canadian guidance, and outline the right timing and sequence.
Answer Key

Counsel against introducing solids before 6 months; growth spurts at 3–6 months are normal and not indications for solids. Solids before 6 months can displace breast milk, expose immature gut and kidneys, and increase obesity/chronic-disease risk. Continue exclusive breastfeeding to 6 months. At 6 months watch for developmental signs (head control, sitting with support, interest in food, loss of tongue-thrust). Begin with iron-rich foods (meat, poultry, fish, well-cooked egg, fortified infant cereal, legumes) and introduce a variety of textures progressively. Introduce allergens (peanut, egg, dairy) around 6 months rather than delaying. Avoid honey before 12 months, choking hazards, juice. Continue breastfeeding to 2 years or beyond. Vitamin D 400 IU/day supplement throughout breastfeeding.

13
[6]
STSE — Eating Disorders and Social Media: A friend on social media follows accounts that promote extreme restriction and exercise. You notice she has lost weight rapidly and is making negative comments about her body. (a) Identify three warning signs. (b) Outline a sensitive, supportive response and the Canadian resources you would mention.
Answer Key

(a) Warning signs: rapid unexplained weight loss; preoccupation with body weight/shape; social media consumption focused on "thinspiration" or extreme dietary content; mood changes; over-exercise; meal restriction or skipping. (b) Response: speak privately, non-judgmentally; focus on her feelings and well-being, not body or food specifically; listen; don't comment on appearance even positively; encourage seeking professional support; offer to help find resources. Canadian resources: National Eating Disorder Information Centre (NEDIC) — 1-866-NEDIC-20; Kids Help Phone — 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868; school counsellor; family doctor; provincial eating-disorder programs. Reinforce that early intervention dramatically improves outcomes; eating disorders are serious mental-health conditions, not phases.
