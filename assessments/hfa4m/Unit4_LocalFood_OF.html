Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [12 marks]
1
[3]
Describe Canada's supply-management system. Name the three sectors involved and the three core mechanisms.
Answer Key

Supply management covers dairy, poultry (chicken, turkey), eggs. Mechanisms: (1) production quotas held by farmers; (2) import controls (tariffs above quota volumes); (3) administered pricing by provincial marketing boards. Aim: stable farmer income, consistent supply and quality; critics cite higher consumer prices.

2
[2]
Which agency administers the Canada Organic Regime?
Answer Key

B. CFIA administers Canada Organic. Three-year transition from synthetic inputs, prohibition of GMOs and most synthetic pesticides.

3
[3]
Describe one benefit and one concern about GMO crops in Canada.
Answer Key

Benefits: reduced pesticide spraying (Bt corn/cotton); drought-tolerant varieties; vitamin enrichment (Golden Rice, vitamin A); higher yields preventing food loss. Concerns: corporate consolidation (few firms own most patented seeds); herbicide-resistant superweeds; ecological uncertainty; lack of mandatory GMO labelling in Canada limits consumer choice. Note: scientific consensus is that approved GM foods are safe to eat.

4
[2]
In the NOVA classification, what does NOVA 4 describe?
Answer Key

C. NOVA 4 = ultra-processed (UPF) — industrial formulations with ingredients rarely used in home kitchens; ~48% of Canadian energy on average.

5
[2]
Approximately what proportion of Canadian households experienced food insecurity in 2022 (PROOF)?
Answer Key

D. About 18.4% in 2022 — the highest rate since national tracking began. Driven primarily by inadequate household income.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Label audit: Compare two granola bars per 30 g. Bar X: 130 kcal, 4 g sat fat (20% DV), 180 mg Na (8% DV), 11 g sugar (10 g added), 1 g fibre (4% DV), 2 g protein, first ingredient: sugar. Bar Y: 115 kcal, 1 g sat fat (5% DV), 65 mg Na (3% DV), 4 g sugar (2 g added), 4 g fibre (14% DV), 4 g protein, first ingredient: rolled oats. (a) Apply the 5%/15% DV rule. (b) Recommend one and justify with three label-specific reasons.
Answer Key

(a) Bar X: sat fat 20% (a lot), sodium 8% (in between), fibre 4% (a little). Bar Y: sat fat 5% (a little), sodium 3% (a little), fibre 14% (close to a lot). (b) Recommend Bar Y because: (1) substantially less added sugar (2 g vs 10 g); (2) higher fibre (14% vs 4% DV — better satiety and gut health); (3) less saturated fat (5% vs 20% DV); plus first ingredient is oats (whole grain) not sugar — lower NOVA processing tier.

7
[4]
Carbon-footprint comparison: in November in Toronto, you can buy local Ontario apples (~100 km transport, controlled-atmosphere storage 5 months) or fresh New Zealand apples (~14,000 km ocean shipping). Discuss the carbon footprint trade-offs and recommend a choice for a climate-conscious household. Mention the role of production method.
Answer Key

Storage in Ontario uses electricity (mostly low-carbon hydroelectric and nuclear in Ontario's grid). Ocean shipping is surprisingly efficient per kg (vs air freight) but adds emissions. Production method dominates — both apples grown conventionally with similar inputs. For a climate-conscious household: Ontario apples are likely the better choice given low-carbon Ontario grid + short transport. The bigger climate decisions are shifting away from beef/lamb, reducing food waste, and avoiding air-freighted out-of-season produce (asparagus, berries flown in February). Local-stored generally beats long-distance fresh.

8
[4]
Design an investigation to assess whether Grade 12 students can identify ultra-processed (NOVA 4) products from a shelf of 20 packaged foods. Identify variables and analysis.
Answer Key

Hypothesis: students with a brief NOVA tutorial identify NOVA 4 foods more accurately than untrained peers. Sample: 40 Grade 12 students randomly assigned to tutorial (intervention) vs no-tutorial control. IV: NOVA tutorial. DV: number of correct classifications out of 20 foods (10 NOVA 1–3, 10 NOVA 4 — independently scored by two dietitians for ground truth). Controlled: same foods, same time limit, individual completion. Ethics: voluntary, anonymous. Analysis: compare mean correct identifications and % accuracy with 95% CIs; independent t-test. Limitations: classifying borderline foods is hard; reliability of scoring; short-term effect.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
Write a paragraph (~150 words) explaining household food insecurity in Canada: what it is, who is most affected, why income is the main driver, and one policy lever that addresses it.
Answer Key

Look for: PROOF definition — inadequate or insecure access due to financial constraints; severity range (marginal, moderate, severe); 2022 rate ~18.4% of households; populations at higher risk (lone-parent female-led, Black, Indigenous, renters, social-assistance recipients); nutritious diets cost more per kcal than ultra-processed; food charity (food banks) reaches only ~23% of food-insecure households; policy levers include indexed social-assistance rates, guaranteed minimum income (Ontario 2017–19 pilot), Canada Disability Benefit, National School Food Program, $15/hour living wage. Income transfers reduce food insecurity more than food charity.

10
[4]
Construct a 4-row table comparing conventional, organic, regenerative, and Indigenous-led agriculture. Columns: typical practices, certification or governance, one strength, one limitation.
Answer Key

Conventional: synthetic fertilisers/pesticides, GM seed where licensed, monoculture; CFIA/PMRA-regulated; highest yields; environmental and soil impacts. Organic: synthetic inputs prohibited, no GMO, IPM, crop rotation; Canada Organic (CFIA); lower pesticide residues, better pollinator habitat; lower yields (20–25%), more expensive. Regenerative: cover crops, no-till, rotational livestock, biodiversity; emerging certifications; rebuilds soil and sequesters carbon; data variable. Indigenous: place-based knowledge, polyculture (Three Sisters), country foods, community governance; community-led/treaty rights; supports food sovereignty, biodiversity, cultural continuity; small scale, requires policy support.

11
[4]
In a labelled image or careful description, list the major sections of a Canadian Nutrition Facts table. Identify three pieces of information a careful shopper should compare.
Answer Key

Sections: Serving size; Calories; Fat (sat + trans); Carbohydrate (sugar incl. added, fibre); Protein; Vitamins and minerals; %DV column. Smart shopper compares per-100 g, sodium, added sugar, saturated fat, fibre, and the ingredient list (shorter, recognisable items, no sugar as first ingredient). Also notes priority-allergen statements and (mandatory from January 2026) the front-of-pack symbol for high sat fat/sodium/sugar.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[6]
Case study — Northern Food Costs: A family in Iqaluit reports that a 2-litre carton of milk costs $8.99 and a head of broccoli costs $6.49. Their household income is below the federal poverty line. (a) Identify three factors that drive northern food costs. (b) Describe the Nutrition North Canada subsidy and one critique. (c) Outline how Indigenous food sovereignty would complement (not replace) southern-style food assistance.
Answer Key

(a) Factors: long, complex transport (sea-lift in summer, air freight in winter); limited retail competition; high operating costs; short growing seasons; colonial supply-chain legacy; corporate concentration. (b) NNC is a federal subsidy on shipping costs for eligible perishable foods to isolated communities; criticised for opaque pass-through to consumers and uncertain price reductions. (c) Indigenous food sovereignty restores community-led harvesting (hunter-support programs), promotes traditional foods (caribou, seal, char, berries) and supports community freezers, language and intergenerational knowledge. Combines short-term subsidies with long-term self-determination and food security on culturally appropriate terms.

13
[6]
STSE — Community fridge: A local NGO opens a community fridge in a high-food-insecurity neighbourhood, stocked by volunteers and surplus from grocery stores. (a) Identify two strengths and two limitations. (b) Evaluate it against the income-based root cause of food insecurity. (c) Recommend two policies that would tackle the root cause.
Answer Key

(a) Strengths: immediate relief for acutely hungry; reduces food waste; builds community connection. Limitations: short-term supply (a few meals); inconsistent supply and quality; depends on volunteer labour; doesn't address income; can stigmatise (depending on design). (b) Community fridges do not address the income inadequacy that drives food insecurity; they are a stop-gap, not a solution. (c) Policies: (1) raise social-assistance rates so they cover actual cost of food + shelter; (2) guaranteed minimum income (e.g., expanded Canada Disability Benefit, GMI pilot continuation); (3) living-wage employment standards; (4) National School Food Program (2024) expansion. Income transfers consistently outperform charity on food-security outcomes.
