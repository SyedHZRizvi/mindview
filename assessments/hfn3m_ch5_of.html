Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [14 marks]
1
[3]
Define food security and food insecurity (FAO 1996). List the four pillars of food security.
Answer Key

Food security = all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life. Food insecurity = absence of any of these. Four pillars: availability, access, utilisation, stability.

2
[2]
The DSM-5 distinguishes anorexia nervosa from bulimia nervosa by:
Answer Key

B. Anorexia nervosa: restriction → significantly low body weight + intense fear of gaining weight + body-image disturbance. Bulimia nervosa: recurrent binge + compensatory behaviours (vomiting, laxatives, fasting, exercise), often normal or above-normal weight.

3
[3]
List four nutrient sources from a pre-contact Inuit traditional diet and the nutrient each provides.
Answer Key

Seal blubber/oil — energy + omega-3 EPA/DHA + vitamin D + vitamin A. Caribou organ meats (liver, kidneys) — vitamin A, iron, B12. Arctic char or salmon — omega-3 EPA/DHA + protein + vitamin D. Muktuk (whale skin) and berries — vitamin C. Traditional diets demonstrated full nutritional adequacy with no supplementation in pre-contact populations.

4
[3]
Name four current food trends and one limitation or critique of each.
Answer Key

(Any four) Keto — restricts whole grain, fruit, legumes; long-term CVD outcomes uncertain. Paleo — historical premise debated; no long-term CVD evidence. Intermittent fasting — modest benefit; main mechanism is reduced intake. "Clean eating" — undefined; can verge on orthorexia. Detox cleanses — body already detoxes via liver/kidneys; no evidence for "cleansing". Plant-based — well-supported; needs B12 supplementation.

5
[3]
List the 11 priority allergens that must be declared on Canadian food labels.
Answer Key

Peanuts, tree nuts, sesame, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish (crustaceans + molluscs), soy, wheat, sulphites, mustard. Plus gluten (wheat, rye, triticale, barley, oats) for celiac-relevant labelling.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[4]
Estimate the carbon footprint of two dinners and compare. Dinner A: 200 g beef sirloin, ½ cup rice, ½ cup broccoli. Dinner B: 1 cup lentil curry, ½ cup rice, ½ cup vegetables. Use these averages: beef 60 kg CO₂-eq/kg; cooked rice 2.7 kg/kg; vegetables 0.5 kg/kg; lentils cooked 0.9 kg/kg.
Answer Key

Dinner A: 0.2 × 60 = 12 (beef) + 0.125 × 2.7 ≈ 0.34 (rice) + 0.08 × 0.5 = 0.04 (broccoli) ≈ 12.4 kg CO₂-eq. Dinner B: 0.25 × 0.9 ≈ 0.23 (lentils) + 0.125 × 2.7 ≈ 0.34 (rice) + 0.08 × 0.5 = 0.04 (vegetables) ≈ 0.6 kg CO₂-eq. Dinner A produces ~20× more emissions than Dinner B for similar protein and energy.

7
[4]
A celebrity claims that an "alkaline diet" of cucumber and leafy greens "raises blood pH and prevents cancer". Using A1.4 inquiry skills, critique this claim under three headings: mechanism, evidence, source bias.
Answer Key

Mechanism: blood pH is buffered at 7.35–7.45 by bicarbonate + respiration + kidneys. Food cannot meaningfully shift blood pH. Excess acid or base is excreted by the kidney; if blood pH drifts even 0.1 unit it is a medical emergency. Evidence: no high-quality randomised trial supports alkaline diets for cancer. Source bias: celebrity endorsement, often selling branded supplements (conflict of interest). Plausible kernel: eating plenty of vegetables is healthy for many evidence-based reasons (fibre, micronutrients), but not because of "alkalisation".

8
[4]
A 16-year-old runner has lost 8 kg in 4 months, refuses meals with family, exercises 90 min twice a day, and stopped menstruating. List five DSM-5-style warning signs of anorexia nervosa and outline an evidence-based response plan.
Answer Key

Warning signs: (1) significant weight loss; (2) restriction of intake; (3) intense fear of weight gain implied by excessive exercise; (4) body-image disturbance (denying she is too thin); (5) amenorrhoea — formerly a DSM-IV criterion, still a clinical marker. Response plan: (1) approach privately, expressing concern about behaviour rather than appearance; (2) avoid comments about her body; (3) connect with NEDIC (1-866-633-4220) and the family physician; (4) consider family-based therapy (Maudsley method) as first-line; (5) urgent referral if medical instability (bradycardia <50, syncope, electrolyte abnormality).

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[4]
Explain the 2019 EAT–Lancet planetary-health diet. Reference both health and planetary outcomes.
Answer Key

The EAT–Lancet Commission (2019) proposed dietary targets compatible with planetary boundaries and human health: ~2 500 kcal/day, dominated by vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, with modest fish, poultry, eggs, dairy, and ≤14 g/day red meat plus very little added sugar. Modelled global adoption would prevent ~11 million premature deaths/year while halving the food system's GHG emissions. The diet's principles align well with the Mediterranean pattern and the 2019 Canada's Food Guide plate.

10
[4]
Construct a one-page community-pamphlet (text only) titled "Three Truths About Food Insecurity in Canada" aimed at a high-school audience. Cite at least two statistics.
Answer Key

TRUTH 1 — It is common: 18.4% of Canadian households (Statistics Canada, 2022) — about one in six — were food-insecure in the past 12 months. TRUTH 2 — It is not equal: rates exceed 30% in Indigenous off-reserve, Black-identifying, and single-parent households; and surpass 50% in some Nunavut communities. TRUTH 3 — Food banks help but do not solve it: 1.9 million food-bank visits per month in 2024. The deeper cause is income inadequacy. Policy levers that work: Canada Child Benefit, raising social-assistance rates, the new National School Food Program announced April 2024 ($1 B over 5 years).

11
[4]
Compare and contrast pre-contact Inuit and modern Northern processed-food diets in a 3-row table covering omega-3, vitamin D, vitamin C, fibre, and sodium. Note the health consequence.
Answer Key

Traditional: omega-3 high (seal, char); vitamin D high (oily fish, seal); vitamin C ~50 mg (muktuk, berries); fibre low; sodium ~1 g/day. Modern processed: omega-3 low; vitamin D low; vitamin C low; fibre low; sodium ~4 g/day. Health consequence: nutrition transition has driven ~3–5× higher type-2-diabetes rates and elevated cardiovascular disease in many Indigenous communities relative to the Canadian average. Country-food revival initiatives are a public-health priority for Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami and other Indigenous organisations.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[4]
Case Study — School cafeteria sustainability audit. A school cafeteria serves daily: beef burgers, chicken nuggets, instant-noodle cups, sugary drinks. Apply EAT–Lancet, planetary-health, and Food-Guide principles. Suggest three concrete changes the cafeteria can make in one semester at minimal cost increase.
Answer Key

Critique: heavy on red meat and ultra-processed foods; no plant-protein day; sugar-sweetened beverages dominant. Changes (low-cost): (1) Add a "Meatless Monday" with lentil chili, hummus wraps, or bean burritos — costs ~$0.50 less per serving than beef. (2) Replace sugary drinks with water bottles, infused waters, and unsweetened milk by default; offer juice as exception. (3) Add a salad bar with seasonal vegetables sourced from Foodland Ontario growers; price by weight. Estimate ~15% reduction in cafeteria CO₂ footprint and substantial improvement in Food-Guide compliance. Track student acceptance and adjust recipes.

13
[4]
Case Study — Bill S-228 debate. The proposed federal Child Health Protection Act would have restricted commercial advertising of unhealthy foods to children <13. Identify two evidence-based arguments in favour and one industry argument against. State your evidence-based recommendation.
Answer Key

Arguments in favour: (1) Strong evidence linking ad exposure to preference, request, and consumption of advertised foods (mostly high in sugar, salt, saturated fat). (2) Quebec's 1980 ban is associated with lower fast-food consumption and lower childhood obesity in French-speaking Québec children compared with English-speaking Québec children who are exposed to ROC television. Industry argument against: voluntary self-regulation (Canadian Children's Food and Beverage Advertising Initiative) is sufficient; ban would harm broadcasting business. Recommendation: evidence supports stronger restrictions — voluntary self-regulation has not reduced ad exposure meaningfully. Modelled population health benefit and Quebec's natural experiment make S-228-style legislation a defensible public-health intervention. The bill died in 2019 but the policy debate continues.

14
[4]
STSE — Plant-based meat alternatives. Beyond Beef and similar products mimic ground beef. Critically evaluate them for (i) nutrition vs ground beef, (ii) carbon footprint, (iii) NOVA classification.
Answer Key

(i) Nutrition: similar energy (~250 kcal/100 g) and protein (~20 g); typically less saturated fat than beef but more sodium; iron and B12 fortified to match beef levels. Beef offers heme iron (more bioavailable) and naturally-occurring B12; plant-based alternatives match these through fortification. (ii) Carbon footprint: ~85–90% lower than beef per gram of protein (life-cycle assessments by University of Michigan and others), making plant-based meat a meaningful climate intervention. (iii) NOVA classification: Group 4 (ultra-processed) because of methylcellulose, soy/pea protein isolates, beet-juice colourant, etc. Verdict: lower carbon and saturated fat than beef, but eaten in moderation alongside whole plant foods (legumes, tofu, tempeh) — not as a primary protein.
