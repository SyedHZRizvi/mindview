Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) Shannon-Weaver's elements of communication; (b) Carl Rogers's "active listening"; (c) the Behaviour-Impact-Suggestion (BIS) feedback model.
Answer Key

(a) Shannon-Weaver: sender, encoded message, channel, receiver, noise (1949); Schramm later added the feedback loop. (b) Active listening: Rogers's term (1957) for purposeful listening to understand the speaker's experience, not to respond to it — attending behaviours, open questions, paraphrasing, reflecting feeling, silence. (c) BIS: structured feedback model — Behaviour (specific observable action), Impact (effect of that behaviour), Suggestion (concrete alternative for next time). Prevents both vague praise and personal attack.

2
[2]
In the Thomas-Kilmann conflict model, the five styles are plotted on which two axes?
Answer Key

A. Assertiveness (how much you pursue your own concerns) and cooperativeness (how much you pursue the other party's concerns). The five styles emerge at different combinations: competing, accommodating, avoiding, compromising, collaborating.

3
[2]
In 2–3 sentences, explain why the "sandwich method" of feedback (praise-criticism-praise) often fails.
Answer Key

The sandwich method often delivers neither honest praise (the opening praise is felt as setup, not real recognition) nor clear criticism (the criticism is softened so much that the receiver does not register the actionable point). It also trains the receiver to brace whenever praise comes in. A clearer alternative is BIS (Behaviour-Impact-Suggestion): name the specific behaviour, describe the impact, offer a concrete suggestion. Or feed-forward (Goldsmith): focus on what to do next time.

4
[3]
Define each: (a) passive communication; (b) passive-aggressive communication; (c) assertive communication. Give one short example sentence in each style for the same situation (a teammate is consistently late).
Answer Key

(a) Passive: avoiding your own needs to keep peace. "Oh, no worries, it's fine." (b) Passive-aggressive: indirect expression of frustration. *Sighs loudly when teammate enters; posts vague subtweet.* (c) Assertive: clear statement of needs while respecting others. "I want to flag something — you've been 20 minutes late to the last three meetings. I'd like us to either start on time or shift our meeting to a time that works better. Which would you prefer?" Marks: 1 per style + example.

5
[3]
Identify and briefly describe three of the IIRP's restorative questions.
Answer Key

Any three of: (1) What happened? (2) What were you thinking at the time? (3) What have you thought about since? (4) Who has been affected by what you did, and how? (5) What do you think you need to do to make things as right as possible? For the harmed party: What did you think when you realised? What impact has this had? What has been the hardest thing? What needs to happen to make things right? The framing is curious, not accusatory — invites accountability rather than triggering defensiveness.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Mateo texts his friend Lucia at 11 p.m. on a Thursday during exam-prep week: "We need to talk." Lucia spirals into anxiety for two days, sure she has done something wrong. They meet on Saturday; Mateo wanted to ask whether she'd co-host a charity event.
Diagnose the failed communication using Shannon-Weaver. In 5 sentences, identify the encoding problem, the channel problem, the noise, and the feedback gap; suggest a better encoding for the same message.
Answer Key

Encoding: "We need to talk" is an emotionally-loaded phrase that triggers relationship-threat schemas; mismatched the actual content (an invitation). Channel: text strips tone and context; channel-content mismatch (sensitive-feeling content on lightweight channel). Noise: Lucia is in exam-prep stress; her psychological state amplifies the threat reading. Feedback loop: two-day delay in the feedback loop; no opportunity to correct the read in real time. Better encoding: "Hey — want to co-host the Spring Drive with me? No pressure, just throwing the idea out — happy to chat at lunch tomorrow." Same intent; much less noise. Marks: 1 per element, 1 for the rewrite.

7
[4]
Apply the Thomas-Kilmann model to three different scenarios. For each, identify the conflict style you would recommend and justify in one sentence: (a) a fire alarm during your group meeting; (b) a teammate insisting on a topic for a project you both feel strongly about; (c) a low-stakes preference between two equally-acceptable lunch options.
Answer Key

(a) Competing — emergencies demand speed; "Everyone out, now." (b) Collaborating — both care; both have a future relationship; invest the time to find a third option that captures what each values. (c) Accommodating (or compromising) — low stakes; save energy for things that matter. The point of the question is to demonstrate style-flexibility: different situations, different correct answers. Marks: 1 per match, 1 for synthesis comment that no single style is "best."

8
[4]
Apply Heen and Stone's three feedback triggers (truth, relationship, identity). In 4 sentences, describe a recent piece of feedback you received, identify which trigger(s) it activated, and explain how the trigger awareness could have changed your response.
Answer Key

Strong response names a specific piece of feedback, identifies the trigger ("the feedback came from someone I don't fully trust — relationship trigger"; "the feedback challenged my self-picture as 'the careful one' — identity trigger"; "the feedback felt factually wrong — truth trigger"), and identifies how trigger-awareness could change response ("if I'd named 'this is a relationship trigger' to myself, I could have asked: is the message still useful even though I don't fully trust the source? often yes"). Marks: 1 specific situation, 1 trigger naming, 1 reflection on response, 1 framework application.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write an analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) on the claim: "Restorative practices in schools are softer than punitive discipline and therefore less effective." Engage the research evidence and at least one counter-argument.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) thesis disagreeing with the claim (restorative practices are often harder, not softer); (2) evidence — Anne Gregory's Rutgers research, WestEd evaluations, reductions in suspension and disciplinary disparities, modest school-climate improvements; (3) recognition of complexity — restorative practices require both a culture-building (community-circle) layer AND a responsive layer; without the foundation, the responsive work fails; (4) counter-argument — they don't work for every incident, and for serious harm (e.g., sexual assault) restorative approaches must be paired with safety measures; (5) honouring the Indigenous and Māori roots of the practices. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 counter-engagement, 1 conventions.

10
[6]
Write a coherent 8–10 sentence comparison of two helping conversation styles: (a) advice-giving and (b) active listening (Rogers/Covey). For each, identify when it is appropriate, when it backfires, and what skills are required. Reference Unit 2 frameworks.
Answer Key

Advice-giving: appropriate when the peer asks for advice and you have relevant experience; backfires when offered before the peer has felt heard, when the situation is emotional rather than instrumental, or when it cuts off the peer's autonomy. Requires: subject knowledge, restraint about timing. Active listening: appropriate when the peer needs to be heard, when the emotion outweighs the instrumental need, when the peer is finding their own answer; backfires when the peer is in immediate danger (referral required) or when actually they just want logistical advice. Requires: attending behaviours, open questions, paraphrasing, reflecting feeling, tolerating silence. Strong synthesis: active listening typically comes first; advice (if any) follows the listening, not the other way around. Marks: 1 per appropriate-context, 1 per backfire, 1 per skill set, 2 for synthesis.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
Your group of six is two weeks into a class project. At today's meeting, Sara argues the focus should be Indigenous mental-health resources; Liam argues for LGBTQ resources; Mateo argues for general anti-stigma; Aanya is quiet but visibly frustrated; Priya tries to mediate but ends up confusing things; Devon checks his phone.
Apply Tuckman's stages and active-listening skills to plan your next 10 minutes as the group leader. In 5–6 sentences, describe what stage the group is in, what concrete moves you'd make, and what you'd specifically avoid.
Answer Key

Recognise the group is in storming — real disagreement about direction. Concrete moves: (1) name what's happening ("I want to surface what's going on — we're in storming, this is normal and good"); (2) hold space for each voice with an open question ("Sara, Liam, Mateo — what would have to be true for your idea to be the right one?"); (3) include the quiet voices ("Aanya, what's your read? Devon, put down the phone for ten minutes and tell us what you see"); (4) look for an integrative third option (a daily focus that rotates); (5) confirm before moving on. Avoid: smoothing over with a forced vote; personalising ("Devon is being disrespectful"); shutting down the storming. Marks: 1 stage identification, 2 specific moves, 1 inclusion of quiet voices, 1 what to avoid.

12
[4]
Apply the BIS feedback model. Rewrite the following weak feedback into strong BIS feedback: "You did okay at the presentation, but you should slow down a bit. Good energy though!"
Answer Key

Sample BIS rewrite: "Behaviour: When you got to Slide 3, you sped up and read the text word-for-word. Impact: The audience couldn't tell which of those five points you were emphasising — I noticed three people looked at their phones during that minute. Suggestion: Try summarising each slide in your own words in one sentence before going into detail, and pause for two seconds before each transition. The opening question hooked the room — that worked really well — and applying the same energy with slower pacing through the middle would make the whole talk stronger." Marks: 1 each for B, I, S identifiable; 1 for honest balance (recognising what worked).

13
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: Identify one place in your school or community where restorative practices could be applied to address a recurring conflict pattern. In 3–4 sentences, describe the pattern and outline one specific restorative intervention you would propose.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) specific recurring pattern (Grade 9 lunchroom exclusion; team conflicts among student-council; social-media-driven cliques; sports-team hazing); (2) concrete restorative response (community circles in advisory periods; trained peer-mediator first responders; an opening protocol for new team formations); (3) recognition that restorative work requires culture-building, not just incident-response. Strong responses match the intervention to the harm's scale and avoid restorative-as-magic-bullet framing. Marks: 1 pattern, 1 intervention, 1 communication/realism.
