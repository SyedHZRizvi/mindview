Topics 2.1–2.2 — Communication Models and Active Listening
Question 1
In Shannon and Weaver's communication model, "noise" refers to:
Solution: Noise is multi-source — physical (a loud cafeteria), psychological (the receiver is tired), semantic (a word means different things), cultural (gestures and norms vary). Effective communicators check for understanding instead of assuming.
Question 2
Mehrabian's "7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language" rule is correctly understood as:
Solution: Mehrabian (1967) studied messages where verbal and non-verbal channels conflicted. The accurate generalisation: when channels conflict, receivers trust non-verbal cues. The "7-38-55" numbers do NOT mean those are the relative weights of all communication.
Question 3
Peer: "I just feel like everything is falling apart." You (after 4 seconds of silence): "It sounds like things feel really overwhelming right now."
Your response uses which active-listening technique?
Solution: Reflecting feeling — naming the emotion tentatively ("it sounds like..."). The 4-second silence beforehand is also a technique: most peer-helpers under-use silence. Together they create space for the peer to go deeper.
Topics 2.3–2.4 — Assertive Communication and Feedback
Question 4
The classic assertive I-statement structure is:
Solution: The assertive I-statement owns the feeling, specifies the behaviour, explains the impact, and offers a concrete request. The other options are aggressive, passive, and passive-aggressive respectively.
Question 5
Which is the strongest example of constructive (BIS) feedback?
Solution: (b) names the Behaviour ("read slide 3 word-for-word"), the Impact ("audience lost the main idea"), and offers a Suggestion. (a) is vague; (c) is personal attack; (d) is empty.
Question 6
Heen and Stone's three "triggers" that produce defensiveness when receiving feedback are:
Solution: Truth triggers (the feedback feels wrong), relationship triggers (the source isn't trusted), identity triggers (the feedback contradicts self-image). Naming the trigger ("I'm having a relationship trigger right now") helps you separate the message from the noise.
Topics 2.5–2.6 — Conflict Styles and Restorative Practices
Question 7
In the Thomas–Kilmann model, the "Collaborating" style is characterised by:
Solution: Collaborating is high on both axes; (d) describes Compromising. Collaboration finds a "both win" third option; compromise splits the difference. Both are valuable but different.
Question 8
No single Thomas–Kilmann conflict style is "best." Each fits some situations and not others. Which situation calls for the COMPETING style (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness)?
Solution: Competing is right in emergencies, on principle, or when speed is critical. A fire alarm calls for "everyone, out, now." The others call for collaborating, accommodating, and collaborating respectively.
Question 9
Restorative practices are rooted in:
Solution: Restorative practices have deep roots in Indigenous justice traditions and were adapted into Western criminal and educational settings in the late 20th century. Honouring those roots — not just borrowing the techniques — matters.
Synthesis
Question 10
The IIRP's five restorative questions include all EXCEPT:
Solution: Restorative questions are curious, not accusatory. They ask "what happened" not "why are you bad." This framing invites accountability rather than triggers defensiveness.
Question 11
Which channel is appropriate for delivering hard, sensitive feedback for the first time?
Solution: Face-to-face (or video) allows for tone, real-time clarification, and dignity-preserving privacy. Text and email strip tone; group/public channels add humiliation, which destroys learning.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, describe one situation in which you would use the AVOIDING conflict style legitimately, and one situation in which avoiding would be a mistake.
Sample: Legitimate avoiding: a teammate has just received bad news and is upset; raising your concern about their missed deadline today would be cruel. Take an hour. The conflict is real; the timing isn't. Mistake avoiding: you've felt overlooked at the last three meetings and keep telling yourself "it's fine." It isn't fine; the resentment is building; the longer you avoid, the bigger the eventual conversation will be. Avoiding is a tool — useful in the short term, destructive as a default.
