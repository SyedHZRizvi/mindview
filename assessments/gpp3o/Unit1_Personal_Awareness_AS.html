Topics 1.1–1.2 — Self-Awareness and Emotional Intelligence
Question 1
According to Tasha Eurich's research, most adults:
Solution: Eurich found roughly 10–15% of adults have what her team called meta-cognitive accuracy — the rest overestimate. The lesson: external feedback is essential to calibrate self-knowledge.
Question 2
Goleman's five domains of emotional intelligence are:
Solution: Goleman's framework: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills — the first three are intrapersonal, the last two interpersonal.
Question 3
"I notice I'm getting frustrated. This conversation has gone in circles for ten minutes."
This is an example of:
Solution: Affect labelling (Lieberman, UCLA) — naming an emotion deactivates some of its automatic response. This is foundational to building the gap between trigger and reaction.
Topics 1.3–1.4 — Learning Styles, Strengths, and Values
Question 4
VARK is Neil Fleming's model of:
Solution: VARK (1987) — Visual, Auditory, Reading/Writing, Kinesthetic. Note: the strong claim "teach to the style and outcomes improve" is not well-supported; the weak claim "preferences vary; mixed-mode delivery reaches more people" is supported.
Question 5
The Seven Grandfather Teachings from Anishinaabe tradition are:
Solution: The Seven Grandfather Teachings — wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility, truth — describe a values framework grounding leadership and life in Anishinaabe traditions. TRC Call to Action 62 calls on K-12 systems to integrate Indigenous teachings.
Question 6
A "values card-sort" exercise primarily helps you:
Solution: The card-sort narrows. The narrowing is the work — values that survive being chosen against an easier path are the real ones; the rest are aspirational.
Topic 1.5 — Resilience and Stress Management
Question 7
Ann Masten's research describes resilience as:
Solution: Masten's term "ordinary magic" describes resilience as the regular use of available supports — relationships, routines, meaning — by most people. Resilience is built, not born.
Question 8
Hans Selye's distinction between eustress and distress is based on:
Solution: Both eustress and distress produce similar physiological signals (cortisol, adrenaline, raised heart rate); the difference is in duration, intensity, and appraisal. Kelly McGonigal showed that the appraisal itself shapes the response.
Question 9
In Ontario, a peer-helper supporting a friend who discloses persistent suicidal thoughts should:
Solution: Suicidal ideation is beyond peer-helper scope. The peer-helper's job is to bring in professional help — that's competent peer support, not betrayal. 9-8-8 (suicide crisis), Kids Help Phone 1-800-668-6868, and Hope for Wellness 1-855-242-3310 are all 24/7 resources.
Topic 1.6 — SMART Goals and Time Management
Question 10
Which of these is a SMART goal?
Solution: (b) is Specific (training + co-mediation), Measurable (training completed; one mediation), Achievable, Relevant (peer-mediator role), and Time-bound (December 6). The others are wishes.
Question 11
Stephen Covey's time matrix identifies four quadrants by urgency and importance. The quadrant where leadership growth happens most is:
Solution: The "important, not urgent" quadrant is where long-term work, relationships, health, and leadership growth live. Without protected time here, you live in crisis-management mode.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, describe one specific time-management or stress-management practice from Unit 1 that you intend to apply this semester. Why this one?
Sample: I'm going to do a Sunday-evening weekly review (15 minutes), scanning the week's commitments and pre-deciding which two are "important, not urgent" leadership-growth tasks I will protect time for. I chose this because last term I lived in firefighting mode — everything urgent, nothing developmental — and I ended the term tired and behind on the things that actually mattered. The weekly review converts "I should make time for X" into "X is on Wednesday from 4 to 5." A concrete, repeatable practice; one I can actually do.
