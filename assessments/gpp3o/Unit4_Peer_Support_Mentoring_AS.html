Topics 4.1–4.2 — Peer Support Forms and the Helping Conversation
Question 1
A peer-helper's primary role is best described as:
Solution: Peer-helpers are bridges, not destinations. The role is to listen, support, recognise when a situation is beyond peer-helper scope, and connect to professionals. Trying to be a peer-therapist exceeds scope and is unsafe.
Question 2
The five-step helping conversation framework is:
Solution: Engage (set the time/place/frame), Listen (active listening), Clarify (summarise and check), Options (offer pathways, not advice), Next Steps (specific small commitment + follow-up). Real conversations loop through Listen-Clarify multiple times.
Question 3
Peer: "I don't know, everything's just falling apart." You: "Have you tried making a list and prioritising?"
The peer-helper's response is problematic because:
Solution: Skipping Listen — jumping to advice in the first two minutes — closes the conversation. The peer has not yet said the actual hard thing; advice arrives at the wrong layer. Better: reflect the feeling, ask an open question, tolerate silence.
Topics 4.3–4.4 — Confidentiality and Referral
Question 4
A peer-helper's standard opening on confidentiality should:
Solution: Stating exceptions upfront protects both parties — the peer knows what will trigger help; the peer-helper isn't betraying anyone by responding to those triggers. Ontario's CYFSA section 125 imposes legal duty to report reasonable suspicion of child abuse.
Question 5
Under Ontario's Child, Youth and Family Services Act (2017), the duty to report reasonable suspicion of child abuse:
Solution: CYFSA section 125 imposes a duty on every person with reasonable grounds to suspect abuse of a child under 16 to report directly to a Children's Aid Society. The duty is personal — telling a teacher is wise but doesn't fully discharge the duty.
Question 6
Which of the following is a 24/7 Canada-wide suicide crisis line, launched in 2023?
Solution: 9-8-8 launched in Canada in late 2023; call or text for 24/7 suicide crisis support. Memorise alongside: Kids Help Phone 1-800-668-6868, Hope for Wellness 1-855-242-3310, Black Youth Helpline 1-833-294-8650.
Topics 4.5–4.6 — Mentoring and Boundaries
Question 7
Mentoring is distinguished from peer support primarily by:
Solution: Mentoring is sustained and developmental — walking alongside a less-experienced person over weeks or months toward growth. Peer support is typically a single conversation or short series. Both are valuable; the training and time commitments differ.
Question 8
Christina Maslach's research names six drivers of burnout. They include:
Solution: Maslach's six drivers: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, values mismatch. The fix is structural, not just motivational — reduce workload, increase control, recognise contribution, build community, ensure fairness, align with values.
Question 9
A "dual relationship" boundary issue arises when:
Solution: Dual relationships combine the helper role with another role. The combination usually compromises one or both — peer-mediating between siblings, peer-counselling a romantic partner. Generally avoided or carefully managed with supervision.
Question 10
Big Brothers Big Sisters Canada research shows mentoring benefits are largest when:
Solution: Sustained (12+ months) and structured (matched, trained, supervised) mentoring shows the strongest evidence of benefit. Short or unstructured "mentoring" can have positive effects but they are smaller and less consistent.
Question 11
When a peer-helper is becoming depleted (emotional exhaustion, dread, sleep disruption), the right response is:
Solution: Burnout is structural. Tell the coordinator early, reduce caseload, take a break. The coordinator's job includes monitoring helper wellbeing. Hidden burnout produces poor help and personal harm.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, describe the kind of peer-support boundary that is most difficult for you to maintain, and one specific practice you would adopt to protect it.
Sample: My hardest boundary is time — when a friend texts at midnight in crisis, my instinct is to stay up. I'm going to adopt the practice of replying with "I'm here, but for tonight, can we please call Kids Help Phone together? I'll stay on text with you while you call." That keeps me present without making myself the only resource — and protects my sleep, which I need to actually be useful tomorrow. Strong responses name a specific boundary, acknowledge why it's hard, and give a concrete practice.
