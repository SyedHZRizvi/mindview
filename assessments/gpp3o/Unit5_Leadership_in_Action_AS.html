Topics 5.1–5.2 — Charter, Back-Planning, Risk
Question 1
A project charter typically has six sections. Which is NOT a standard section?
Solution: Standard sections: Purpose, Scope, Stakeholders, Success Criteria, Resources, Risks. A hashtag is marketing, not planning.
Question 2
"Back-planning" means:
Solution: Back-planning starts from the deadline and works backward to today's tasks. It exposes dependencies and prevents the common student-project failure of running out of runway.
Question 3
The four standard risk responses are:
Solution: Avoid (change the plan to remove the risk); Mitigate (reduce probability or impact); Transfer (shift the risk to someone better placed); Accept (acknowledge and plan a fallback). Used together depending on risk type.
Topics 5.3–5.4 — Team Stages and Implementation
Question 4
Tuckman's stages of group development, in order, are:
Solution: Forming (initial uncertainty), Storming (disagreement surfaces), Norming (shared methods develop), Performing (work flows), Adjourning (1977 addition; team disbands and reflects).
Question 5
Team member: "Why are we even doing this — we should pivot to a totally different approach." Another member: "Are you saying our work is wrong?" Tension is rising.
In Tuckman's stages, this is most likely:
Solution: Storming — most-uncomfortable stage; teams try to skip it. The leader's job is to name the stage and hold space for productive disagreement, not smooth it over (which sends the conflict underground).
Question 6
A weekly "stand-up" is typically:
Solution: Stand-ups are short and frequent — three questions per person. Problems surface early; the team stays aligned; work flows. Most professional teams use a version of this.
Topic 5.5 — Reflection
Question 7
The six stages of Gibbs's Reflective Cycle are:
Solution: Gibbs (1988): Description (facts, no judgment), Feelings (emotional reality), Evaluation (good/bad), Analysis (connect to frameworks), Conclusion (what learned), Action Plan (specific next-time commitment).
Question 8
An "action plan" in Gibbs's cycle should be:
Solution: The action plan is what makes reflection portable to future situations. "Next time X happens, I will do Y" is testable. Vague aspirations are not.
Topic 5.6 — Portfolio and Pathways
Question 9
A strong leadership-portfolio artefact for a project typically includes:
Solution: The portfolio artefact names what you specifically did and what changed. Documentation is leadership too — the work matters and the documentation makes the work visible to admissions committees, employers, and your own future self.
Question 10
The Ontario Individual Pathways Plan (IPP) is:
Solution: The IPP is maintained from K through 12; students record self-knowledge, opportunities explored, decisions, and reflection. GPP3O is a key contributor in Grade 11; the IPP is the bridge to postsecondary applications and pathway planning.
Question 11
Which of these is most likely to be valued by university supplementary-essay readers and college-program interviewers?
Solution: Specific stories with named impact, reflective insight, and forward-looking connection are what admissions readers find compelling. Lists without specifics or stories without reflection are less effective.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, identify one leadership initiative (current or possible) at your school or community, and one specific aspect of Unit 5's frameworks that would help make it more successful. Be concrete.
Sample: Our school's eco-club has been running an annual waste audit for three years but rarely produces follow-up. The fix from Unit 5 is back-planning combined with a stand-up rhythm — set the audit-report date six weeks out, weekly 15-minute stand-ups for each of those six weeks where each member reports their tasks, blockers, and progress. The weekly visibility would catch slippage early; right now we're running on goodwill and getting tired in week four. Strong responses name a specific initiative + a specific framework.
