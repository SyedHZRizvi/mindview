Topic 1.1 — Literary Devices
Question 1
"Life for me ain't been no crystal stair." — Langston Hughes, "Mother to Son"
The literary device most clearly at work in this line is:
Solution: The speaker says life has not been a crystal stair — directly comparing life to a stair (without "like" or "as"). That is a metaphor. Hughes extends the metaphor across the whole poem with tacks, splinters, and torn-up boards.
Question 2
"The wind whispered through the trees."
The device at work in this sentence is:
Solution: Wind cannot literally whisper — only humans can. Giving a non-human thing a human ability is personification.
Question 3
In a story where the audience knows Juliet is only sleeping but Romeo believes she is dead, the irony is best described as:
Solution: Dramatic irony — there is a gap between what the audience knows and what Romeo knows. Verbal irony would be a character saying the opposite of what they mean; situational irony would be an unexpected outcome.
Topic 1.2 — Close Reading
Question 4
"TRUE!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am! but why will you say that I am mad?" — Edgar Allan Poe, "The Tell-Tale Heart"
The narrator's opening sentence most clearly signals that:
Solution: The all-caps "TRUE!", the stutters ("very, very"), and the rhetorical question "why will you say that I am mad?" together create an unreliable narrator. He insists on his sanity so insistently that the reader doubts him.
Question 5
A theme statement is best understood as:
Solution: A theme is a claim — a complete sentence asserting how the text sees a topic — not a single word. "Love" is a topic; "Hughes's poem argues that love takes the form of instruction, not promises" is a theme.
Topic 1.3 — Reading Poetry
Question 6
"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? / Thou art more lovely and more temperate." — Shakespeare, Sonnet 18
The dominant metre of these lines is:
Solution: Scan: "Shall I / com-PARE / thee TO / a SUM- / mer's DAY?" — five iambs. Iambic pentameter is Shakespeare's standard verse.
Question 7
The Shakespearean sonnet is structured as:
Solution: The Shakespearean (English) sonnet has 14 lines in three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a final couplet (GG) — typically delivering a turn or resolution. Choice (a) describes the Petrarchan sonnet.
Topic 1.4 — Paraphrase to Analysis
Question 8
In a PEEL paragraph (Point–Evidence–Explain–Link), the "Explain" step requires you to:
Solution: PEEL: Point (topic sentence), Evidence (quotation), Explain (interpretation of how the evidence proves the point — the analysis), Link (connection back to thesis). The Explain step is where literary analysis happens.
Question 9
Which of the following best embeds a quotation into a sentence?
Solution: Choice (b) integrates the quotation grammatically into the sentence with a signal phrase ("Romeo's declaration that") and a citation, then immediately interprets the line. The others are floating quotations.
Topic 1.5 — Thesis Statements
Question 10
Which of the following is a thesis rather than a topic?
Solution: A thesis is specific, debatable, and supportable. Choice (b) names the text, the technique, and a precise interpretation that someone could disagree with. Choice (a) announces intent; (c) and (d) state facts no one disputes.
