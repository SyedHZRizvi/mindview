Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [12 marks]
1
[3]
Define and give an example of each: (a) bandwagon, (b) testimonial, (c) glittering generality.
Answer Key

(a) Bandwagon — appeal to popularity ("Join the millions"). (b) Testimonial — endorsement by celebrity, expert, or ordinary person. (c) Glittering generality — vague positive language without specifics ("Premium quality. Total satisfaction.").

2
[3]
Define each of Aristotle's three appeals in the context of advertising: (a) ethos, (b) pathos, (c) logos. Give one advertising example for each.
Answer Key

(a) Ethos — appeal to credibility/character; e.g., a doctor in a white coat endorses a medication. (b) Pathos — appeal to emotion; e.g., a charity ad showing a child reuniting with a parent. (c) Logos — appeal to logic/evidence; e.g., "Three out of four dentists recommend …"

3
[2]
In Caulfield's SIFT method for evaluating online claims, what does each letter stand for?
Answer Key

Stop (before sharing); Investigate the source; Find better coverage in reputable outlets; Trace claims, quotes, and media to the original context.

4
[2]
A camera angle that looks down at a subject is called and typically suggests:
Answer Key

B. High angle (camera above the subject) makes the subject appear weak or vulnerable. Low angle reverses this — subject appears powerful.

5
[2]
Define the difference between misinformation and disinformation.
Answer Key

Misinformation — false content shared without intent to deceive (the sharer believes it). Disinformation — false content shared with intent to deceive (the sharer or original creator knows it is false).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[6]
A 30-second TV ad opens with footage of a melting glacier and the voiceover "The Earth has 12 years left," cuts to children running in a green park, and ends with a charity logo and the words "Join us. Donate today."
Analyse this ad. Identify (a) two persuasion techniques, (b) one ethical concern about its construction, (c) the implied audience response, and (d) what the ad omits.
Answer Key

(a) Pathos through children and glacier imagery; emotional juxtaposition (despair → hope); urgency through "12 years." (b) Ethics: oversimplified science (the "12 years" figure is a popular metaphor from a 2018 IPCC special report on 1.5 °C limits, not a literal deadline for "catastrophe"); use of children's images for fundraising can be questioned. (c) Implied response: viewer's grief and protectiveness redirected into immediate donation. (d) Omissions: where the donation goes, the charity's overhead ratio, what specifically the donation funds. Marks: 2 techniques, 2 ethics, 1 implied response, 1 omissions.

7
[4]
Analyse the following social-media headline: "You Won't BELIEVE What This Teacher Did to a Student! #JusticeNow"
Answer Key

The headline uses (1) clickbait formula — "You won't believe" promises a strong emotional payoff; (2) vague subject — neither teacher nor student named, so the reader has to click; (3) moral framing — "#JusticeNow" implies an injustice has occurred before any evidence is offered; (4) capitalisation as emphasis — "BELIEVE" all-caps signals urgency. The combination is engineered to provoke outrage-engagement without committing to a fact. A SIFT response: stop, look up the actual incident, find reputable coverage. Marks: 2 techniques, 1 omission, 1 SIFT response.

8
[3]
Compare and contrast a news article and an opinion column on the same topic. List three specific differences you would expect to see in (a) source attribution, (b) diction, and (c) structure.
Answer Key

(a) Sources: a news article attributes every claim ("according to Statistics Canada"); an opinion column may rely on the writer's experience and reasoning, citing sources selectively. (b) Diction: news uses neutral terms ("the policy"); opinion may use evaluative diction ("the overdue policy"). (c) Structure: news uses inverted pyramid (most important first); opinion follows argument logic (hook → claim → reasons → call to action). Marks: 1 per category.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[8]
Major Task — Media-Creation Plan

Plan a 60-second public-service announcement (PSA) on ONE of the following topics:
1. Distracted driving by teenagers.
2. Social-media literacy for incoming Grade 9 students.
3. Climate-friendly transit choices for high-schoolers.

Your plan should include:
(a) target-audience analysis (2–3 sentences),
(b) a 4-shot storyboard (each shot specifies: framing/shot type, action, on-screen text, sound),
(c) one ethical consideration in your construction,
(d) one reflection on which Unit 5 concept(s) shaped your design.
Marking Rubric

(a) Audience analysis (2): age-appropriate, prior knowledge identified, platform considered. (b) Storyboard (4): four shots, each with framing, action, on-screen text, and sound. (c) Ethics (1): one specific ethical concern (accuracy, fact-checking, consent, no fear-mongering, no graphic imagery without warning). (d) Reflection (1): one specific Unit 5 concept (codes/conventions, ethos/pathos/logos, SIFT, audience analysis).

10
[5]
Identify and correct each error in the following passage from a draft PSA script:

"Their are too many teenagers who's lifes are effected by social media. Each of them are spending hours per day scrolling, but they doesnt realize the affect it has on they're mental health."
Answer Key

(1) "There are" (existential there, not possessive their). (2) "whose lives" (possessive whose; "lives" is the plural of "life"). (3) "affected by" (affect = verb, effect = noun). (4) "Each of them is spending" (each = singular subject). (5) "they don't realize" (correct contraction with apostrophe). (6) "the effect it has" (noun). (7) "their mental health" (possessive). Marks: 0.5 per correctly identified and corrected error; full marks for ≥10 corrections.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
Apply media-literacy skills. Choose any real advertisement (TV, print, online) that you have seen recently and analyse it in 5–7 sentences. Identify the audience, the dominant persuasion technique, the main appeal (ethos/pathos/logos), and one thing the ad omits or hides.
Answer Key

Look for: specific ad reference (1), target audience identified (1), dominant technique named (1), correct appeal identified (1), one specific omission (1). Acceptable choices: any current ad — perfume, smartphone, fast food, energy drink, streaming service, political campaign.

12
[4]
Apply the SIFT method. Imagine a friend forwards you a screenshot of a viral claim ("Scientists at the University of [X] have proven that [shocking claim]"). Describe, step-by-step, what you would do in 3–5 minutes to evaluate the claim.
Answer Key

Strong responses walk through the four SIFT moves with specifics: (1) Stop — pause before forwarding or believing; (2) Investigate the source — look up the named university and the researcher (lateral reading in a new tab); (3) Find better coverage — check whether reputable outlets (CBC, BBC, New York Times) have reported the claim; (4) Trace to original — if the claim cites a study, find the actual paper (or its abstract); confirm wording, methodology, sample size, and whether peer-reviewed. Marks: 1 per move correctly applied.

13
[3]
STSE / Cross-strand Synthesis: Connect a skill from this unit (Strand D) with a skill from an earlier unit (Strand B or C). In 3–4 sentences, identify the connection and explain why both skills are versions of the same critical-attention practice.
Answer Key

Strong responses might note: close-reading a poem (Unit 1/4) is structurally the same skill as close-reading a 30-second ad (Unit 5); building a thesis (Unit 1) is the same skill as designing a media-text rationale (Unit 5); ethos/pathos/logos in persuasive essays (Unit 2) is the same framework as ethos/pathos/logos in advertising (Unit 5). Marks: 1 connection identified, 1 explanation of the shared skill, 1 specific example.
