Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define each device and give one example: (a) verbal irony, (b) dramatic irony, (c) situational irony.
Answer Key

(a) Verbal irony — saying the opposite of what is meant; e.g., "Lovely weather!" during a storm. (b) Dramatic irony — audience knows something the character does not; e.g., the audience knows Juliet is asleep, but Romeo believes her dead. (c) Situational irony — outcome is the opposite of what was expected; e.g., a fire station burns down.

2
[2]
Identify the rhyme scheme of a Shakespearean sonnet:
Answer Key

A. Three quatrains in alternating rhyme + final couplet. The couplet typically delivers a turn.

3
[2]
Define tone and distinguish it from mood.
Answer Key

Tone is the speaker's (or author's) attitude toward the subject — bitter, reverent, hopeful, sardonic. Mood is the emotional atmosphere the text creates in the reader — eerie, joyful, anxious. Tone is produced; mood is felt.

4
[3]
Name and define three points of view used in narrative fiction.
Answer Key

First-person ("I"): the narrator is a character inside the story (Poe's narrator in "The Tell-Tale Heart"). Third-person limited: the narrator is outside but follows one character's mind. Third-person omniscient: the narrator knows every character's interior. Also acceptable: second-person ("you"), third-person objective (no inner thoughts).

5
[3]
Define denotation, connotation, and diction. Use the word "cheap" to illustrate the first two.
Answer Key

Denotation — the dictionary meaning. "Cheap" = low in price. Connotation — the emotional/associational meaning. "Cheap" connotes poor quality, low value, sometimes meanness, in a way a synonym like "affordable" does not. Diction — the writer's chosen vocabulary, ranging from formal to colloquial, abstract to concrete.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"Well, son, I'll tell you: / Life for me ain't been no crystal stair. / It's had tacks in it, / And splinters, / And boards torn up, / And places with no carpet on the floor — / Bare." — Langston Hughes, "Mother to Son"
Analyse how Hughes uses the metaphor of the stair and the free-verse form to develop a theme of persistence in struggle. Refer to specific words and to the shape of the lines on the page.
Answer Key

The "crystal stair" is the ideal Hughes denies — the speaker has not had it. By listing specifics (tacks, splinters, boards torn up, no carpet), Hughes extends the metaphor into a portrait of cumulative hardship. The short isolated line "Bare." visually mimics the brokenness it names. The dialect ("ain't been no") roots the voice in lived experience and rejects the formal English a sonnet would impose. Hughes's free verse is itself a thematic choice: the speaker's experience does not fit a regular form. Marks: 2 for naming the extended metaphor, 1 for analysing form (short lines, dialect), 1 for connecting form to theme, 1 for quotation accuracy.

7
[5]
"TRUE!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am! but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses, not destroyed, not dulled them." — Edgar Allan Poe, "The Tell-Tale Heart"
Identify two literary devices in this opening and explain how each helps Poe construct an unreliable narrator. Refer to specific words and punctuation.
Answer Key

Possible devices: (1) Repetition / parallelism ("very, very," "not destroyed, not dulled"); the obsessive repetition mimics agitated thinking. (2) Rhetorical question ("why will you say that I am mad?"); the narrator answers an accusation no one has made, inviting the reader to suspect madness. (3) Punctuation as device (all-caps TRUE!, em-dashes mid-clause); the interruptions enact mental disturbance on the page. (4) Paradox ("disease had sharpened my senses") — claiming illness as a gift. Any two well-explained devices earn full marks. Marks: 2 for naming devices, 2 for explaining each effect, 1 for textual precision.

8
[3]
Turn this topic into a thesis statement: "Setting in 'The Tell-Tale Heart.'"
Answer Key

Sample: "Poe restricts 'The Tell-Tale Heart' to a single airless room because the smallness of the setting mirrors the narrator's mental confinement — there is nowhere for the imagined heartbeat to escape, just as there is nowhere for the narrator to escape his own guilt." Look for: a specific text reference, a debatable claim, and an analytical verb. Marks: 1 specificity, 1 debatability, 1 analytical verb / structure.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write one analytical PEEL paragraph (Point–Evidence–Explain–Link) on the following claim: "In Hughes's 'Mother to Son,' the form (free verse, short lines, dialect) is itself part of the message." Embed at least one direct quotation. Aim for 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Look for: (P) clear topic sentence about how Hughes's form supports his theme; (E) one embedded quotation with signal phrase; (E) interpretation of how the choice of free verse and dialect functions (rejection of polished sonnet form, voicing of lived experience); (L) closing sentence linking back to thesis. Marks: 1 P, 1 E, 2 Explanation, 1 Link / conventions.

10
[4]
Rewrite this weak topic sentence into a strong analytical thesis: "'The Tell-Tale Heart' is about a man who kills someone."
Answer Key

Sample: "Poe's 'The Tell-Tale Heart' shows that guilt does not require external punishment — the mind that performs an evil act is the same mind that will hear its consequences forever afterward." Look for: a debatable claim (1), specific reference to text/effect (1), an interpretive verb (1), conventions (1).

11
[3]
Compare a metaphor and a symbol in a short paragraph (3–4 sentences). Define each, explain how they overlap, and use one literary example for each.
Answer Key

A metaphor is a single comparison stating that one thing is another ("Juliet is the sun"). A symbol is a sustained image that accumulates meaning across a whole text (the crystal stair in Hughes; the heart in Poe). All sustained metaphors can become symbols; not every metaphor does. Marks: 1 each definition, 1 the overlap / example.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
"Two roads diverged in a yellow wood, / And sorry I could not travel both / And be one traveler, long I stood / And looked down one as far as I could / To where it bent in the undergrowth." — Robert Frost, "The Road Not Taken"
Apply your close-reading skills: in 5–7 sentences, analyse the tone of this first stanza. Identify two specific words that establish the tone and explain how they do so. Then briefly note what the dominant tone tells us about how to read the rest of the poem.
Answer Key

The tone is regretful and uncertain, not triumphant. Words like "sorry" and "long I stood" suggest hesitation rather than decisive choice. The line "And looked down one as far as I could" emphasises an attempt to see consequence before choosing. This sets a tone of cautious looking-back rather than confident self-direction, preparing the reader to read the famous "made all the difference" later as wistful rather than triumphant. Marks: 2 tone identification, 2 word-level evidence, 1 interpretive consequence.

13
[4]
Apply the concept of unreliable narration. Choose a real or invented text (a TV show, film, novel, or short story) and explain in 4–6 sentences how the narrator's unreliability changes the meaning of the text for an attentive viewer/reader.
Answer Key

Look for: a specific text (1); identification of how we recognise the narrator's unreliability (1); explanation of how a re-read or careful first-read changes meaning (2). Acceptable texts include The Tell-Tale Heart, Fight Club, The Great Gatsby, The Sixth Sense, The Kite Runner, anything where the narrator's credibility is undercut.

14
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: Why does close-reading matter outside English class? Choose one real-world context (a news headline, an advertisement, a politician's speech, a social-media post) and explain in 3–4 sentences how close-reading skills (tone, diction, irony, connotation) help you evaluate it critically.
Answer Key

Strong responses identify how the skills transfer: tone detection spots sarcasm; diction analysis flags loaded language ("crackdown" vs "policy"); irony recognition catches performed vs sincere apologies; connotation reveals bias ("rebels" vs "freedom fighters"). Marks: 1 context, 1 skill transfer, 1 specific example.
