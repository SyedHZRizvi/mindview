Topic 3.1 — Elizabethan Context
Question 1
Shakespeare's company performed Romeo and Juliet primarily at:
Solution: Elizabethan public theatre was open-air and depended on daylight. The Globe (built 1599) was Shakespeare's company's most famous venue. There were no spotlights, very little scenery, and no curtain.
Question 2
In Shakespeare's plays, who typically speaks in prose rather than verse?
Solution: Verse (especially blank verse) is for noble or serious speech. Prose is reserved for servants, low comedy (like Sampson and Gregory in 1.1), and the disordered speech of madness. Shakespeare's register shifts are themselves character markers.
Topic 3.2 — The Prologue & Act 1
Question 3
The opening prologue of Romeo and Juliet is structured as:
Solution: Shakespeare opens the play with a 14-line sonnet — traditionally a love-poem form, here announcing a tragedy. The form itself begins the play's tension between love and death.
Question 4
"Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean." — Prologue, lines 1–4
The prologue's phrase "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" tells the audience that:
Solution: "Ancient grudge" tells us the feud is generations old; "break to new mutiny" tells us the violence is currently fresh. The prologue frames the feud as an inherited grievance still claiming new victims — which the play then dramatises.
Topic 3.3 — The Balcony Scene
Question 5
"O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" — Juliet, 2.2.33
In Shakespeare's English, "wherefore" means:
Solution: "Wherefore" = "why" (compare "therefore" = "for that"). Juliet is asking why Romeo has to be Romeo (a Montague), not where he is. This is one of the most frequently misread lines in English literature.
Question 6
"What's in a name? that which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet." — Juliet, 2.2.43–4
Juliet's argument here is that:
Solution: Juliet argues that names are arbitrary — Romeo's essence is not tied to "Montague." The play's tragedy will prove her wrong: the names carry real social consequences that her romantic argument cannot defeat.
Topic 3.4 — Act 3, the Turning Point
Question 7
In Act 3, Scene 1, Mercutio is killed by:
Solution: Tybalt kills Mercutio (under Romeo's arm, when Romeo tries to break up the fight). Romeo then kills Tybalt in grief and rage. Mercutio's dying line — "A plague o' both your houses!" — names the feud as the cause.
Question 8
Romeo's punishment for killing Tybalt is:
Solution: Prince Escalus banishes Romeo to Mantua, sparing his life. This partial mercy creates the geographical distance that makes the Friar's letter critical — and its failure to arrive catastrophic.
Topic 3.5 — The Tragic Ending
Question 9
The Friar's plan fails primarily because:
Solution: The plague quarantine prevents Friar John from delivering the letter explaining the plan. Romeo learns of Juliet's "death" from Balthasar instead. One bureaucratic / public-health failure kills both lovers.
Question 10
The play's closing rhymed couplet is spoken by:
Solution: Prince Escalus delivers the closing couplet: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309–10). His role bookends the play — he silenced the brawl in Act 1; he names the tragedy in Act 5.
