Topic 4.1 — Elements of Short Fiction
Question 1
The traditional five-stage plot arc is:
Solution: Freytag's pyramid: exposition sets up the situation, rising action builds tension through complications, the climax is the peak, falling action unwinds the consequences, and the resolution / denouement settles into a new state.
Question 2
A character whose traits sharply contrast with the protagonist's, highlighting both, is called a:
Solution: A foil is a character whose traits highlight another's by contrast (Mercutio and Romeo; Tybalt and Benvolio). An antagonist actively opposes; a foil reflects.
Topic 4.2 — Character & Theme
Question 3
Which of the following is a theme rather than a topic?
Solution: A theme is a defensible claim about a topic. Choice (b) names the author, the text, and an analytical claim that someone could disagree with. The others are topics (a, d) or plot summary (c).
Question 4
A character who changes meaningfully across the course of a story is described as:
Solution: Dynamic characters change over the story. Static characters do not. Flat / round describes complexity (single-trait vs multi-trait), not change.
Topic 4.3 — Sound & Form in Poetry
Question 5
"The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew." — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner"
The dominant sound device in this line is:
Solution: Coleridge uses internal rhyme (blew/flew matching within the same line) and the alliterative /f/ sounds (fair, foam, flew) to create a rhythmic, sweeping movement that mimics wind and sea.
Question 6
A line of poetry that runs past its line break without punctuation (so the sentence continues on the next line) is called:
Solution: Enjambment is the running of a sentence across a line break without punctuation. A caesura is a strong pause within a line; the volta is the turn in a sonnet; a refrain is a repeated line.
Topic 4.4 — The Sonnet
Question 7
In a Petrarchan sonnet, the volta most often occurs at:
Solution: In a Petrarchan sonnet, the volta (turn in argument) typically arrives at line 9, between octave (ABBAABBA) and sestet (CDECDE). In Shakespearean sonnets, the volta is more often at line 13, just before the closing couplet.
Question 8
"My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun; / Coral is far more red than her lips' red." — Shakespeare, Sonnet 130
In these opening lines, Shakespeare is doing what?
Solution: Sonnet 130 is an "anti-Petrarchan" sonnet. The first 12 lines mock the conventional tropes; the closing couplet ("And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare / As any she belied with false compare") flips the argument: he loves his mistress more truly because he does not lie about her.
Topic 4.5 — Comparative Reading
Question 9
In a comparative essay, the "basis for comparison" is:
Solution: A strong comparison rests on a meaningful basis — a theme, technique, or question both texts illuminate. "Both are by Canadian poets" is geography, not analysis; "Both poems use travel metaphors to think about choice" is a basis for comparison.
Question 10
In a comparative essay, the "synthesis" at the end should:
Solution: The synthesis is what the comparison earns you — a claim that depends on both texts being read together. If the closing claim could be made about either text alone, you have not yet synthesised.
