Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define and distinguish: (a) protagonist, (b) antagonist, (c) foil.
Answer Key

(a) Protagonist — central character; the story is about their experience or change. (b) Antagonist — the force or character opposing the protagonist (can be another person, society, nature, the protagonist's own self). (c) Foil — character whose traits contrast with another's, highlighting both by comparison (e.g., Tybalt and Benvolio, Mercutio and Romeo).

2
[3]
Identify and define each of the five metrical feet: iamb, trochee, spondee, dactyl, anapest. Show the stress pattern for each (use DUM for stressed, da for unstressed).
Answer Key

Iamb — da-DUM (unstressed-stressed): "be-LOW." Trochee — DUM-da (stressed-unstressed): "WAL-king." Spondee — DUM-DUM (stressed-stressed): "HEART-BREAK." Dactyl — DUM-da-da (stressed-unstressed-unstressed): "EL-e-phant." Anapest — da-da-DUM (unstressed-unstressed-stressed): "in the WOODS."

3
[3]
Distinguish: end rhyme, internal rhyme, slant rhyme, eye rhyme. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

End rhyme: matching sound at line ends (cat / hat). Internal rhyme: matching sound within a line ("The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew" — blew/flew). Slant rhyme: imperfect match (soul / all; Emily Dickinson uses these often). Eye rhyme: words that look like rhymes but do not sound the same (love / move; cough / bough).

4
[2]
The Shakespearean sonnet rhymes:
Answer Key

B. Three quatrains in alternating rhyme + closing couplet. The couplet typically delivers the volta.

5
[2]
Define volta and explain where it typically falls in (a) a Petrarchan sonnet and (b) a Shakespearean sonnet.
Answer Key

Volta = the "turn" — the shift in argument or emotional direction in a sonnet. (a) Petrarchan: line 9 (between octave and sestet). (b) Shakespearean: line 13 (before the closing couplet) — the couplet itself often delivers the turn.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[6]
"I knew that George was watching out the kitchen window. He had laughed when my mother came out and said, 'Wait till you become a girl. Then you'll know.' My father did not say anything." — adapted from Alice Munro, "Boys and Girls"
Analyse this short passage. Identify the point of view, characterise the narrator's relationship with each family member named, and explain what the father's silence contributes.
Answer Key

POV: first-person retrospective — the narrator is recounting a past event from adult perspective. Mother: explicit voice, voicing the gendered expectation ("Wait till you become a girl"). Brother George: silent observer who "laughed" — already absorbing the same gender script. Father's silence: pointed; in a family where the daughter has been "the father's helper," his refusal to defend her in this moment is a form of betrayal by omission. The silence is the passage's most important detail. Marks: 1 POV, 2 character relationships, 3 silence analysis.

7
[4]
"And miles to go before I sleep, / And miles to go before I sleep." — Robert Frost, "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening"
Analyse the function of the repetition in these final two lines. Why does Frost repeat the line rather than vary it?
Answer Key

The repetition deepens the line's resonance. The first iteration registers as literal (a journey to complete), the second invites metaphorical reading (life's remaining obligations, or the longer sleep of death). The repetition forces the reader to re-read the line at two different registers. The unchanged words become an emblem of the speaker's commitments — exactly the obligations he is being tempted to abandon by the "lovely, dark, and deep" woods. Marks: 1 identification of the device, 2 layered interpretation, 1 connection to the poem's larger argument.

8
[3]
Write a theme statement (one complete sentence, with author and text) for a short story you have studied — ideally one of "The Tell-Tale Heart" (Poe), "Eleven" (Cisneros), or "Boys and Girls" (Munro).
Answer Key

Examples: "Poe's 'The Tell-Tale Heart' argues that the human mind cannot suppress its own knowledge of what it has done — guilt does not require external punishment to torture its subject." OR "Cisneros's 'Eleven' suggests that growing up is not linear but cumulative — eleven contains every prior age, all crammed into one small body that adults refuse to believe." Look for: author, text, debatable claim, analytical verb. Marks: 1 specificity, 1 debatable claim, 1 form (complete sentence).

Major Task — Comparative Mini-Essay

Write a comparative mini-essay (250–350 words) comparing two of the following poems on a meaningful basis of comparison:
1. Robert Frost, "The Road Not Taken"
2. Langston Hughes, "Mother to Son"
3. Shakespeare, Sonnet 130

Your essay should include: introduction with thesis (specifying the basis for comparison), 1–2 body paragraphs (point-by-point structure), and a synthesis sentence at the end.
Marking Rubric

Introduction (2): clear basis for comparison + debatable thesis. Body (4): point-by-point structure, at least one embedded quotation from each text with citation, analytical interpretation. Synthesis (1): closing sentence that depends on both texts. Voice / conventions (1): literary present, third-person, MLA citation, accurate quotation.

10
[4]
Identify and correct each error in the following short passage:

"Hughes poem Mother to Son is about a women whose talking to her son. The poem uses free verse because it dont have a regular meter. The metaphor of the stair is symbolizing struggle and persistence."
Answer Key

(1) "Hughes's poem" (possessive). (2) "Mother to Son" — italicise the title. (3) "a woman who is talking" (singular noun, who-not-whose, contraction expanded). (4) "the poem uses free verse because it does not" (no contraction; or "doesn't" with apostrophe). (5) "the metaphor of the stair represents" (avoid the cumbersome "is symbolizing"). Marks: 0.5 per correctly identified and corrected error.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[5]
"I, too, sing America. / I am the darker brother. / They send me to eat in the kitchen / When company comes, / But I laugh, / And eat well, / And grow strong." — Langston Hughes, "I, Too"
Apply close-reading skills to this passage. Identify two devices Hughes uses and explain in 5–7 sentences how each contributes to the speaker's response to racial exclusion.
Answer Key

Possible devices: (1) Allusion — "I, too, sing America" alludes to Walt Whitman's "I Hear America Singing" (1860), claiming Whitman's celebratory tradition for Black speakers. (2) Contrast / juxtaposition — "send me to eat in the kitchen" / "I laugh, / And eat well, / And grow strong" places exclusion next to defiance. (3) Enjambment — short lines and run-ons make the speaker's tone declarative and patient. (4) Synecdoche — "the kitchen" stands for an entire social geography of segregation. Marks: 2 devices identified, 2 effect explained, 1 textual precision.

[4]
Apply short-story analysis to a film, TV episode, or video game you know well. In 4–6 sentences, identify the protagonist, an antagonist (force or character), and one specific moment of theme.
Answer Key

Look for: specific text (1), correctly identified protagonist (1), correctly identified antagonist (1), one specific moment with brief theme analysis (1). Acceptable choices: any narrative film, TV episode, or story-driven game.

STSE / Cross-strand: How does the close-reading skill you have built in this unit transfer to your reading of media texts (advertising, news, social media)? Use 3–4 sentences and one specific example.
Answer Key

Marks: 1 specificity, 1 transfer, 1 example.
