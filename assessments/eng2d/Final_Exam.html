Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[3]B · Devices
Define and give a one-line example of each: (a) verbal irony, (b) dramatic irony, (c) situational irony.
Answer Key

(a) Saying the opposite of what is meant ("Lovely weather!" in a storm). (b) Audience knows what character does not (audience knows Juliet is asleep; Romeo believes her dead). (c) Outcome opposite of expectation (a fire station burns down).

2
[3]B · Drama
Define soliloquy, aside, and blank verse. Give one example from Romeo and Juliet for one of the three.
Answer Key

Soliloquy — speech delivered alone on stage, voicing inner thought (e.g., Juliet alone, "O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" 2.2). Aside — brief remark heard by audience but not other characters. Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter; Shakespeare's standard verse for noble speech.

3
[2]B
The rhyme scheme of a Shakespearean sonnet is:
Answer Key

B. Three quatrains in alternating rhyme + a final couplet.

4
[3]C · Essay
Identify the four parts of a PEEL paragraph and the three qualities of a strong analytical thesis.
Answer Key

PEEL: Point (topic sentence), Evidence (quotation), Explain (analysis), Link (transition back to thesis). Strong thesis qualities: specific, debatable, supportable.

5
[3]C · Citation
Write the correct MLA-9 in-text citations for the following:
(a) A quotation from page 42 of To Kill a Mockingbird by Harper Lee.
(b) A quotation from Act 3, Scene 1, line 99 of Romeo and Juliet.
(c) A quotation from line 12 of a poem.
Answer Key

(a) (Lee 42). (b) (3.1.99). (c) (line 12), or once you have established you are using line numbers, (12).

6
[3]A · Oral
Define Aristotle's three rhetorical appeals: ethos, pathos, logos.
Answer Key

Ethos — appeal through credibility / character. Pathos — appeal to audience emotion. Logos — appeal to logic, reason, evidence.

7
[2]D · Media
"SIFT" (for evaluating online claims) stands for:
Answer Key

A. Mike Caulfield's SIFT method.

8
[3]D
Define and give one example of each advertising technique: bandwagon, testimonial, glittering generality.
Answer Key

Bandwagon: appeal to popularity ("Everyone uses it"). Testimonial: endorsement by celebrity/authority. Glittering generality: vague positive language without specifics ("Premium quality").

9
[3]B · Poetry
Identify and define three of the five metrical feet (iamb, trochee, spondee, dactyl, anapest). For each, show the stress pattern.
Answer Key

Any three of: Iamb da-DUM ("be-LOW"); Trochee DUM-da ("WAL-king"); Spondee DUM-DUM ("HEART-BREAK"); Dactyl DUM-da-da ("EL-e-phant"); Anapest da-da-DUM ("in the WOODS").

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
10
[8]B · Comparative Passage Analysis
Passage A: "Two roads diverged in a yellow wood, / And sorry I could not travel both / And be one traveler, long I stood / And looked down one as far as I could / To where it bent in the undergrowth." — Robert Frost, "The Road Not Taken"
Passage B: "Well, son, I'll tell you: / Life for me ain't been no crystal stair. / It's had tacks in it, / And splinters, / And boards torn up." — Langston Hughes, "Mother to Son"
Compare and contrast these two passages. Address: (i) the dominant metaphor or figure in each, (ii) the speaker's stance toward struggle/choice, (iii) the function of form (regular stanzas/rhyme vs free verse, dialect). Aim for 8–10 sentences.
Answer Key

Frost: extended metaphor (roads = life choices); speaker stance = retrospective, hesitant ("sorry," "looked as far as I could"); form = formal stanzas with rhyme, the regularity contrasting with inner uncertainty. Hughes: extended metaphor (stair = life of struggle); speaker stance = forward-facing, instructive — the mother teaches the son to keep climbing; form = free verse, dialect ("ain't been no"), short lines visually mimicking the broken stair. Common ground: both use a metaphor of travel/movement for life's challenges. Difference: Frost's speaker reflects with uncertainty; Hughes's mother instructs with insistence. Marks: 2 figures, 2 stance, 2 form, 2 synthesis.

11
[8]B · Romeo and Juliet Close Reading
"O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you. / She is the fairies' midwife, and she comes / In shape no bigger than an agate-stone / On the forefinger of an alderman, / Drawn with a team of little atomies / Athwart men's noses as they lie asleep." — Mercutio, 1.4.53–58
Analyse this passage. Identify two literary devices, characterise Mercutio's voice and worldview, and explain how the speech functions as a warning to Romeo just before they enter the Capulet feast.
Answer Key

Devices: (1) Personification / extended conceit — Queen Mab as a literal small being. (2) Hyperbolic miniature imagery — "no bigger than an agate-stone" makes the dream-bringer comically tiny. (3) Conceit — the speech extends the Mab idea across many lines, beginning playful and turning darker. Mercutio's voice: skeptical, inventive, wit-driven, prone to overflow; he distrusts dreams and romance. Function as warning: Romeo has just described a foreboding dream; Mercutio's speech, ostensibly mocking dreams as mere fancy, is in fact a caution against following emotional impulse — a caution Romeo immediately ignores by entering the feast and meeting Juliet. Marks: 2 devices, 2 voice/worldview, 2 function as warning, 2 communication.

12
[5]D · Media Analysis
A 30-second TV ad for a new sports drink: a young athlete sprints in slow motion across a sunlit field, sweat dripping, fast-paced electronic music; quick cuts to the bottle hitting his palm; tagline appears: "Beyond Limits."
Analyse this ad. Identify (a) two persuasion techniques, (b) the dominant appeal (ethos/pathos/logos), (c) one ethical concern, (d) what the ad omits.
Answer Key

(a) Persuasion techniques: pathos through slow-motion / sunlight / sweat (effort and beauty); transfer (linking the brand to athletic excellence and freedom); glittering generality ("Beyond Limits" — no specific claim). (b) Dominant appeal: pathos. (c) Ethics: the implied claim — that the drink contributes to athletic achievement — is not supported by evidence in the ad; sugar/sodium content is not mentioned. (d) Omits: ingredients, nutrition facts, the everyday context (no one watches their favourite athlete drink a sports drink while sitting on a couch). Marks: 2 techniques, 1 appeal, 1 ethics, 1 omissions.

13
[4]C · Argument
Construct a debatable thesis for the following question: "Is the tragedy of Romeo and Juliet caused primarily by the lovers' youth, by adult failure, or by fate?" Provide your thesis and two pieces of textual evidence to support it.
Answer Key

Sample thesis: "Shakespeare frames the lovers as 'star-crossed' but consistently shows that the tragedy is produced by adult failure — Capulet's commanded marriage to Paris, Friar Lawrence's haste despite his own counsel of 'wisely and slow,' and a postal failure caused by plague quarantine." Evidence: (1) Friar Lawrence's "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94) — ironic, since he himself runs fast. (2) The plague quarantine that stops Friar John's letter (5.2.6). Marks: 2 thesis, 2 evidence + brief analysis.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
14
[15]B + C · Major Literary-Analysis Essay
Major Task — Write a literary-analysis essay (400–550 words) responding to ONE of the following prompts:

Option 1: "How does Shakespeare use the sonnet form (in the prologue and the lovers' first meeting at 1.5.92–105) to set up romantic expectations that the play will then frustrate?"
Option 2: "How does Poe use punctuation, syntax, and repetition in the opening of 'The Tell-Tale Heart' to establish an unreliable narrator?"
Option 3: "How does Hughes use the extended metaphor of the stair and the choice of free verse in 'Mother to Son' to argue for persistence over polish?"

Include: introduction with thesis, 3 body paragraphs (each PEEL), conclusion. Embed at least 3 quotations with proper citation. Use literary present tense and third-person voice.
Marking Rubric

K/U (3): correct understanding of text and devices. Thinking (4): quality of argument; specific evidence selection; counter-reading awareness. Communication (5): clear introduction with thesis, well-organised PEEL paragraphs, embedded quotations with citation, conclusion that synthesises rather than merely restates, voice/conventions (literary present, third-person, MLA). Application (3): transfer of literary analysis terms; relevance of evidence to claim.

15
[5]C · Grammar in Context
Identify and correct the error in each sentence:
(a) "Each of the students were prepared for the exam."
(b) "The poem which I read last semester it was very moving."
(c) "Romeo, who is the protagonist of Shakespeare's play, are conflicted about avenging his cousin's death."
(d) "Hughes's poem is more better than I expected."
(e) "The students who's essays were submitted on time received feedback."
Answer Key

(a) "Each … was prepared." (Each = singular.) (b) "The poem that I read last semester was very moving." (Remove redundant "it"; "that" preferred for restrictive clauses.) (c) "Romeo … is conflicted." (Singular subject.) (d) "better" — drop "more" (no double comparative). (e) "whose essays" (possessive — "who's" = "who is").

16
[5]A · Oral / Written Communication
Write the opening 4–6 sentences of a 3-minute oral presentation arguing for the inclusion of one specific Canadian short story or poem on the ENG2D syllabus. Include: a hook, a clear claim, and a plan of evidence.
Answer Key

Look for: a specific text and author; a clear claim about why the work should be added (literary merit + curriculum value); a plan of evidence (form, theme, voice, relevance to Grade 10 readers). Marks: 1 hook, 2 claim specificity, 1 plan, 1 conventions.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
17
[8]D · Media-Text Construction
Application Task — Storyboard a 60-second PSA on the topic "Social-media literacy for incoming Grade 9 students."

Provide:
(a) target-audience analysis (2 sentences),
(b) 4-shot storyboard (each shot specifies: framing, action, on-screen text, sound),
(c) one ethical consideration in your construction,
(d) one reflection on which Unit 5 concept(s) shaped your design.
Marking Rubric

(a) Audience analysis (2): age-appropriate, prior knowledge, platform considered. (b) 4 shots × 1 = 4 (each with framing, action, text, sound). (c) Ethics (1): no fear-mongering, no graphic imagery, no overclaiming. (d) Reflection (1): connects to Unit 5 concept (codes/conventions, audience analysis, appeals).

18
[5]B + D · Cross-Strand Synthesis
Synthesis: In 5–7 sentences, identify one substantive connection between literary analysis (close-reading a text) and media analysis (close-reading a media text). Use one specific example from each side and explain why the same critical skill operates in both.
Marking Rubric

Strong responses identify a specific connection: framing operates in both (what the narrator foregrounds in Munro / what the camera frames in a TikTok); ironic tone works the same in advertising and in literary verbal irony; ethos/pathos/logos appear in both Shakespearean rhetoric and modern ad campaigns. Marks: 2 connection, 2 examples (one each), 1 communication.

19
[5]C · Reflection
Reflection: In 5–7 sentences, reflect on which Unit (1–5) most expanded your reading and writing skills. Identify one specific challenge you faced and one specific skill that transferred to another part of the course or your life outside English.
Marking Rubric

Look for: specific identification of a unit and skill; honest acknowledgement of a challenge; transfer to a specific outside-English context (university work, social-media use, civic engagement, professional communication). Marks: 1 specificity of skill, 2 challenge depth, 1 transfer, 1 communication.

20
[7]A · Course-Wide Application
Final Synthesis: In 7–9 sentences, articulate the most important thing you learned in ENG2D about how language and media construct meaning. Draw on at least two of the four strands (Oral, Reading, Writing, Media) and use one specific example from a text or media we studied. Identify one way this insight will travel beyond English class.
Marking Rubric

K/U (2): accurate use of two strands' concepts. Thinking (2): substantive insight, not generic. Communication (2): organisation, register, conventions. Application (1): explicit transfer to a non-English context. Strong responses tie language-construction insights — the gap between speaker and word in Shakespeare, the framing choices of news writers, Hughes's free-verse argument, Cisneros's compressed setting — to a coherent claim about what literary and media skills do in the world.

📝 Communication Category Rubric (instructor-graded)

Per Ontario's Growing Success (2010) Achievement Chart, Communication is one of the four equally-weighted categories (25% of the course mark). This rubric is applied to every student-written response (short-answer, extended-response, essay, lab report, deputation, etc.) on this assessment.

Criterion Level 1 — Limited (50–59%) Level 2 — Some (60–69%) Level 3 — Considerable (70–79%) Level 4 — Thorough (80–100%)
Expression and organisation of ideas Ideas expressed with limited clarity; little organisation. Ideas expressed with some clarity; some organisation. Ideas expressed clearly and organised effectively. Ideas expressed with clarity, precision, and a high degree of organisation.
Communication for different audiences and purposes Limited awareness of audience/purpose; tone often inappropriate. Some awareness of audience/purpose; tone occasionally inappropriate.
