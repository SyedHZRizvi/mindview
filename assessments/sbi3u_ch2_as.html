Topic 2.1–2.2 — Natural Selection & Its Modes
Question 1
Which is NOT one of the four conditions required for natural selection to occur?
Solution: Selection requires variation, heritability, differential reproduction, and overproduction. Human observation has nothing to do with whether selection occurs — selection has been operating on Earth for billions of years.
Question 2
In a population of moths, dark-coloured moths are favoured because they are less visible on soot-darkened tree trunks. The mean colour darkens over generations. This is an example of:
Solution: Directional selection favours one extreme of the distribution and shifts the mean — classic example: industrial melanism in the peppered moth, Biston betularia.
Question 3
A peacock's enormous tail is costly to survival. Why does it persist?
Solution: Sexual selection by female choice (intersexual selection) favours males with elaborate ornaments. Females select for "honest signals" of genetic quality. Mating success is a component of fitness even when survival decreases.
Topic 2.3–2.4 — Evidence & Sources of Variation
Question 4
The forelimb bones of a human, a whale, and a bat are very similar in structure. This similarity is best described as:
Solution: Same skeletal plan, different functions — homologous. The shared structure comes from a common tetrapod ancestor.
Question 5
Which is the ULTIMATE source of new genetic variation in a population?
Solution: Only mutation creates new alleles. Meiosis (independent assortment, crossing over) and random fertilization shuffle existing alleles into new combinations.
Question 6
Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes. Ignoring crossing over, how many genetically distinct gametes can independent assortment alone produce?
Solution: Each pair contributes 2 possible gametes (maternal or paternal homolog). With 23 pairs: \(2^{23} = 8\,388\,608\) gametes. Crossing over multiplies this enormously.
Topic 2.5–2.6 — Speciation & Hardy–Weinberg
Question 7
A mountain range rises, dividing a single species of squirrel into two isolated populations that eventually become unable to interbreed. This is:
Solution: Geographic barrier → divergence in isolation → reproductive isolation: classic allopatric speciation.
Question 8
In a population, 16% of individuals are homozygous recessive (aa) for a trait. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg, what is the allele frequency of the recessive allele (q)?
Solution: \(q^2 = 0.16\), so \(q = \sqrt{0.16} = \mathbf{0.4}\).
Question 9
Using the value of q from question 8 (q = 0.4, p = 0.6), what fraction of the population is heterozygous (Aa)? Give as a decimal to 2 dp.
Solution: \(2pq = 2(0.6)(0.4) = \mathbf{0.48}\) or 48% of the population.
Question 10
Which is a postzygotic reproductive barrier?
Solution: Postzygotic = after a zygote forms. Hybrid sterility (mule = horse × donkey) is the classic example. A, B, and D are prezygotic (temporal, gametic, habitat isolation).
Question 11
Polyploidy is a major route to sympatric speciation in:
Solution: Polyploid speciation is common in plants (modern wheat is hexaploid, strawberries are octoploid, many crops are polyploid hybrids). Most animals do not tolerate polyploidy.
Question 12
Which is NOT an assumption of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium?
Solution: Hardy–Weinberg requires large population size (to avoid genetic drift). The five assumptions are large population, no migration, no mutation, random mating, no selection.
