Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
State the Brundtland definition of sustainable development and name the three pillars of the "triple bottom line."
Answer Key

Brundtland (1987): "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." Three pillars: environmental, economic, and social. Sometimes a fourth (cultural/Indigenous) is added.

2
[2]
The Atlantic cod moratorium of 1992 is significant because it:
Answer Key

B. 35,000 fishery workers in Newfoundland and Labrador lost their jobs overnight. The food-web disruption was so severe that the ecosystem flipped to a self-reinforcing capelin-and-shellfish state; cod has only partly recovered in three decades.

3
[3]
Define each Indigenous-stewardship concept in one sentence: Seven Generations, Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk), Indigenous Protected and Conserved Area (IPCA).
Answer Key

Seven Generations: a Haudenosaunee/Anishinaabe principle that decisions should consider effects on the seventh generation to come. Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk): a Mi'kmaw concept for integrating Indigenous knowledge and Western science. IPCA: an area where Indigenous nations exercise the lead role in protecting and conserving ecosystems using Indigenous laws and knowledge.

4
[2]
Earth's biocapacity per person is currently about 1.6 gha, but global average footprint is about 2.7 gha. This means humanity is using:
Answer Key

B. 2.7/1.6 ≈ 1.7 Earths. Humanity is in ecological overshoot, using natural systems faster than they regenerate.

5
[3]
Name two energy storage technologies and explain how each helps integrate intermittent renewables.
Answer Key

(1) Lithium-ion battery: stores electrical energy chemically; can be deployed at grid (≥100 MW) or building scale; rapid response. (2) Pumped hydroelectric storage: pumps water uphill when electricity is abundant, releases through turbines when needed; longest-established grid-scale storage. (3) Compressed air or thermal storage. (4) Green hydrogen: electrolysis stores energy as H₂ for later combustion or fuel cell use. Each balances generation-demand mismatch in time, allowing variable renewables to provide reliable power.

6
[2]
Of nine planetary boundaries identified by the Stockholm Resilience Centre, how many had been crossed as of 2023?
Answer Key

C. Six: climate change, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows (N and P), land-system change, freshwater change, and novel entities. Stratospheric ozone is recovering thanks to the Montreal Protocol (1987).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
A simplified cod population has intrinsic growth rate r = 0.2/yr and carrying capacity K = 2 × 10⁶ tonnes. Calculate Maximum Sustainable Yield using MSY = rK/4. How does this compare with peak catches of 800,000 tonnes/yr in the late 1960s?
Answer Key

MSY = 0.2 × 2 × 10⁶ / 4 = 100,000 tonnes/yr. Peak 1960s catch of 800,000 t/yr was 8× MSY. After the 1992 moratorium, biologists revised K downward by 60% or more due to ecosystem regime shift, lowering revised MSY to ~40,000 t/yr or less. Strong answers note that the formula assumes a stable single-species model; real fisheries require precautionary management below calculated MSY.

8
[4]
A Toronto household considers six actions. Estimate the total tCO₂e reduction: (a) switch beef to chicken half-time, (b) replace gas furnace with heat pump, (c) replace one ICE car with EV, (d) skip one transatlantic round-trip flight, (e) install 10 kW rooftop solar, (f) reduce shopping by 20%. Use textbook values.
Answer Key

(a) ~0.8 t; (b) ~2.0 t; (c) ~2.5 t in Ontario; (d) ~1.5 t; (e) ~1.2 t in Ontario; (f) ~0.5 t. Total ~8.5 t/yr. Starting from ~22 t/yr per Canadian household, this is roughly a 39% reduction. A strong answer notes the per-Canadian Paris target of ~2.5 t requires additional system-level change (grid decarbonization, building stock electrification, transit infrastructure) beyond individual action alone.

9
[4]
A Toronto home consumes 9,000 kWh/year. Toronto receives 1,200 kWh/m²/yr solar irradiance; 20%-efficient panels produce 240 kWh/m²/yr. (a) Required panel area? (b) At $2.50/W installed and 8 W per m² (about 1.4 kW per 1.7 m² panel), capital cost? (c) At $0.15/kWh electricity rate, simple payback?
Answer Key

(a) Area = 9,000 / 240 = 37.5 m². (b) Approximate system rating: 37.5 m² × 8 W/m² = 300 W (this conversion ignores some details; using the common 200 W/m² rating gives 7.5 kW; the textbook uses 8.8 kW for a 22-panel array). At $2.50/W × 8,800 W = $22,000. (c) Annual savings ≈ 9,000 kWh × $0.15 = $1,350. Payback = $22,000 / $1,350 ≈ 16 years. Strong answers note that the Greener Homes loan and grid net metering improve economics; lifetime production is 25+ years.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Use Kate Raworth's "doughnut" model to map Canada onto the diagram. Identify two boundaries Canada exceeds and two social-floor measures Canada generally meets.
Answer Key

Boundaries exceeded: climate change (per-capita emissions ~14 t/yr vs safe ~1.6 t), biodiversity (multiple ecozone declines), nitrogen flow (agricultural fertilizer runoff), land-system change (boreal fragmentation). Social-floor measures met: food, health, education, energy access. Measures often unmet: housing affordability (worsening), Indigenous-equity measures, political voice (Indigenous self-determination). Diagram (or description) should show Canada outside the green doughnut on ecological side, mostly inside on social side. Strong answers note that the policy goal is staying inside both rings.

11
[4]
Explain in 3–5 sentences why Indigenous-led conservation often produces biodiversity outcomes comparable to or better than federally-managed national parks. Use the terms "place-based knowledge," "community legitimacy," and "holistic management."
Answer Key

Indigenous communities draw on millennia of place-based ecological knowledge — detailed empirical observations of species, ecosystems, and processes — that often surpass what visiting professionals know. Community legitimacy means stewards live within and depend on the land, producing strong local enforcement and accountability. Holistic management integrates ecological, cultural, and social goals rather than separating "nature" from "human use." Published research (Schuster et al., 2019) confirms biodiversity outcomes match or exceed those of state-managed protected areas. The Canadian 30 by 30 commitment depends substantially on Indigenous-led conservation.

12
[4]
Define each: levelized cost of electricity, electrification, circular economy, Extended Producer Responsibility.
Answer Key

Levelized cost of electricity (LCOE): the average cost of generated electricity over a project's lifetime, including capital, fuel, O&M, and decommissioning. Electrification: replacing fossil-fuel combustion with electric devices (heat pumps, EVs, electric furnaces) powered by a low-carbon grid. Circular economy: designed-for-reuse, regenerative material model that eliminates the concept of waste by keeping materials in continuous loops. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR): policy framework requiring manufacturers to bear the cost of end-of-life management for their products; Ontario's Blue Box program transitioned to full EPR in 2023.

Part D: Application [11 marks]
13
[5]
An Ontario rural municipality is considering a 50 MW solar farm vs a 50 MW natural-gas peaker plant. Evaluate the trade-offs (capital cost, emissions, land use, local jobs, intermittency) and recommend a decision with justification.
Answer Key

Capital cost: solar ~$2.50/W installed = $125 M; gas peaker ~$0.7/W = $35 M. Emissions: solar near-zero operational; gas peaker ~500 g CO₂/kWh × ~50 GWh/yr (if 12% capacity factor) ≈ 25,000 t/yr. Land use: solar ~250 ha needed; gas plant ~5 ha (but supply chain larger). Jobs: solar farm has more construction jobs but fewer permanent; gas plant has fewer construction but a small permanent crew. Intermittency: solar produces only when sun shines (~17–22% capacity factor in Ontario); gas peaker is dispatchable. Decision usually favours solar paired with grid-scale battery storage for daily flexibility, accepting that some firming capacity may be needed for multi-day cloud events. Strong answers reference Ontario's clean-electricity grid context and 2035 net-zero target.

14
[3]
A First Nation in northern Ontario is considering whether to host a proposed lithium mine on their treaty territory. Use the free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) principle and Impact and Benefit Agreement (IBA) framework to outline what conditions would need to be met.
Answer Key

FPIC requirements: (1) Free — no coercion or pressure on the nation. (2) Prior — consent obtained before any commitment. (3) Informed — full disclosure of environmental, health, social, and economic impacts. (4) Consent — affirmative, ongoing community approval; revocable. IBA conditions: revenue-sharing percentages; preferential local employment quotas; environmental-monitoring programs co-led by the nation; clean-up bonds posted in advance; cultural-protection clauses; right to withdraw consent if conditions deteriorate. Strong answers note that FPIC is a UNDRIP principle and that Canada committed to UNDRIP implementation in 2021, though application is still being clarified.

15
[3]
A clothing retailer is launching a "Circular Fashion" line to reduce environmental impact. List three specific design or business-model strategies that align with the R-hierarchy.
Answer Key

(1) Reduce: design for durability (multi-season construction; classic styles instead of fast-fashion). (2) Reuse/repair: offer free repair service; take-back program for resale. (3) Recycle: use mono-material fabrics (single-fibre garments are easier to recycle than blends). (4) Refuse: phase out problematic dyes and synthetic micro-fibre fabrics. (5) Rent: pilot a clothing-rental subscription model. (6) Closed-loop manufacturing: fibre-to-fibre recycling. Strong answers note that the most impactful R-strategies (refuse, reduce, reuse) sit higher in the hierarchy than recycling and require business-model change, not just supply-chain tweaks.
