Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
List the eight major terrestrial biomes and identify the dominant vegetation of each in one phrase.
Answer Key

(1) Tropical rainforest — multi-layer broadleaf evergreen canopy. (2) Tropical savanna — grasses with scattered trees. (3) Desert — drought-adapted (cacti, succulents, sparse vegetation). (4) Mediterranean/chaparral — drought-tolerant evergreen shrubs. (5) Temperate grassland — perennial grasses, no trees. (6) Temperate deciduous forest — broadleaf deciduous trees (oak, maple). (7) Boreal forest/taiga — cold-tolerant conifers (spruce, fir, pine). (8) Tundra — mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs.

2
[2]
The 10% rule of ecology states that:
Answer Key

B. Lindeman (1942): on average about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is transferred to the next. The rest is lost as metabolic heat, used for movement, or remains undigested. This is why ecosystems can typically sustain only 4–5 trophic levels.

3
[3]
Match each cycle to its dominant reservoir: water cycle, carbon cycle, nitrogen cycle, phosphorus cycle.
Answer Key

Water cycle — oceans (97.5% of total water). Carbon cycle — sedimentary rocks (limestone) by mass, oceans by short-term-active mass. Nitrogen cycle — atmosphere (78% N₂). Phosphorus cycle — sedimentary rock and ocean sediment; no significant atmospheric phase, distinguishing it from the others.

4
[2]
Define primary succession and give one example.
Answer Key

Primary succession is ecological change on a substrate lacking soil (bare rock, fresh lava, post-glacial terrain). It proceeds slowly because soil must form first. Examples: post-glacial Glacier Bay, Alaska; fresh lava on Hawaii's Kilauea; sand dunes on Lake Michigan.

5
[3]
Name three of Canada's 15 terrestrial ecozones and one distinctive feature of each.
Answer Key

Examples: Mixedwood Plains — fertile post-glacial soils, deciduous-temperate forest, half of Canada's population. Boreal Shield — Canadian Shield bedrock, mixed conifer/deciduous boreal forest, vast lakes. Prairies — temperate grassland on Chernozemic soils, Canada's wheat belt. Arctic Cordillera — Baffin Island highlands; polar desert. Pacific Maritime — temperate rainforest, Sitka spruce, western redcedar. Three of these or similar correct examples earn full credit.

6
[2]
Which biome stores the largest amount of terrestrial carbon (including soil)?
Answer Key

B. Boreal forests store more terrestrial carbon than tropical rainforests in total, because much of it is in soil organic matter and permafrost peatlands. Per hectare, tropical rainforests can store more above-ground biomass, but the global boreal totals exceed tropical totals.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
A forest plot contains 30 sugar maples, 14 white pines, 4 yellow birches, 2 American beeches. Calculate Simpson's diversity index D = 1 − Σ(pᵢ)². Show your work.
Answer Key

Total = 50. p = 30/50 = 0.6; 14/50 = 0.28; 4/50 = 0.08; 2/50 = 0.04. Σ(p²) = 0.36 + 0.0784 + 0.0064 + 0.0016 = 0.4464. D = 1 − 0.4464 = 0.5536 ≈ 0.55. Moderately diverse: dominated by sugar maple but with three other species present. A monoculture would give D = 0; an even split among 4 species would give D = 0.75.

8
[4]
A wolf weighs about 40 kg and needs roughly 10,000 kJ/day for maintenance. Use the 10% rule to estimate how many hectares of meadow producing 10,000 kJ/m²/yr at the producer level are needed to support one wolf at the fourth trophic level. Show your work.
Answer Key

Energy reaching T4: 10,000 × 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 10 kJ/m²/yr. Annual wolf need = 10,000 × 365 = 3.65 × 10⁶ kJ/yr. Area needed = 3.65 × 10⁶ / 10 = 3.65 × 10⁵ m² = 36.5 hectares per wolf. A pack of six needs ≥ 220 ha; with hunting losses, real territories often exceed 100 km². Strong answers note this is why apex predators are rare.

9
[4]
A boreal forest plot was last burned in 2010. Predict the dominant vegetation (a) in 2020, (b) in 2050, (c) in 2150, and briefly justify each prediction using succession concepts.
Answer Key

(a) 2020: pioneer herbaceous species — fireweed, blueberry, bunchberry — dominate the open burned ground. (b) 2050: trembling aspen and paper birch suckers form an 8–12 m deciduous canopy that pioneers post-fire; some young white spruce and jack pine appear underneath. (c) 2150: a mixed black-spruce/jack-pine/white-spruce canopy with subdominant balsam fir; near-climax composition for the boreal forest. Concept points: secondary succession; facilitation by pioneers building soil; eventual climax composition determined by climate and disturbance regime.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Using a labeled diagram (description acceptable), describe a simple terrestrial food web with at least 6 species and arrows showing energy flow. Include at least one producer, one herbivore, one omnivore, one carnivore, and one decomposer.
Answer Key

Example: Sugar maple (P) → white-tailed deer (H) → eastern coyote (C). Sugar maple → eastern grey squirrel (H) → red-tailed hawk (C). Black bear (O) eats both squirrels and acorns. Decomposers (fungi, soil bacteria) break down dead organic matter from all levels, returning nutrients to the soil. Arrows should point from prey to predator (energy flow). Strong answer shows web (not just chain) and includes decomposition loop.

11
[4]
Explain in three to five sentences what makes the boreal forest particularly important to global climate. Use the terms carbon storage, permafrost, fire regime.
Answer Key

The boreal forest stores roughly 30% of all terrestrial carbon — more than the tropical rainforest by total amount — primarily in soil organic matter and permafrost peatlands. Permafrost thaw with rising temperatures could release vast amounts of stored carbon as CO₂ and methane, accelerating climate change (positive feedback). The natural fire regime (50–150-year return) is being amplified by warming and drying, increasing emissions and forest loss. Whether the boreal remains a net carbon sink or flips to a source is one of the great uncertainties of 21st-century climate.

12
[4]
Define and briefly distinguish: species richness, species evenness, endemic species, keystone species, invasive species.
Answer Key

Species richness: number of species in an area. Species evenness: how evenly individuals are distributed among species. Endemic: found only in one geographic region (e.g., Vancouver Island marmot in BC). Keystone: disproportionate ecosystem effect relative to abundance (e.g., wolves, sea otters). Invasive: non-native species that spreads aggressively and disrupts ecosystems (e.g., emerald ash borer in Ontario).

Part D: Application [11 marks]
13
[5]
The Carolinian forest of southwestern Ontario is one of Canada's most threatened ecosystems. Identify two ecological reasons it is biodiversity-rich and two specific threats it currently faces. Propose one conservation strategy.
Answer Key

Reasons for biodiversity: (1) Southernmost extent of many species; mild climate supports plants and animals not found elsewhere in Canada (e.g., tulip tree, prothonotary warbler). (2) Mixedwood Plains soils are fertile and varied. Threats: (1) Habitat fragmentation by agriculture and urban sprawl (only ~10% original cover remains). (2) Invasive species (garlic mustard, emerald ash borer, black swallow-wort). Strategy: connecting fragments through protected ecological corridors (e.g., Carolinian Canada Coalition, Greenbelt expansion). Strong answers identify spatial as well as species-level interventions.

14
[3]
An invasive zebra mussel population is introduced to a Canadian lake. Predict its impacts on water clarity, native species, and recreational use. Why are zebra mussels especially difficult to eradicate?
Answer Key

Impacts: water clarity increases as mussels filter phytoplankton (typically by 60% or more); native mussel species decline; algal blooms shift to bottom-dwelling forms; sport-fish populations may decline as the food web is disrupted; recreational impacts include cut feet, fouled water intakes, beach disruption. Eradication is difficult because each female produces 30,000–1 million eggs; veliger larvae spread on boats, equipment, and waterfowl; established beds are inaccessible to chemical treatment; once a watershed is colonised it is essentially impossible to remove.

15
[3]
Indigenous-led conservation areas (IPCAs) like Thaidene Nene have grown rapidly in Canada. Why might Indigenous-managed lands outperform federally-managed parks on biodiversity outcomes?
Answer Key

(1) Indigenous stewardship draws on millennia of place-based ecological knowledge. (2) Community legitimacy makes enforcement easier (community-based stewards vs distant federal staff). (3) Holistic management — integrating ecological, cultural, and social objectives rather than separating "nature" from "human use." (4) Local accountability — leadership lives within and depends on the land. Published research (Schuster et al., 2019) confirms Indigenous-managed lands match or exceed federal protected-area biodiversity outcomes in Canada, Australia, and Brazil.
