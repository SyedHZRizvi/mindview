Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify three independent lines of evidence for anthropogenic climate change.
Answer Key

Examples: (1) Instrumental temperature record (~1.2 °C warming since 1850). (2) Continuous Keeling Curve CO₂ measurements at Mauna Loa from 1958. (3) Sea-level rise (3.4 mm/yr satellite era). (4) Arctic September sea-ice decline (~13%/decade). (5) Glacier retreat worldwide. (6) Paleoclimate proxies (tree rings, ice cores, ocean sediments, corals). Any three with brief explanation earn full credit.

2
[2]
The Paris Agreement's central temperature goal is:
Answer Key

B. Paris (COP21, 2015) commits signatories to "holding the increase in global average temperature to well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit … 1.5 °C."

3
[3]
Distinguish physical and economic water scarcity. Give one regional example of each.
Answer Key

Physical scarcity: renewable supply is genuinely insufficient relative to demand. Examples: Middle East and North Africa, U.S. Southwest, the Sahel. Economic scarcity: water exists physically but human infrastructure cannot deliver it. Examples: much of sub-Saharan Africa. Each requires different policy responses: physical scarcity needs demand reduction and possibly desalination; economic scarcity needs infrastructure investment.

4
[2]
Tropical forests store an estimated:
Answer Key

B. Tropical forests hold roughly 250–300 Gt of biomass carbon — about a quarter of terrestrial carbon — and continue to absorb ~1.3 Gt CO₂ annually. Boreal forests hold even more total terrestrial carbon when soil and permafrost are counted.

5
[3]
Define each: NDVI, Landsat, lidar.
Answer Key

NDVI (Normalised Difference Vegetation Index): (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red); standard chlorophyll proxy useful for vegetation health and deforestation monitoring. Landsat: NASA/USGS Earth-observation satellite program continuous since 1972; currently Landsat 8/9 with 30-m multispectral resolution; free data. Lidar (Light Detection and Ranging): laser-based ranging that produces sub-metre-resolution elevation models of terrain and forest canopy structure.

6
[2]
Free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) is a principle of:
Answer Key

B. UNDRIP (2007) establishes Indigenous rights including FPIC for projects affecting Indigenous lands. Canada committed to UNDRIP implementation in 2021 with the UNDRIP Act, which requires federal law to align with the Declaration.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
The remaining carbon budget for a 50% chance of staying below 1.5 °C is about 400 Gt CO₂ from January 2020. At current emission rates of 40 Gt/yr, calculate when the budget is exhausted assuming steady emissions. What annual percentage reduction would keep us within budget by 2050 (net-zero target)?
Answer Key

Years remaining at steady emissions: 400 / 40 = 10 years from 2020 = budget exhausted by 2030. To reach net-zero by 2050 (30 years), with cumulative budget of 400 Gt, average annual emissions must average 400/30 ≈ 13 Gt/yr. Starting from 40 Gt/yr, this means roughly 9% per year compound reduction. Strong answer notes any year of delay tightens the required trajectory; this is the basis of IPCC's repeated warning that "every year matters."

8
[4]
A satellite pixel of an Amazon site has NIR = 0.42, Red = 0.06 (intact forest); a year later, NIR = 0.18, Red = 0.20 (cleared and ploughed). Calculate NDVI for each and interpret the change.
Answer Key

Pixel 1 (intact): NDVI = (0.42 − 0.06) / (0.42 + 0.06) = 0.36 / 0.48 = 0.75. Healthy forest. Pixel 2 (cleared): NDVI = (0.18 − 0.20) / (0.18 + 0.20) = −0.02 / 0.38 = −0.053 ≈ −0.05. Essentially bare soil. The dramatic drop from +0.75 to ≈ 0 indicates a clear-cut between observations. This is exactly the kind of pixel-level analysis Global Forest Watch uses to detect deforestation in near-real-time.

9
[4]
Design a geographic inquiry: "Which Toronto neighbourhoods are most exposed to summer extreme heat, and how does the pattern relate to tree-canopy cover?" Outline the five steps of the inquiry process and identify one data source for each.
Answer Key

(1) Formulate question: spatially explicit, tractable — given. (2) Gather information: Landsat thermal-IR data for surface temperature; City of Toronto Open Data Portal for tree-canopy GIS layer; Statistics Canada census for income and demographic data. (3) Interpret/analyse: load in QGIS; calculate mean surface temperature per neighbourhood; calculate canopy cover; statistical correlation. (4) Evaluate: produce thematic maps and bivariate plot; test correlation; compare with peer-reviewed Toronto UHI studies. (5) Communicate: 6-page report with maps, infographic for public audience. Strong answers also include limitations (single-day satellite vs multi-day heat events; missing demographic controls).

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Write a clear 4–6 sentence explanation of why the 2021 British Columbia heat dome was a climate-attribution event, suitable for a general newspaper audience.
Answer Key

The 2021 BC heat dome was the deadliest weather disaster in Canadian history, killing 619 people in less than a week. The heat dome itself — a stagnant high-pressure system trapping hot air — could in principle happen without climate change. But attribution scientists at the World Weather Attribution group compared the observed event with climate-model simulations both with and without anthropogenic greenhouse gases. They concluded the heat dome was at least 150 times more likely to occur because of human-caused warming than it would have been in a pre-industrial climate. The geographic lesson: climate change does not always create new extremes; it sharply increases the probability and intensity of extremes that the physical system was already capable of.

11
[4]
Explain the difference between mitigation and adaptation in climate policy. Give one Canadian example of each.
Answer Key

Mitigation reduces the cause (emissions): replacing coal with wind, building heat pumps, electrifying transport, restoring forests. Adaptation reduces the consequences for human and natural systems: floodplain mapping updates, urban tree canopy programs, building cooling centres for heatwaves, irrigation planning. Canadian examples: federal carbon pricing (mitigation, Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act 2018); Toronto Climate Action Plan trees and cooling-centres expansion (adaptation). Both are essential; mitigation is fundamentally limited (already-locked-in warming requires adaptation), and adaptation alone is overwhelmed if mitigation fails.

12
[4]
Define each: vector data, raster data, virtual water, climate attribution, just transition.
Answer Key

Vector data: GIS representation of discrete features as points, lines, or polygons with attached attributes. Raster data: GIS representation of continuous surfaces as regular pixel grids (e.g., satellite images). Virtual water: water embedded in goods through production, distinct from direct household use. Climate attribution: statistical method quantifying how much anthropogenic warming has changed the probability or intensity of a specific weather event. Just transition: policy framework ensuring workers and communities are not abandoned during the shift away from carbon-intensive industries.

Part D: Application [11 marks]
13
[5]
A Canadian municipality is considering buying a 100-hectare wetland for permanent protection. As a geographer, list five ecosystem services the wetland provides and propose how each could be valued in dollars (qualitatively).
Answer Key

(1) Water filtration: replaces water-treatment plant cost (≈ $thousands/year saved). (2) Flood storage: reduces downstream flood damage (insurance and property-loss savings). (3) Carbon storage in peat soils and biomass (≈ tonnes CO₂ × carbon price). (4) Biodiversity habitat: harder to monetise; sometimes via tourism, recreation, biodiversity offsets. (5) Recreation, education, mental health: contingent valuation through visitor surveys. The 2014 federal-economic-impact study estimated wetlands provide $50–200/ha/year in services. Strong answers note ecosystem-services valuation is contested but provides a useful frame for cost-benefit analysis of conservation.

14
[3]
A small Northern Ontario community is exposed to a 1-in-100-year flood risk. The community has 200 homes worth average $200,000 each. Calculate the expected annual flood damage if a 100-year flood damages 30% of home value. Recommend two adaptation strategies and discuss their relative cost.
Answer Key

200 homes × $200,000 × 30% damage = $12 M total exposure. Annual probability = 0.01, so expected annual loss ≈ $120,000/yr. Adaptation strategies: (1) Floodplain buy-out and relocation: very high one-time cost (~$40 M for the houses) but eliminates risk permanently. (2) Levee construction: moderate cost ($5–20 M depending on engineering); reduces but does not eliminate risk; requires ongoing maintenance. (3) Building code retrofits (flood-resistant materials, elevated foundations): low cost ($5,000–$20,000/home) for partial protection. (4) Nature-based: wetland and riparian restoration upstream. Strong answers note that climate change is rapidly increasing flood frequency, making longer-term strategies more economically viable.

15
[3]
A grade 11 student is designing their culminating CGF3M inquiry project. Critique this draft question and suggest improvements: "Is climate change real and how can we fix it?"
Answer Key

Problems: (1) Not spatially explicit — climate change is global; no specific place. (2) Closed/rhetorical first part — "is climate change real" has a settled scientific answer, not an open inquiry. (3) Too broad in scope ("how can we fix it") — exceeds project length. (4) Mixes science (is it real) with policy (fix it) — different question types. Improved versions: "Which Toronto neighbourhoods show the greatest summer extreme-heat exposure, and how does the pattern relate to tree-canopy cover?" or "How effective has Ontario's net-metering policy been at expanding rooftop solar between 2010 and 2024?" Both are spatially explicit, tractable, significant, and open.
