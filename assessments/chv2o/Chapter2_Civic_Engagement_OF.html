Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [12 marks]
1
[3]
Define and distinguish: (a) lobbying, (b) advocacy, (c) civil disobedience. Provide one Canadian example of each.
Answer Key

(a) Lobbying = direct attempt to influence elected officials' decisions; in Canada regulated under the federal Lobbying Act. Example: an industry association meeting with the Minister of Finance about a tax measure. (b) Advocacy = public support for a cause or policy, often through media, education, and coalition-building. Example: Climate Action Network Canada campaigning for stronger emissions targets. (c) Civil disobedience = deliberate, public, non-violent law-breaking to draw attention to its injustice. Example: Indigenous land-defender blockades at Wet'suwet'en or Fairy Creek; the willingness to be arrested is part of the action.

2
[3]
Identify three Canadian leaders or movements that contributed to the common good and one issue each addressed: e.g., the Famous Five, Tommy Douglas, Viola Desmond, Cindy Blackstock, Murray Sinclair, Autumn Peltier, or another of your choice.
Answer Key

Sample acceptable answers: (1) Famous Five (Murphy, McClung, Parlby, Edwards, McKinney) — Persons Case 1929, women's Senate eligibility; (2) Tommy Douglas — Saskatchewan and federal medicare, 1962–1966; (3) Viola Desmond — 1946 stand against cinema segregation in New Glasgow, NS; (4) Cindy Blackstock (Gitxsan) — First Nations Child and Family Caring Society, multiple human-rights tribunal wins for First Nations child welfare equity; (5) Murray Sinclair (Anishinaabe) — chair of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, 2008–2015; (6) Autumn Peltier (Anishinaabe) — water-protector and youth advocate. 1 mark each.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) misinformation, (b) disinformation, (c) filter bubble.
Answer Key

(a) Misinformation — false information shared without intent to deceive. (b) Disinformation — false information deliberately created and spread to deceive (foreign-state interference, partisan astroturf campaigns). (c) Filter bubble — narrow band of viewpoints created by algorithmic curation of one's media feed, producing the impression of consensus where none exists.

4
[3]
Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises and affirms which rights, of which peoples, in which constitutional Part?
Answer Key

Section 35 recognises and affirms "the existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada." It explicitly includes First Nations, Métis, and Inuit. It is in Part II of the Constitution Act, 1982 — separate from the Charter (Part I). This means s. 25 of the Charter must be read so that the Charter does not derogate from these rights.

4b
[3]
Define each: (a) lateral reading, (b) echo chamber, (c) cherry-picking statistics.
Answer Key

(a) Lateral reading — leaving a source you are evaluating to check it against other sources, rather than relying on cues internal to the page. (b) Echo chamber — environment in which one's existing views are repeatedly reinforced because feed algorithms and social networks privilege similar voices. (c) Cherry-picking — selectively citing data that supports a position while ignoring data that does not (e.g., citing one favourable poll while ignoring the broader trend).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
5
[5]
A municipal council is considering whether to designate a downtown block as a "low-vehicle zone," restricting through-traffic and adding pedestrian and bike infrastructure. Some local business owners say it will hurt commerce; many residents and active-transportation advocates say it will improve neighbourhood life.
Construct a stakeholder map for this issue. List at least five stakeholders, what each likely wants, and one consideration each brings that should shape the final decision.
Answer Key

Sample stakeholders (any five with reasoning): (1) Local business owners — concerned about loss of drive-by customers and delivery access; consider phased pilot with adapted delivery windows. (2) Residents — interested in noise, air quality, safety; consider before/after community air-quality monitoring. (3) Active-transportation users — want safe walking and biking; consider connections to broader bike network. (4) Accessibility advocates — must ensure persons with disabilities retain mobility, including paratransit drop-off; consider accessible-vehicle access plans. (5) Emergency services — require unimpeded access for fire, ambulance, police. (6) Low-income workers and commuters — some may rely on cars for night shifts when transit is sparse; consider time-of-day rules. (7) Municipal staff / planners — bring data, modelling, design options. Marks: 1 each for five stakeholders with both interest and consideration named.

6
[4]
A viral image claims that "70% of newcomers to Canada in 2024 did not file a tax return."
Apply SIFT (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims). For each letter, write one specific action you would take.
Answer Key

S — Stop: resist the urge to share or reply emotionally; pause and open a separate tab. I — Investigate the source: who is sharing the image — a verified news outlet, an anonymous account, an advocacy group? Have they been right before? Have they been corrected publicly? F — Find better coverage: search the claim on CBC News, The Globe and Mail, the Toronto Star; check if Statistics Canada or the Canada Revenue Agency has released data of this type. T — Trace claims: follow the citation to the original source — does the data match? What was the denominator (all newcomers, or only those with reportable income)? What was the timeframe? Without all four checks passing, do not share. Marks: 1 each.

7
[4]
A school is asked to revise its land acknowledgment. Outline four design principles a strong land acknowledgment should follow.
Answer Key

(1) Name specific nations and treaty: generic "Indigenous peoples" language is weak; name the specific First Nations whose traditional territory the school sits on (e.g., Mississaugas of the Credit, Anishinaabeg, Haudenosaunee, Wendat in Toronto), the relevant treaty (e.g., Treaty 13), and identify whether the territory is treaty-covered or unceded. (2) Co-design with Indigenous community partners: do not write alone; consult Indigenous educators, students, or local Indigenous service organisations. (3) Pair words with action: an acknowledgment that the school does nothing with is performative; align with TRC Calls to Action 62, 63, 66 (education and youth). (4) Renew annually: as learning grows, the acknowledgment changes — it is not a frozen artifact. Marks: 1 each.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
8
[6]
Write a 5-minute deputation (approximately 600–800 words) to your local municipal council, asking council to vote in favour of a real or invented policy item of your choice (e.g., a separated bike lane, a youth advisory committee, an Indigenous land-acknowledgment policy, mandatory single-use plastic phase-out). Follow the deputation structure: open, ask, three reasons with evidence, close. Include at least one quotation or citation.
Answer Key

Look for the following five-part structure: (1) Opening — formal address to the Chair and council, speaker name, role/connection to the issue. (2) Ask — clear one-sentence specific request directed at council action. (3) Reason 1 with at least one piece of evidence (data, citation, comparison case). (4) Reason 2 with evidence. (5) Reason 3 with evidence, often anticipating a counterargument. (6) Close — restate the ask, thank council, offer to take questions. Marks: 1 opening, 1 ask, 1 each three reasons with evidence, 1 close, 1 conventions (grammar, citation form). Strong deputations stay under the 5-minute limit (~700–900 words spoken).

9
[6]
Write a 700–900 word op-ed for a school or community newspaper arguing for or against a specific civic issue. Open with a vivid hook; state the ask; give 2–3 supporting paragraphs with evidence; address one counterargument; close with a memorable sentence. Cite at least two sources (informal hyperlinks or end notes are acceptable).
Answer Key

Marks: 1 hook (vivid, image-based, claim-based); 1 ask within first 3 paragraphs; 2 supporting paragraphs with cited evidence; 1 counterargument addressed; 1 close (memorable sentence) and conventions / citation form. Op-eds should be persuasive, single-argument, and accessible to a general reader — avoid jargon, define any specialised civic terms.

Part D: Application [13 marks]
10
[8]
Apply the Civic-Action Planning Framework to a real civic issue in your school, neighbourhood, city, or country. In 8–12 sentences, design a plan that addresses the six planning questions: (1) What is the issue? (2) Who is affected and how? (3) Whose decision is this? (4) What evidence do we have? (5) What is our specific ask? (6) How will we measure success? End with one sentence on what you would do if the decision-maker says no.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 each of the six questions answered substantively; 1 for evidence base (data, lived experience, comparison case); 1 for plan B / escalation; 1 for community-partnership awareness (who is involved beyond the planner); 1 for conventions and clarity. Strong plans show that the issue is bounded, the decision-maker is correctly identified at the right level of government, the ask is specific and measurable, and the success criterion is observable within a defined timeframe.

11
[5]
STSE Application — Reconciliation in Practice: Choose ONE of the TRC's 94 Calls to Action that relates to education (Calls 62–65) or to youth (Call 66) and write 5–7 sentences explaining what the Call says and one specific way a Grade 10 student could contribute to it (e.g., a project, an advocacy step, a school-level initiative).
Answer Key

Examples of acceptable Calls: Call 62 — age-appropriate residential-school curriculum, treaty rights, and Indigenous contributions to Canada; Call 63 — Council of Ministers of Education sharing of best practices; Call 64 — denominational schools studying Indigenous spirituality and the history of religion's role in Indigenous policy; Call 65 — research program on the educational and life impacts of residential schools; Call 66 — multi-year youth program by the federal government supporting Indigenous-led youth networks. Student-action examples: organising a National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (30 September) school event with Indigenous community speakers; auditing the English curriculum for Indigenous authors and proposing additions; presenting a TRC Calls 62–66 deputation to the school board; partnering with the local Indigenous education centre on a youth event. Marks: 2 for accurate restating of the Call; 2 for the student-action specifics; 1 for partnership / community-rooted framing.
