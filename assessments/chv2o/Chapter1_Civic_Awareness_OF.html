Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
Define each: (a) responsible government, (b) rule of law.
Answer Key

(a) Responsible government = the constitutional convention that the executive (Cabinet) must hold the confidence of the elected legislature; loss of a confidence vote means resignation or an election. (b) Rule of law = the principle that everyone, including the government, is bound by known, public, equally applied, independently enforced laws.

2
[2]
The Constitution Act, 1867 (section 91) gives the federal Parliament authority over which of the following?
Answer Key

B. Federal heads of power include the criminal law, currency, defence, and immigration (shared with provinces). Education, health-care delivery, highways, property and civil rights, and municipalities are provincial heads of power under section 92.

3
[2]
Name three categories of rights protected by the Charter and identify one specific right or freedom in each category.
Answer Key

Acceptable categories (any three): Fundamental Freedoms (s. 2): conscience & religion, expression. Democratic Rights (s. 3–5): right to vote and run for office. Mobility Rights (s. 6): right to enter, remain in, and leave Canada. Legal Rights (s. 7–14): right to counsel, presumption of innocence. Equality Rights (s. 15): equal protection without discrimination. Language Rights (s. 16–22): use of English/French in federal institutions.

4
[2]
Distinguish between majority government, minority government, and coalition government. Which is most common in Canadian history?
Answer Key

Majority: one party has more than half the seats and can pass legislation without negotiating. Minority: largest party has less than half; must negotiate to legislate. Coalition: two or more parties formally share Cabinet — rare in Canada (the most recent federal coalition discussion ended in 2008 without formation). Majority governments are most common federally; minority governments occur regularly; formal coalitions are very rare.

5
[2]
A municipal council in Ontario is created by:
Answer Key

C. Municipalities are not a separate constitutional order; they are creatures of provincial statute. The province can amend their structure, powers, and even existence by ordinary legislation — as Ontario did when it cut Toronto's council size by half in 2018.

6
[3]
Name three civic ideals (besides freedom and democracy themselves) and define each in one sentence.
Answer Key

Acceptable ideals: Common good — benefits shared by the community collectively. Equity — giving people what they need to achieve fair outcomes given different starting points. Inclusion — ensuring everyone can fully participate in civic life. Rule of law — government and citizens subject to known, equal, independently enforced laws. Pluralism — recognition and accommodation of diverse cultures and worldviews. Reconciliation — ongoing right-relationship work between Indigenous peoples and the Crown. Accountability — officials answer for their decisions.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
7
[5]
A provincial legislature passes a law requiring all public-sector workers (teachers, nurses, police, judges) to remove visible religious symbols (kirpans, hijabs, crosses, kippahs) while on duty. The legislature invokes section 33 of the Charter to shield the law from a court challenge.
Analyse: (a) which Charter rights are engaged; (b) why the government invoked section 33; (c) two democratic values in tension; (d) what restraint section 33 still places on the legislature.
Answer Key

(a) The law engages section 2(a) freedom of conscience and religion and section 15 equality rights (disproportionate impact on certain religious groups; protected ground "religion"). (b) The government invoked section 33 because a court applying the Oakes test would likely find the law fails minimal impairment — narrower options exist (e.g., individual accommodation requests). Section 33 prevents the court from striking down the law. (c) Tensions: religious freedom + equality vs. state neutrality / laïcité as the government conceives the common good; democratic majoritarianism vs. constitutional protection of minorities. (d) Section 33 has limits — the override is only five years (renewable), must be passed by the legislature explicitly, applies only to sections 2 and 7–15 (not democratic, mobility, or language rights), and the political cost can be significant. Marks: 1 for identifying the rights, 1 for explaining s. 33, 2 for the tension analysis, 1 for the limits.

8
[4]
First-past-the-post (FPTP) and proportional representation (PR) produce different outcomes from the same votes. List two outcomes that systematically favour FPTP and two that systematically favour PR, with brief reasoning.
Answer Key

FPTP-favoured outcomes: (1) decisive single-party majority governments — FPTP often gives a party with 38–40% of the vote more than 50% of the seats, enabling clear governance without coalition negotiation; (2) strong local riding-level representation — each MP represents a defined geographic constituency. PR-favoured outcomes: (1) seat share closer to vote share — small parties (Greens, Bloc) are not systematically penalised when their support is dispersed; (2) more diverse parliaments (gender, smaller parties) because list nominations are visible to party central agencies; coalition governments often build cross-party policy stability. Marks: 1 each.

9
[4]
A municipal council is considering a bylaw to ban single-use plastic bags in retail.
Apply the four concepts of political thinking to this case: (i) significance, (ii) objectives and results, (iii) stability and change, (iv) political perspective. One sentence on each.
Answer Key

Significance: precedent for municipal environmental regulation; reduction in landfill and roadside-bag pollution; signal to provincial and federal regulators. Objectives and results: objective — reduce plastic waste; results need monitoring — has bag consumption dropped, have substitutes (paper, reusables) created different environmental tradeoffs? Stability and change: stability — municipal authority to regulate retail under existing provincial statute; change — shift in consumer and retailer behaviour, driven by environmental science, public concern, and youth activism. Political perspective: retailers, environmental groups, low-income shoppers (cost of substitutes), municipal-budget officials, manufacturers — each holds a distinct view; the bylaw should be designed with all in mind. Marks: 1 each.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Write a 5–7 sentence paragraph explaining why Canadians describe Canada as a "constitutional democracy" rather than simply a "democracy." Use the terms rule of law, Charter, and section 1 (reasonable limits) at least once each.
Answer Key

Sample: Canada is a constitutional democracy because it is more than majority rule; the highest law of the country — the Constitution, including the Charter of Rights and Freedoms — limits what any government can do, even with a popular majority. Government and citizens alike are bound by these limits, which is the principle of the rule of law. The Charter sets out fundamental freedoms, democratic, mobility, legal, equality, and language rights that protect minorities from being out-voted on matters of basic dignity. Section 1 (the reasonable-limits clause) allows infringement of rights only when "demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society," through the Oakes test — pressing objective, rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionality. The Notwithstanding Clause (section 33) lets legislatures override some rights for up to five years, a feature that itself is contested. Together, these features mean Canadian democracy is bounded — not less democratic, but democratic with constitutional discipline.

11
[4]
In a paragraph (4–6 sentences), explain the difference between equality and equity. Use one concrete Canadian example (housing, schooling, language access, accessibility, or another of your choice).
Answer Key

Sample: Equality means treating everyone the same way; equity means giving people what they need to achieve fair outcomes, recognising that different starting points require different supports. For example, in Ontario public education, equal treatment would give every school the same per-pupil funding regardless of student need. Equitable treatment recognises that schools serving high-poverty neighbourhoods, large newcomer populations, or many students with disabilities need additional resources — funded through formulae like Ontario's Learning Opportunities Grant — to reach comparable outcomes. The civic principle of equity does not contradict equality; it interprets equality as fairness of outcome, not identicalness of treatment. Marks: 1 definition equality, 1 definition equity, 1 example, 1 conventions.

12
[4]
A first-time voter is unsure whether to vote in their municipal election because it "doesn't matter as much as the federal election." Write a short response (4–6 sentences) explaining why municipal voting matters, using at least two specific examples of municipal responsibilities.
Answer Key

Sample: Municipal government often touches a citizen's daily life more frequently than federal government does. Municipalities run the public transit you take to school, the local police service, the libraries, the parks, the water-treatment plant, the garbage collection, the snow clearing, and the planning rules for housing supply in your neighbourhood. Decisions about whether your street gets a bike lane, whether new apartments can be built nearby, or whether the local rink stays open through summer are made at council, not in Ottawa or Toronto. Municipal turnout is often the lowest of all three levels (around 38% in Ontario), so each vote carries proportionally more weight. Refusing to vote at the municipal level abandons the most-direct lever you have over the place you actually live. Marks: 2 for examples; 2 for the persuasive structure and accuracy.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[6]
A 17-year-old student has been refused admission to a private community swimming pool because the pool's policy is "no head coverings in the water." The student wears a hijab. The pool argues the policy is for hygienic reasons.
Apply what you have learned to analyse this case in a structured paragraph (6–8 sentences): identify whether the Charter applies; identify whether human-rights legislation applies; name the rights and the test (Oakes / Meiorin) that would govern; sketch a possible resolution that respects both swimmer and pool concerns.
Answer Key

(1) The Charter binds government; a private pool is not government, so the Charter does not directly apply. (2) The Ontario Human Rights Code (the relevant statute for private providers of services) does apply — it prohibits discrimination in services on the protected ground of creed, which covers religious observance. (3) The pool's policy may be a constructive discrimination case (a neutral-seeming policy that disproportionately impacts a protected group). (4) The Code's Meiorin / bona fide occupational requirement test asks: is the rule rationally connected to a legitimate purpose (hygiene)? Adopted in good faith? Reasonably necessary, including showing that accommodation short of undue hardship has been considered? (5) Accommodations that should be canvassed: swim hijabs (close-fitting polyester garments designed for pool use); private swim hours; alternative facilities. (6) A reasonable resolution: the pool revises the policy to allow swim-specific religious head coverings, like accredited swim hijabs already used at the Olympics, while continuing to bar loose street clothing for hygienic reasons. The pool meets both the swimmer's right and its sanitary objective. Marks: 1 for the Charter/Code distinction; 2 for naming the Code and the test; 2 for the accommodation analysis; 1 for the resolution and conventions.

14
[6]
Apply the Civic Issue Analysis Toolkit: choose one current Canadian civic issue (housing affordability, climate adaptation, the cost of living, public-transit funding, education resource gaps, healthcare wait times, Indigenous self-government, or another current issue) and write 5–7 sentences identifying (a) which level(s) of government has responsibility, (b) which Charter section(s) might be engaged if any, (c) one democratic value at stake, and (d) one specific civic-action step a Grade 10 student could take.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 for the issue framing; 2 for accurate identification of the responsible level(s) of government (most issues involve more than one); 1 for a plausibly engaged Charter section (e.g., s. 15 equality for housing discrimination, s. 7 security of the person for healthcare-access cases, s. 35 outside the Charter for Indigenous self-government); 1 for a clearly named democratic value (common good, equity, reconciliation, accountability); 1 for a realistic action step (writing to an MP, deputing to a council, joining a youth advocacy network, organising a school petition). Strong answers connect all four elements coherently rather than treating them as a checklist.
