Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[3]B · Government
Name Canada's three levels of government and one major responsibility you associate with each.
Answer Key

Federal (defence, criminal law, citizenship, currency); provincial (health-care delivery, education, highways, property and civil rights); municipal (water/wastewater, fire/police, garbage, planning and zoning, transit, libraries). Indigenous governments are increasingly recognised as a fourth pillar.

2
[3]B · Constitution
Identify two key things the Constitution Act, 1982 accomplished, and explain what is meant by "patriation."
Answer Key

The 1982 Act (1) added the Charter of Rights and Freedoms as constitutional law; (2) created the amending formula; (3) included s. 35 recognising Aboriginal and treaty rights. Patriation means bringing the power to amend Canada's Constitution home to Canada (from the British Parliament).

3
[3]B · Charter
Name three categories of rights protected by the Charter, with one specific right in each.
Answer Key

Acceptable categories: Fundamental Freedoms (s. 2 — religion, expression), Democratic Rights (s. 3 — right to vote), Mobility (s. 6 — entry/exit), Legal Rights (s. 7–14 — security of person, counsel), Equality (s. 15 — non-discrimination), Language (s. 16–22), Minority Language Education (s. 23).

4
[2]B
Section 33 of the Charter (the "notwithstanding clause") allows a legislature to override which Charter sections, and for how long?
Answer Key

B. S. 33 covers fundamental freedoms (s. 2) and legal/equality rights (s. 7–15); does not cover democratic rights (s. 3–5), mobility (s. 6), language (s. 16–22), or s. 28 sex-equality. S. 35 (Aboriginal rights) is outside the Charter and cannot be subject to s. 33.

5
[2]B · Elections
Under first-past-the-post (FPTP), a candidate wins a riding by:
Answer Key

B. Single-member plurality: highest vote count wins, regardless of majority.

6
[3]C · Movements
For each person/group, identify one civic contribution: (a) the Famous Five; (b) Tommy Douglas; (c) Viola Desmond.
Answer Key

(a) The Famous Five won the 1929 Persons Case at the JCPC, holding women are "persons" eligible for Senate appointment, articulating the "living tree" doctrine. (b) Tommy Douglas established universal hospital insurance and then medicare in Saskatchewan (1962); the federal Medical Care Act followed in 1966. (c) Viola Desmond stood against cinema segregation in New Glasgow, NS (1946); she now appears on the $10 banknote.

7
[3]C · Indigenous
Define: (a) the Indian Act; (b) the Royal Proclamation of 1763; (c) the TRC's 94 Calls to Action.
Answer Key

(a) Indian Act — federal statute (1876, still in force amended) imposing federal control over First Nations governance, status, and reserves. (b) Royal Proclamation of 1763 — Crown document recognising Indigenous title to unceded lands and requiring Crown-to-Nation purchase before settlement; still cited in modern Indigenous-rights litigation. (c) TRC's 94 Calls to Action — 2015 set of recommendations from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission spanning child welfare, education, health, justice, and reconciliation; guidance for reconciliation work.

8
[3]A · Inquiry
Name and briefly define the four concepts of political thinking.
Answer Key

(1) Political significance — why something matters and for whom. (2) Objectives and results — what actors set out to do vs. what happened. (3) Stability and change — what remained constant and what was transformed. (4) Political perspective — whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue.

9
[3]A · Media Literacy
Define and distinguish: misinformation, disinformation, propaganda.
Answer Key

Misinformation — false information shared without intent to deceive. Disinformation — false information deliberately created and spread to deceive. Propaganda — information (true, false, or selectively framed) produced by an actor to advance a political agenda.

9a
[1]B · Government
A Member of Provincial Parliament (MPP) sits in:
Answer Key

B. MPPs (called MLAs in most other provinces) sit in the provincial legislature. MPs sit in the federal House of Commons.

9b
[1]B
The minimum age to vote in a federal election in Canada is:
Answer Key

B. The Canada Elections Act sets the federal voting age at 18 on election day. Some provinces and parties debate lowering it to 16.

9c
[1]B · Charter
Section 15 of the Charter protects:
Answer Key

A. Section 15(1) guarantees equality without discrimination. Section 15(2) protects affirmative-action programs. Note: the Charter does not include a "right to bear arms" or a "right to private property."

9d
[1]B
Senators in Canada must retire at age:
Answer Key

C. Mandatory Senate retirement is age 75, set by amendment in 1965.

9e
[1]B
The Constitution Act, 1867 was originally called:
Answer Key

B. Renamed in 1982 when the Constitution was patriated.

9f
[1]B
A federal election is called by:
Answer Key

B. The Crown formally dissolves Parliament on the PM's advice; a defeat on a confidence vote also triggers an election under the convention of responsible government.

9g
[1]C · Movements
The Famous Five appealed the Persons Case to:
Answer Key

B. Before 1949, the JCPC in London was Canada's final court of appeal. The 1929 decision reversed the Canadian Supreme Court and held women were "persons" within s. 24.

9h
[1]C · Indigenous
The National Day for Truth and Reconciliation is held on:
Answer Key

C. Established by Bill C-5 (2021) as a federal statutory holiday, marked by Orange Shirt Day commemoration. 21 June is National Indigenous Peoples Day, which is also significant but separate.

9i
[1]C · Indigenous
Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises rights of:
Answer Key

C. Section 35(2) explicitly states "the aboriginal peoples of Canada includes the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada."

9j
[1]B
A bill becomes law in the federal Parliament after passing:
Answer Key

B. Bills must pass first, second, and third readings in both chambers (with committee study between second and third reading), then receive Royal Assent from the Governor General.

9k
[1]A · Inquiry
The concept of political thinking most useful for asking "Whose voices shape this issue?" is:
Answer Key

D. Political perspective is the distinctively political lens for "whose voices."

9l
[1]A · Sources
A government statistical release (e.g., Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey) is best classified as a:
Answer Key

A. Government data releases are primary — they present original measurements, methodology, and data. Commentary on them is secondary.

9m
[1]C · Engagement
A 5-minute formal address to a municipal council is called a:
Answer Key

C. A deputation is a structured spoken address under the council's procedural rules.

9n
[1]B
The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms applies to:
Answer Key

C. The Charter binds governments under s. 32(1). Private discrimination is addressed by provincial human-rights codes and the Canadian Human Rights Act, not the Charter directly.

9o
[1]B
In a parliamentary democracy, "responsible government" means:
Answer Click

B. Responsible government is the convention that the executive's authority depends on its ability to command a majority in the elected house. Loss of a confidence vote leads to resignation or an election.

9p
[1]A · Media
The discipline of leaving a source you are evaluating to check it against other sources is called:
Answer Key

B. Lateral reading is the practice researchers found professional fact-checkers use habitually.

9q
[1]B
The "duty to consult" Indigenous peoples on Crown decisions affecting their rights was established in:
Answer Key

C. The Supreme Court in Haida Nation articulated the Crown's duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Indigenous peoples when contemplating actions that may adversely affect their rights — further developed in Tsilhqot'in Nation (2014).

9r
[1]C
"Civil disobedience" requires which of the following?
Answer Key

B. Drawing on Thoreau, Gandhi, and King: the act must be public, non-violent, targeted at the unjust law, and accept the legal penalty as part of the protest's moral force.

9s
[1]B
"Section 1" of the Charter is best described as:
Answer Key

B. Section 1 allows rights to be limited "subject only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society" — applied via the Oakes proportionality test.

9t
[1]B · Elections
Approximately how many seats are in the federal House of Commons?
Answer Key

C. 338 seats currently; the Representation Order increases this to 343 effective at the next general redistribution. 124 is the Ontario Legislative Assembly; 105 is the appointed Senate.

9u
[1]B
In Ontario, municipal elections happen:
Answer Key

B. The Municipal Elections Act sets fixed four-year terms in Ontario. The fixed date is the fourth Monday of October in the election year (e.g., 24 October 2022, next 26 October 2026).

9v
[1]A · Communication
A one-page document that lays out the problem, evidence, and recommended action for a decision-maker is called a:
Answer Key

B. Position papers (also called briefing notes or policy briefs) are the standard short-form for decision-maker audiences.

9w
[1]B · Values
The phrase "common good" refers to:
Answer Key

B. The common good is what is good for the community as a whole — including goods that no individual can produce alone (public health, civic infrastructure, a functioning democracy).

9x
[1]A · Statistics
A poll's "margin of error" of ±3% at "19 times out of 20" means:
Answer Key

B. "19 times out of 20" = 95% confidence interval. The ±3% bounds the random sampling error; it does not address question-wording bias, non-response bias, or other systematic errors.

9y
[1]B
Which Canadian jurisdiction is responsible for health-care delivery (running hospitals, paying doctors)?
Answer Key

B. Provinces deliver and operate the health system. The federal government funds and sets standards (Canada Health Act 1984: universality, comprehensiveness, accessibility, portability, public administration).

9z
[1]C
When the federal government formally said sorry for the Indian Residential Schools system, the apology was delivered in 2008 by:
Answer Key

B. Prime Minister Stephen Harper delivered the federal apology on the floor of the House of Commons on 11 June 2008. A second apology, on behalf of the Government of Canada to Inuit, was delivered by Prime Minister Justin Trudeau on 8 March 2019.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
10
[8]B + A · Case Analysis
In Quebec, the National Assembly passed Bill 21 (2019), which prohibits certain public-sector workers from wearing visible religious symbols while on duty. The Bill invoked s. 33 of the Charter ("notwithstanding") to shield it from s. 2 and s. 15 challenges.
Apply the four concepts of political thinking to this case in a structured response. Address: (i) political significance, (ii) objectives and results (so far as known), (iii) stability and change, (iv) political perspective. Aim for 10–14 sentences.
Answer Key

Sample marking guide. Significance: first major peacetime use of s. 33 for fundamental freedoms in Canada; precedent for future federal/provincial use of the override; high stakes for religious minorities (Muslim women wearing hijab, Sikh men wearing turbans, observant Jews wearing kippah) in public-sector employment in Quebec. Objectives and results: Quebec's stated objective — preserving state laïcité (secularism) — vs. actual results (Muslim women teachers in Quebec have lost positions or feared transfer; the federal government has hesitated to intervene; court challenges continue under provisions outside s. 33 protection like s. 28 sex-equality and s. 23 minority-language education). Stability and change: stability — Canadian constitutional federalism, parliamentary sovereignty principle that s. 33 protects. Change — public-sector workforce composition; previously rare s. 33 use is becoming more frequent (Ontario, Saskatchewan). Political perspective: Quebec majority public opinion (defending laïcité as Quebec identity); affected workers and communities (rights violation); federal Crown (constrained); civil-liberties organisations (CCLA opposed); religious-pluralism advocates. Marks: 2 each concept applied substantively.

11
[8]A · Inquiry
Construct a political-inquiry plan to investigate: "What civic-action steps should the City of [your city] take to address transit-equity gaps for low-income riders?" Walk through all five stages of the inquiry process: (1) refine the question with one or two sub-questions; (2) name three credible sources you would consult; (3) name one analytical tool you would use (cause-and-effect chain, stakeholder matrix, comparison table) and what it would compare; (4) name the strongest counterargument; (5) name the form (deputation, op-ed, infographic, position paper) you would use to communicate, and to whom.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 2 for question refinement and sub-questions; 2 for credible sources (e.g., city transit-equity audit, Statistics Canada household-income data, transit-rider advocacy organisations like TTCriders, academic research from the University of Toronto School of Cities); 1 for analytical tool with specific application; 1 for steel-manned counterargument (e.g., budget constraints, free-fare programs reducing service capacity); 2 for form-and-audience match (deputation to council transit committee; op-ed for the local paper; infographic for social media outreach to ridership). Strong plans show internal coherence.

12
[5]A · Source Evaluation
A social-media post by an unverified account shows a graphic claiming that "Crime in [Canadian city] is at a 20-year high. Source: Police report."
Apply the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) — one specific lateral-reading question for each criterion. Conclude whether you would share the post.
Answer Key

Currency: when was the underlying police report published? Crime data lags 6–12 months. Relevance: "crime" is broad — is it violent crime, property crime, or aggregate? Headlines often hide composition shifts. Authority: who is the account sharing this — a verified local outlet, a politician, an anonymous account? Can the original police-services-board report be located? Accuracy: does the graphic's number match the underlying report? Has the report been quoted accurately and in context? Purpose: what is the apparent goal — to inform, to alarm, to influence an upcoming election? Conclusion: not credible enough to share without confirming against the actual police report and corroborating coverage from CBC News and the local paper. Marks: 1 each criterion.

13
[4]B · Federalism
For each issue, identify the level(s) of government primarily responsible and briefly explain why: (a) standards for safe drinking water on First Nations reserves; (b) high-school graduation requirements in Ontario; (c) the prosecution of a criminal assault case; (d) the operation of the local bus system.
Answer Key

(a) Federal — Indigenous Services Canada regulates and funds drinking-water systems on reserve. (Note: provincial drinking-water standards under the Safe Drinking Water Act apply off-reserve.) (b) Provincial — Ontario's Ministry of Education sets graduation requirements (30 credits, OSSLT, 40 community-involvement hours). (c) Federal Criminal Code + provincial administration of justice — Parliament writes the law (s. 91); the provincial Crown prosecutes. (d) Municipal — bus systems are run by municipalities, with provincial funding and federal capital transfers (e.g., the federal Permanent Public Transit Fund).

13a
[2]B
A municipality wants to ban e-scooters from sidewalks. Which level(s) of government has the authority to regulate this, and under what statute or framework?
Answer Key

Primarily municipal, under provincial enabling statute (Municipal Act, 2001). The province (under the Highway Traffic Act) sets the broader framework for vehicle classification; municipalities have local authority over sidewalks, parks, and public spaces.

13b
[2]A
Identify two ways the four concepts of political thinking might disagree about a single case. Briefly explain.
Answer Key

(1) An event can be highly significant for some perspectives and unimportant from others (e.g., a Charter ruling on s. 15 equality is highly significant to affected minority groups but unnoticed by an indifferent majority). (2) Objectives and results can diverge — a policy's intent (e.g., carbon-pricing for emissions reduction) may not match its results (mixed impact, regional pushback). The concepts illuminate different aspects of the same case, not a single "right" answer.

13c
[2]C
Identify two practical reasons why community partnership (the "Nothing about us, without us" principle) matters more than good intentions in civic action.
Answer Key

(1) Better solutions — people closest to a problem know its texture, history, and existing attempts that an outsider would miss. (2) Broader legitimacy — solutions designed without affected communities are often rejected by them, regardless of merit. (3) Stronger relationships — partnerships built in one campaign make the next campaign possible. Marks: 1 each for any two.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
14
[8]A · Communication
Write a 600–800 word op-ed for a Canadian newspaper on a civic issue of your choice. Follow op-ed conventions: hook (vivid first sentence), state your specific ask within the first three paragraphs, give two to three argumentative paragraphs with evidence, address one strong counterargument with a steel-man, close with a memorable sentence. Cite at least two sources informally (hyperlinks or end-references acceptable).
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 hook; 1 ask placed early; 2 supporting paragraphs with evidence; 1 counterargument steel-manned; 1 close (memorable sentence); 2 conventions (grammar, voice, citation form, word-count discipline). Strong op-eds remain accessible to a general reader and have a single argumentative through-line.

15
[8]C · Communication
Write a 5-minute deputation (approximately 600–800 words) to a municipal council asking for a specific action on a real or invented issue. Follow the deputation structure: respectful opening; speaker name, role, and connection; specific ask in one sentence; three reasons with evidence (data, comparison case, quotation, lived experience); short close repeating the ask.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 respectful opening to council; 1 identification and connection; 1 clear specific ask; 1 each three reasons with evidence (3); 1 close; 1 conventions and word-count discipline. The deputation should stay within 5 minutes when read aloud.

16
[4]B · Communication
Explain to a Grade 9 student in 4–6 sentences why Canada is described as a constitutional democracy and not just a democracy. Use the terms rule of law, Charter, and either section 1 (reasonable limits) or section 33 (notwithstanding clause) at least once each.
Answer Key

See Chapter 1 OF Question 10 for a sample answer (5–7 sentence model). Marks: 1 rule-of-law definition, 1 Charter introduced, 1 section 1 or section 33 explained, 1 conventions.

17
[5]C · Communication
Design (in words — describe what you would draw) a 30-second infographic on either: (a) Canada's three levels of government, (b) the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, or (c) the political-inquiry process. Describe the three or four central elements, the headline/claim, the colour palette (3 colours max), and the citation footer.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 clear topic and headline/claim; 1 three or four central elements identified visually; 1 disciplined colour palette (3 colours max); 1 source citation footer; 1 mobile-readable design discipline (large central numbers, minimal text). Strong infographics shift behaviour or perception with a single image.

17a
[2]A
Write a strong inquiry question for the topic "youth and climate." Apply the four criteria: open-ended, bounded, contestable, significant.
Answer Key

Sample: "Should Ontario require all secondary schools to develop a board-approved climate-adaptation plan by 2028, and what evidence supports each side?" — Open-ended (no yes/no), bounded (Ontario, secondary, 2028), contestable (informed people disagree), significant (real-world policy relevance). Weak versions: "Is climate change bad?" (yes/no, not contestable) or "What is climate change?" (fact-recall).

17b
[2]C
A student wants to start a school-based mental-health initiative. List two community partners they should engage before drafting the proposal, and explain why each matters.
Answer Key

(1) School counselors / social workers — they know existing supports, gaps, and the privacy / safe-messaging rules around mental-health communication. (2) Students with lived mental-health experience — through a peer-support club or advisory student voice; their input shapes whether a proposal lands as helpful or condescending. Acceptable alternatives: local CMHA branch, Indigenous mental-health services, youth health partnerships at the local hospital. Marks: 1 each.

17c
[2]B
A federal government wants to allocate funding to address food insecurity in northern communities. Identify two challenges this involves at the federal-provincial-Indigenous interface.
Answer Key

(1) Jurisdictional overlap — food security touches federal Indigenous Services, provincial social-assistance programs, and Indigenous government / land-claim provisions; coordination is required. (2) Duty to consult — under Crown-Indigenous obligations (Haida Nation, 2004), the federal Crown must meaningfully consult affected Indigenous communities; a federal program designed without consultation risks both political failure and legal challenge. Other valid challenges: transportation costs, climate/access in northern communities; the federal Nutrition North Canada program's mixed track record; the distinction between food security and food sovereignty. Marks: 1 each.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
18
[10]C · Civic-Action Plan
Capstone Application: Design a complete civic-action plan for a real local, national, or global civic issue. Your plan must answer all six framework questions: (1) What is the issue (specific, bounded)? (2) Who is affected and how (stakeholder map)? (3) Whose decision is this (named decision-maker at the correct level of government)? (4) What evidence do we have (at least three sources)? (5) What is our specific ask (measurable, action-able)? (6) How will we measure success (outcome metric, timeframe)? End with a reflection paragraph (3–4 sentences) on the strongest counter-argument and how you would respond.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 each six framework questions answered substantively (6); 1 community-partnership awareness (who is involved beyond the planner); 1 ethical considerations (no harm, transparency, who benefits); 1 reflection / counterargument; 1 conventions and clarity. Top-band plans show that the issue is bounded, the decision-maker is correctly identified at the right level of government, the ask is specific and measurable, the success metric is observable within a defined timeframe, and the planner has engaged the strongest opposing view honestly.

19
[8]B + C · Indigenous-Crown
In Spring 2026, your school board is debating whether to adopt the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 62(i): "Make age-appropriate curriculum on residential schools, Treaties, and Aboriginal peoples' historical and contemporary contributions to Canada a mandatory education requirement for Kindergarten to Grade Twelve students."
Apply what you have learned in 8–12 sentences: (a) identify two democratic values supporting the Call's adoption; (b) name one constitutional or legal source (s. 35, UNDRIP, the TRC report itself) that strengthens the case; (c) identify one practical barrier the board must address (teacher training, curriculum materials, community partnership); (d) name one community partner the board should consult before deciding; (e) state your own conclusion on whether the board should adopt the Call, with reasoning.
Answer Key

Sample marking guide. (a) Reconciliation and inclusion; equity (Indigenous students see their history; non-Indigenous students learn truthful history); the common good. (b) Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognising treaty rights; UNDRIP (UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, Article 14 on Indigenous-controlled education and Article 15 on accurate representation in education); the TRC report itself as evidence of the urgent need. (c) Teacher training (most current Ontario teachers were not themselves taught Indigenous history); resourcing of age-appropriate texts; sustained community partnership beyond a single launch. (d) The local First Nation, Métis Nation, or Inuit organisation; the school board's Indigenous Education Advisory Committee; an Elder-in-residence. (e) Conclusion should be defended with reference to the values, the legal source, the practical considerations, and the partnership pathway. Marks: 2 for (a) and (b), 2 for (c) and (d), 4 for (e) — well-reasoned conclusion.

In 6–10 sentences, identify: (a) one civic skill you developed in this course that you will use after the course ends; (b) one civic issue you commit to follow over the next year, and how you will follow it; (c) one type of civic action you will personally take in the next six months. Your answer should integrate language from the curriculum (democratic values, civic ideals, the political-inquiry process, the four concepts of political thinking) and demonstrate that civic engagement is a habit you have begun.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 2 each (a), (b), (c) substantively answered; 1 for integration of curriculum vocabulary and concepts. This is the capstone reflection — strong answers show that the course produced a habit (not just a grade), name a specific issue and action, and reflect candidly on what was learned.
