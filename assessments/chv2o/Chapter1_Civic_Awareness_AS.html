Topic 1.1 — What is Democracy?
Question 1
Canada is best described as a:
Solution: Each adjective does work. Parliamentary: the executive (Cabinet) is drawn from the legislature. Constitutional: government is limited by supreme law. Federal: powers are shared between Ottawa and the provinces. Democracy: legitimacy comes from the consent of the governed.
Question 2
"Tyranny of the majority" refers to:
Solution: Pure majoritarianism (51% can do anything to 49%) is not democracy in the Canadian sense — the Charter exists precisely to put certain questions out of reach of temporary majorities, protecting minority rights.
Topic 1.2 — The Constitution & Federalism
Question 3
The Constitution Act, 1982 did NOT do which of the following?
Solution: The Canadian federation was created by the Constitution Act, 1867 (originally the British North America Act). The 1982 Act patriated the Constitution, added the Charter, and created an amending formula — but it did not create the federation, which already existed.
Question 4
Helmet laws on Ontario roads are governed by:
Solution: Section 92 of the 1867 Act gives provinces "property and civil rights" and authority over highways. Ontario's helmet rules sit in the provincial Highway Traffic Act.
Topic 1.3 — The Charter of Rights and Freedoms
Question 5
"Everyone has the following fundamental freedoms: (a) freedom of conscience and religion; (b) freedom of thought, belief, opinion and expression…"
This text is from which section of the Charter?
Solution: Section 2 sets out the four fundamental freedoms: (a) conscience and religion, (b) thought, belief, opinion, expression — including press, (c) peaceful assembly, (d) association.
Question 6
Section 33 (the "notwithstanding clause") allows Parliament or a provincial legislature to:
Solution: Section 33 permits a five-year (renewable) legislative override of sections 2 (fundamental freedoms) and 7–15 (legal & equality rights). It does not apply to democratic rights (sections 3–5), mobility (s. 6), language rights (ss. 16–22), or section 28 sex-equality. Indigenous rights under section 35 are outside the Charter entirely.
Question 7
The Oakes test (from R v. Oakes, 1986) requires that a law limiting a Charter right:
Solution: The four-stage Oakes test is the framework courts use to apply section 1 of the Charter (the reasonable-limits clause). Most Charter cases turn on this proportionality analysis.
Topic 1.4 — Levels of Government
Question 8
Public-transit systems (e.g., the TTC in Toronto) are primarily the responsibility of:
Solution: Cities operate transit; provinces create the legal framework (e.g., Metrolinx in the GTA, City of Toronto Act); federal Build Canada / Public Transit Infrastructure programs contribute capital funding. Most public services in Canada involve more than one level — naming which leads matters.
Question 9
Senators in Canada are:
Solution: Senators are appointed; since 2016 the Independent Advisory Board for Senate Appointments recommends candidates to the PM, who advises the Governor General. Senate reform — including election or abolition — would require a constitutional amendment.
Topic 1.5 — Elections & FPTP
Question 10
Under first-past-the-post (FPTP), a candidate wins a riding by:
Solution: FPTP is single-member plurality. The highest vote-getter wins, regardless of whether that's a majority. In a four-way race, 30% can win.
Question 11
A minority government in Canada means:
Solution: A minority government must build legislative majorities issue-by-issue or via a supply-and-confidence agreement (such as the federal Liberal–NDP arrangement of 2022–2024). A coalition government — formal cabinet sharing — is rare in Canada.
Topic 1.6 — Civic Ideals
Question 12
The difference between equality and equity is best captured as:
Solution: Equity drives policies such as targeted social programs, affirmative-action programs (protected by section 15(2) of the Charter), and language-access services — recognising that equal treatment of unequal starting points produces unequal outcomes.
