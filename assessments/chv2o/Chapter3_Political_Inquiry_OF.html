Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [12 marks]
1
[3]
Name and briefly define the four concepts of political thinking.
Answer Key

(i) Political Significance — why an event, issue, decision, or person matters, for whom, and over what time horizon. (ii) Objectives and Results — what actors intended to achieve vs. what actually happened (separating empirical evaluation from value judgement). (iii) Stability and Change — what has remained constant in the political system vs. what has been transformed, and what drives the change. (iv) Political Perspective — whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue (recognising politics as the contest of perspectives).

2
[3]
List the five stages of the political inquiry process. Explain why the process is described as iterative rather than linear.
Answer Key

(1) Formulate questions. (2) Gather and organise evidence. (3) Interpret and analyse. (4) Evaluate and draw conclusions. (5) Communicate findings. The process is iterative because new evidence can require returning to an earlier stage — e.g., evidence may reveal that the original question was poorly framed, prompting reformulation; or analysis may produce sub-questions that require more evidence-gathering. The inquiry circle, not a straight line.

3
[3]
Distinguish: (a) primary source, (b) secondary source, (c) tertiary source. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

(a) Primary — created at the time and place of the event (Hansard transcript of a parliamentary debate; an Indigenous treaty document; a court ruling; a news article published on the day of an event). (b) Secondary — interpretation of primary sources after the fact (a textbook chapter; an academic article in the Canadian Journal of Political Science). (c) Tertiary — summaries of secondary literature (a Wikipedia article; an encyclopedia entry) — useful as a starting point but rarely citable in formal civic inquiry.

4
[3]
Name three common logical fallacies and provide a one-sentence example of each in a civic context.
Answer Key

Sample answers: Ad hominem — attacking the speaker rather than the argument ("Don't trust her on housing policy — she's young.") Strawman — mischaracterising the opponent's view to attack it ("She says we should fund harm-reduction; she wants to legalise all drugs."). False equivalence — treating a fringe view as equal to a well-supported view ("Some say the Earth is round; others say flat — it's a debate."). Slippery slope — claiming one small change inevitably leads to disaster without showing the steps. Bandwagon — "Everyone is supporting this, so should you." Marks: 1 each, with example.

4b
[2]
Identify the form most appropriate for each civic-communication situation: (a) addressing a municipal council meeting; (b) reaching a general newspaper readership; (c) sharing a single striking statistic on social media; (d) presenting a structured policy recommendation to an MPP.
Answer Key

(a) Deputation — 5-minute formal address under procedural rules. (b) Op-ed — 700–1000 word persuasive opinion piece. (c) Infographic — visual representation of one or two data points, readable in 30 seconds. (d) Position paper — one-page structured summary of problem, evidence, ask. Marks: 0.5 each.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
5
[5]
A Grade 10 student wants to investigate the question: "Should Ontario lower the voting age from 18 to 16 in provincial elections?"
Construct three strong sub-questions that would help the student answer the main question, and for each sub-question identify one credible source (primary or secondary, Canadian or international) that would be useful.
Answer Key

Sample sub-questions and sources: (1) "How does FPTP voting work in Ontario, and what is current voter turnout by age cohort?" Source: Elections Ontario post-election reports. (2) "What happened to youth turnout in Scotland after voting age was lowered to 16 (2014 independence referendum)?" Source: Electoral Commission UK or peer-reviewed comparative study. (3) "What does the Canadian Charter say about voting-age limits, and have they been challenged in court?" Source: Charter s. 3 + the Fitzgerald v. Canada (2013) decision. (4) "What do current Ontario civil-society groups (Vote 16 Ontario, Canadian Civic Education Network) argue?" Source: position papers. (5) "What concerns do critics raise (maturity, undue influence, school-day politicisation)?" Source: think-tank papers from the C.D. Howe Institute or academic articles. Marks: 1 each sub-question (3), 1 each source (3), minus overlap = 5 marks.

6
[4]
An infographic claims: "Indigenous high-school graduation rates in Canada are 5x lower than non-Indigenous rates."
Apply lateral reading: list four questions you would ask before treating this claim as credible. Be specific.
Answer Key

(1) What is the original source? Statistics Canada? AFN? An advocacy group? Following the citation matters. (2) How are the populations defined? "Indigenous" includes First Nations on-reserve, First Nations off-reserve, Métis, and Inuit — these populations have very different graduation rates; aggregating them hides meaningful differences. (3) What is the timeframe? Education data lags 5–10 years; pre-2018 data tells a different story from current. (4) What is the comparison? "5x lower" is a striking framing; the actual numbers (e.g., on-reserve First Nations grade-12 completion ~48%, national average ~89%) tell a more precise story without the multiplication framing. Marks: 1 each.

7
[4]
Apply the four concepts of political thinking to one case of your choice: the 1969 White Paper on Indian policy; the 1982 patriation of the Constitution; the 1995 Quebec Referendum; the 2015 federal election; the 2019 SNC-Lavalin affair; or another case of comparable significance.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 for accurate application of significance (who is affected, why it matters, when, and over what time horizon); 1 for accurate application of objectives and results (intended vs. actual outcomes); 1 for accurate application of stability and change (what remained constant, what changed, and what drove change); 1 for accurate application of political perspective (which voices held which views, and on what grounds). Strong answers integrate the four concepts coherently rather than treating them as a checklist.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
8
[5]
Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences) that explains, to a Grade 9 student, why lateral reading is more useful than vertical reading for evaluating information on the internet. Include one concrete example.
Answer Key

Sample: When you read a webpage vertically, you stay on that page and look for credibility cues — the design, the author bio, the references at the bottom. The problem is that all of those can be faked. Lateral reading means leaving the page to check what other sources say about the same claim. For example, if a Twitter thread cites "Stanford research," lateral reading would open a new tab to check whether Stanford actually published it. Professional fact-checkers (researched by the Stanford History Education Group) use lateral reading by habit; novice readers tend to read vertically and are fooled more often. The discipline is to leave the source you are evaluating and triangulate before you draw conclusions. Marks: 1 definitions, 1 contrast, 1 example, 1 evidence/example specificity, 1 conventions.

9
[8]
Write a 700–900 word op-ed for a Canadian newspaper on a civic issue of your choice. Follow op-ed conventions: hook, ask, two to three argumentative paragraphs with evidence, counterargument addressed, memorable close. Cite at least two sources.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 hook (vivid, image-based or claim-based first sentence); 1 ask within the first three paragraphs; 2 argumentative paragraphs with evidence each cited; 1 counterargument addressed with a steel-man, not strawman; 1 close (memorable single sentence); 1 conventions (grammar, citation format). Strong op-eds are persuasive, single-argument, accessible to a general reader, and balanced in tone — they make a case without strawmanning the opposition.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
10
[6]
Apply the source-evaluation framework to an issue in your own life right now: choose any current civic claim circulating in your school, neighbourhood, or social-media feed. Identify the claim, the source, and run the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) on it in 6–8 sentences. Conclude whether the claim is credible, partially credible, or not credible.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 clear identification of the claim and source; 1 each five CRAAP criteria applied substantively (not just named); 1 explicit credibility verdict with reasoning. Strong answers note that "not credible" does not mean "false" — it means "insufficiently supported to warrant confident assertion or sharing."

11
[6]
Apply the political-inquiry process to a real-world civic issue. In 8–10 sentences, walk through the five stages: (1) formulate a sharp inquiry question; (2) identify two evidence sources you would consult; (3) name one analytical tool you would use (cause-and-effect chain, stakeholder matrix, comparison table); (4) identify the strongest counterargument to your likely conclusion; (5) name the form (deputation, op-ed, infographic, position paper) you would use to communicate, and to whom.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 each five stages substantively applied; 1 for the integration — does the inquirer show the stages connect (e.g., does the form they choose match the audience and the conclusion they expect to reach)? Strong answers also include a falsifying condition: "I would change my conclusion if I learned X."
