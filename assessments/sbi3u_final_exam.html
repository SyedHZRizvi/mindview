[2]B · Diversity
Which is the correct order from broadest to narrowest taxonomic rank?
Answer Key

B. Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.

2
[2]B
Name the three domains of life and one defining feature of each.
Answer Key

Bacteria — prokaryotic, peptidoglycan cell walls; Archaea — prokaryotic, ether-linked membrane lipids, often extremophiles; Eukarya — eukaryotic (nucleus, membrane-bound organelles).

3
[2]B
Simpson's diversity index for a community of 50/50/0 = ?
Answer Key

Proportions 0.5 and 0.5. Σp² = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5. D = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5. Two equally abundant species → moderate diversity.

4
[3]C · Evolution
List the four conditions required for natural selection.
Answer Key

(1) Variation in a trait; (2) heritability of the variation; (3) differential survival/reproduction; (4) overproduction of offspring. All four must hold.

[2]C
A new river splits an ancestral species into two non-overlapping populations. Over time they cannot interbreed. This is:
Answer Key

B (Allopatric). Geographic barrier + isolation + accumulated divergence = allopatric speciation.

[2]C
In a population, 9% are homozygous recessive (aa). Under Hardy-Weinberg, the frequency of the recessive allele q is:
Answer Key

\(q^2 = 0.09 \Rightarrow q = \mathbf{0.3}\).

[2]D · Genetics
Two heterozygous (Tt) tall plants are crossed. What fraction of the offspring are expected to be short (tt)?
Answer Key

Tt × Tt: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt → 1/4 = 0.25 short.

[2]D
A man with type AB blood (IAIB) and a woman with type O (ii) have a child. What blood types are possible?
Answer Key

C (A or B). IAIB × ii → IAi (type A) or IBi (type B) — never O or AB.

Answer Key

B. Prophase I — homologs pair (synapsis) and exchange DNA segments at chiasmata.

In the human digestive system, most absorption occurs in the:
Answer Key

B. Small intestine — villi and microvilli provide ~250 m² of absorptive surface. Stomach absorbs little; large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes.

The chamber that pumps blood into the pulmonary circuit is the:
Answer Key

B. Right ventricle → pulmonary trunk → lungs. Left ventricle pumps into the systemic circuit.

12
[2]E
Define the term nephron and state its main role.
Answer Key

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney (~1 million per kidney). Each nephron filters blood at the glomerulus, reabsorbs useful substances along the tubule, and secretes wastes into the filtrate, producing urine.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
14
[5]B · Biodiversity Calculation
A boreal forest plot contains: 150 black spruce, 50 balsam fir, 30 paper birch, 20 trembling aspen. Calculate Simpson's diversity index D = 1 − Σ(pi)². Show all work.
Answer Key

Total N = 250. Proportions: spruce 0.60, fir 0.20, birch 0.12, aspen 0.08. Σp² = 0.36 + 0.04 + 0.0144 + 0.0064 = 0.4208. D = 1 − 0.4208 ≈ 0.58. Black spruce dominates, lowering D below the maximum (0.75) achievable with four equally abundant species.

15
[5]C · Hardy-Weinberg
In a Canadian population, 4% of newborns have a recessive disorder. Assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, calculate (a) q, (b) p, (c) the carrier (heterozygous) frequency. Discuss the implication for genetic counselling.
Answer Key

(a) \(q^2 = 0.04 \Rightarrow q = 0.2\). (b) \(p = 1 − q = 0.8\). (c) Carrier \(2pq = 2(0.8)(0.2) = 0.32\) — 32% of the population is heterozygous. Implication: most carriers are unaware they carry the allele. Genetic counselling allows at-risk couples to assess transmission probabilities; for two carriers, each child has a 25% chance of being affected.

16
[5]D · Dihybrid
In tomatoes, red fruit (R) is dominant to yellow (r), and tall vine (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). A double heterozygote (RrTt) is crossed with a homozygous recessive plant (rrtt). Predict the offspring phenotypic ratio, assuming independent assortment.
Answer Key

Test cross. RrTt gametes (1/4 each): RT, Rt, rT, rt. rrtt gives only rt. Offspring: 1/4 RrTt (red tall), 1/4 Rrtt (red dwarf), 1/4 rrTt (yellow tall), 1/4 rrtt (yellow dwarf). Ratio = 1 red tall : 1 red dwarf : 1 yellow tall : 1 yellow dwarf.

17
[5]E · Experimental Design
Design an experiment to test whether 20 minutes of moderate exercise increases tidal volume in healthy subjects. State the IV, DV, controlled variables, and how you would analyse the data.
Answer Key

IV: exercise (20 min moderate exercise on a stationary bike) vs control (20 min rest). DV: tidal volume (mL per breath) measured with a spirometer immediately after the treatment. Controlled variables: age, sex, fitness level (matched), time of day, hydration, food intake, temperature, equipment. Use a within-subjects (paired) design with ≥ 15 subjects, randomising the order of treatments across days. Analyse with a paired t-test. Predicted result: tidal volume is greater immediately after exercise (~700–1000 mL vs ~500 mL at rest) due to elevated CO2 driving deeper breathing.

18
[5]F · Transpiration
A potometer measures water uptake by a leafy shoot. Predict and explain how each of the following affects transpiration rate: (a) high humidity, (b) wind, (c) high light intensity, (d) high temperature.
Answer Key

(a) High humidity → slower transpiration because the water-vapour gradient between leaf and air is smaller. (b) Wind → faster because moving air removes the boundary layer of saturated air at the leaf surface, steepening the gradient. (c) High light → faster because light stimulates stomata to open (for CO2 uptake), allowing more water vapour to exit. (d) High temperature → faster up to a point: heat increases evaporation rate and lowers ambient humidity; extreme heat may close stomata via ABA, reducing transpiration. Healthy plants balance gas exchange against water loss using guard-cell regulation.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
19
[5]B
Explain how molecular evidence (DNA and rRNA sequence comparison) refines the classification of living organisms. Give one specific example.
Answer Key

All organisms share the same genetic code and many of the same gene families, so DNA and rRNA sequences can be compared. The more two sequences differ, the longer the two lineages have been evolving separately. This allows objective, quantitative classification independent of morphology. Example: Woese's 1977 rRNA analysis showed that "prokaryotes" actually comprise two genetically distinct domains (Bacteria, Archaea) that diverged billions of years ago — a result the older five-kingdom system masked. Another example: panda phylogeny was resolved as bears (Ursidae) by DNA comparison, ending a century of debate based on morphology.

20
[5]C
Compare and contrast directional, stabilising, and disruptive selection. Give one real-world example of each, and describe how each affects the trait distribution over time.
Answer Key

Directional: one extreme is favoured; mean shifts in that direction; variance unchanged. Example: peppered moth darkening during the British Industrial Revolution. Stabilising: the average is favoured; extremes selected against; variance shrinks. Example: human birth weight (too small or too large reduces survival). Disruptive: both extremes favoured at the expense of the average; variance grows; can split a population. Example: African black-bellied seed-cracker finch with two distinct beak sizes specialised for different seed types.

21
[5]D
Describe meiosis I and meiosis II using proper terminology (homologous chromosomes, sister chromatids, crossing over, independent assortment, haploid, diploid). State two key differences from mitosis.
Answer Key

After DNA replication, the diploid (2n) cell enters meiosis. Meiosis I: prophase I — homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) and crossing over occurs at chiasmata; metaphase I — homolog pairs align at the equator, with random orientation (independent assortment); anaphase I — homologs separate to opposite poles (sister chromatids stay together). End of meiosis I: two haploid cells, each chromosome with 2 sister chromatids. Meiosis II: like mitosis on a haploid cell; sister chromatids separate in anaphase II → four haploid daughter cells, each with 1 chromatid per chromosome. Differences from mitosis: (1) two divisions vs one; (2) four genetically unique haploid daughters vs two identical diploid daughters; (3) crossing over and homolog pairing occur in meiosis only.

22
[5]E
Describe in your own words how the structure of the alveolar–capillary interface is optimally suited for gas exchange. Include at least three structural features and explain each.
Answer Key

(1) Thin walls: alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium are each one cell thick; total diffusion distance ~0.5 µm — gases cross by simple diffusion in a fraction of a second. (2) Large surface area: ~300 million alveoli per lung → ~70 m² of surface, the size of a tennis court — vast capacity for O2/CO2 exchange. (3) Moist surfaces: gases dissolve in the thin film of fluid before diffusing. (4) Rich capillary network: each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries, ensuring continuous blood flow past the diffusion surface. (5) Surfactant: Type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant that lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse. (6) Steep partial-pressure gradients: ventilation continuously refreshes air with high O2 and low CO2, maintaining gradients.

23
[5]F
Explain the cohesion-tension theory of water transport in xylem. Include transpiration, the cohesion and adhesion of water molecules, and what happens at the root and leaf ends of the xylem.
Answer Key

At the leaves, water evaporates from mesophyll cells into the substomatal air space and exits through open stomata — transpiration. Each water molecule lost pulls the next molecule behind it because water molecules H-bond to each other (cohesion). The water column inside the xylem is therefore under tension. Adhesion (water molecules sticking to cellulose walls) keeps the column from breaking against the conduit. At the roots, this tension reaches down to the soil-root interface; water enters the root epidermis (especially via root hairs), passes the endodermis (with the Casparian strip selecting which substances continue), and joins the xylem. Mineral ions and sucrose travel along with the water. The system requires no pump — transpiration at the top is the energy source.

Part D — Application [30 marks]
24
[5]B · STSE
Invasive species in Ontario: The emerald ash borer has devastated Ontario's ash trees since the early 2000s. (a) Identify the HIPPO category. (b) Outline two ecological consequences. (c) Propose two management strategies.
Answer Key

(a) Invasive species (I). (b) Consequences: virtually 100% mortality of untreated ash → loss of mature trees; cascading loss of habitat for ash-specialist invertebrates and the songbirds that eat them; gaps in forest canopy invite weedy or invasive understory species. (c) Strategies: chemical treatment (TreeAzin injection) for high-value urban ash; biological control (parasitoid wasps Tetrastichus planipennisi); strict firewood quarantines; rapid removal and disposal of infested trees; long-term replanting with diverse native species and breeding of resistant ash.

25
[5]C · Application
Antibiotic resistance: A bacterial infection that was once curable now resists common antibiotics. Use the four conditions of natural selection to explain how this happened, and propose one strategy to slow further resistance.
Answer Key

(1) Variation: a small number of bacterial cells in the original population already carry resistance alleles by chance mutation. (2) Heritability: resistance alleles are genetic and transmitted to daughter cells when bacteria divide; plasmids can also spread resistance horizontally. (3) Differential survival: in the presence of antibiotic, susceptible bacteria die; resistant bacteria survive. (4) Overproduction: bacteria divide every ~20 minutes, so the surviving resistant population rapidly multiplies. Over generations, the resistance allele rises to high frequency. Mitigation: antibiotic stewardship (prescribe only when needed, choose narrow-spectrum drugs, finish prescribed courses, restrict agricultural use), develop new antibiotics, improve infection control in hospitals, and use combination therapy.

26
[5]D · STSE
Genetic testing: Discuss two benefits and two ethical concerns of direct-to-consumer DNA tests (e.g., 23andMe, AncestryDNA) that report disease-risk alleles.
Answer Key

Benefits: (1) Personal awareness of inherited disease risk (e.g., BRCA1/2 for breast/ovarian cancer, APOE4 for Alzheimer risk) enables proactive screening and lifestyle change. (2) Genetic counselling for family planning when both partners are carriers of a recessive disorder. Concerns: (1) Privacy — DNA contains sensitive information about disease risk, ancestry, and even unidentified relatives; data may be sold to insurers, employers, or used by law enforcement (Golden State Killer case). (2) Limited clinical context — risk reports can frighten or falsely reassure without proper interpretation by a counsellor or physician; many variants have small effect sizes; ethnic biases in reference databases reduce accuracy for non-European users. (3) Family secrets — unexpected paternity or sibling discoveries can devastate relationships. (4) Insurance discrimination — Canada's Genetic Non-Discrimination Act provides some protection, but loopholes remain.

27
[5]E · Case study
Smoking and lung health: Long-term smoking damages the respiratory tract. (a) Identify two structures damaged and their consequences. (b) Explain why a 65-year-old long-term smoker might develop chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
Answer Key

(a) (1) Cilia: paralysed and destroyed → mucus and trapped particles accumulate → chronic bronchitis, persistent cough, increased lung infections. (2) Alveolar walls: gradually destroyed (emphysema) → reduced gas-exchange surface, hypoxia, hypercapnia, characteristic barrel chest from air-trapping. (b) Decades of smoking cause both bronchitic (mucus, inflammation) and emphysematous (alveolar destruction) changes — together known as COPD. Loss of elastic recoil traps air; small airways collapse on exhalation; gas exchange becomes inadequate. Patients are short of breath, exercise-intolerant, dependent on supplemental oxygen, and at high risk of respiratory failure. The damage is largely irreversible. Smoking cessation slows further decline, however late.

28
[5]F · STSE
Sustainable agriculture: Compare modern monoculture farming with a diversified system (crop rotation, cover crops, polyculture). Identify two ecological and one economic trade-off.
Answer Key

Modern monoculture maximises short-term yield of a single high-value crop but increases vulnerability to pests/diseases, requires heavy fertilizer and pesticide inputs, accelerates soil erosion and biodiversity loss, and produces nutrient run-off (eutrophication). Diversified systems include: crop rotation (e.g., corn-soybean-wheat) replenishes soil nitrogen (legumes fix N), breaks pest cycles, improves soil structure; cover crops protect soil between harvests and add organic matter; polyculture mimics natural communities and increases biodiversity. Trade-offs: lower per-acre yield of any single product but higher overall productivity per hectare; reduced reliance on chemical inputs but more management complexity; reduced economic risk through diversification but more labour and learning required during transition. Economic: short-term margins may dip during transition; long-term costs and risk fall. Government incentives and crop insurance can support farmers through transitions.

[5]Synthesis
Synthesis question — Climate change and biology: Climate change affects organisms across all five strands of SBI3U. Choose any THREE strands (Diversity, Evolution, Genetics, Animals, Plants) and explain ONE specific consequence of warming for each.
Answer Key

(Strong answers will choose 3 from below.) Diversity (B): species ranges shift poleward and upward; some endemic alpine species lose habitat entirely; coral bleaching collapses reef biodiversity. Evolution (C): rapid climate change applies new directional selection on traits like flowering time, migration timing, heat tolerance; some populations adapt but many cannot evolve fast enough; range shifts split or merge populations, affecting speciation. Genetics (D): warming may increase mutation rate in some species; reduced effective population sizes increase drift; loss of genetic diversity in endangered species. Animals (E): heat stress disrupts homeostasis (heat stroke, dehydration); altered disease vectors (Lyme disease ticks moving north in Canada); migratory species mismatched with food availability. Plants (F): shifted growing seasons, drought-stress, transpiration up; CO2 fertilisation boosts growth in some crops but heat caps yields; agricultural zones shift; northern boreal forests pressured.
