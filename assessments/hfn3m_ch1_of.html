Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [14 marks]
1
[3]
List the six classes of nutrients. For each, state whether it is a macronutrient, micronutrient, or neither, and whether it provides kilocalories.
Answer Key

Carbohydrates — macro, 4 kcal/g. Lipids — macro, 9 kcal/g. Proteins — macro, 4 kcal/g. Vitamins — micro, 0 kcal/g. Minerals — micro, 0 kcal/g. Water — neither, 0 kcal/g.

2
[2]
Which is the energy value of fat?
Answer Key

C — 9 kcal/g. Fat is the most energy-dense macronutrient because its highly reduced C–H bonds yield more energy when oxidised.

3
[3]
List the nine essential amino acids in adults. Why are they considered essential?
Answer Key

Histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine. They are essential because the body cannot synthesise them in adequate amounts and they must be obtained from food.

4
[3]
Distinguish between fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins. Give an example of each and one consequence of excessive intake.
Answer Key

Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) — dissolved in dietary fat, stored in liver/adipose; toxicity possible (e.g., vitamin-A hypervitaminosis causes liver damage and skin changes). Water-soluble (B-complex, C) — limited storage, excess excreted in urine, daily intake required; toxicity from food is rare although megadose vitamin C can cause diarrhoea.

5
[3]
Define dietary fibre. Distinguish between soluble and insoluble fibre and give one source of each.
Answer Key

Dietary fibre = plant carbohydrate humans cannot digest. Soluble fibre dissolves in water and forms a gel — lowers LDL cholesterol, slows glucose absorption; sources: oats, beans, apples, psyllium. Insoluble fibre adds bulk — accelerates transit, reduces colorectal-cancer risk; sources: wheat bran, vegetables, nuts.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[4]
A breakfast cereal lists 35 g carbohydrate, 4 g protein, 5 g fat per 40 g serving. Calculate total kilocalories per serving and the percentage of energy from each macronutrient. Comment on whether the energy distribution meets the AMDR.
Answer Key

Carb: 35 × 4 = 140 kcal. Protein: 4 × 4 = 16 kcal. Fat: 5 × 9 = 45 kcal. Total = 201 kcal. %carb = 140/201 ≈ 70%; %protein ≈ 8%; %fat ≈ 22%. AMDR: carb 45–65%, protein 10–35%, fat 20–35%. Carbohydrate is above the upper end (70% vs 65%); protein is below the lower end (8% vs 10%); fat is in range. The cereal is somewhat carbohydrate-heavy and could be balanced with a higher-protein topping (yogurt, milk, or nuts).

7
[4]
Design an experiment to test whether iron absorption is improved when an iron-fortified breakfast cereal is paired with orange juice rather than with tea. Identify variables, controls, and how you would measure absorption.
Answer Key

IV: beverage (water control, orange juice, black tea). DV: serum iron 2–3 h after the meal, or longitudinal change in serum ferritin over 4–6 weeks. Controlled: cereal type, serving size, fasting state, time of measurement, body weight. Predict: vitamin C in OJ should increase non-heme iron absorption (~ 2–4×); tannins in tea should decrease it. Replicate ≥3 per condition, ideally in a randomised cross-over design within the same subjects.

8
[4]
A 60 kg vegan student wants to know whether her diet provides adequate protein. She eats 1 cup lentils (18 g protein), 250 mL fortified soy milk (8 g), 30 g almonds (6 g), and 1 cup tofu (20 g). Calculate her intake and compare with her RDA.
Answer Key

RDA = 0.8 × 60 = 48 g/day. Intake = 18 + 8 + 6 + 20 = 52 g/day. She exceeds her RDA by 4 g (≈ 8%). Her amino-acid profile is also complete: tofu/soy is a complete protein, and lentils + almonds complement any limiting amino acids in plant grains she eats elsewhere.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[4]
Explain the difference between saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated, and trans fatty acids. Reference structure, food source, and health effect.
Answer Key

Saturated — no C=C double bonds; straight tails pack tightly, solid at room temperature (butter, beef tallow); excess raises LDL cholesterol. Monounsaturated — one C=C kink; liquid (olive oil); lowers LDL when replacing saturated. Polyunsaturated — multiple C=C (sunflower oil, fish oil); essential omega-3 and omega-6 are in this group; lowers LDL. Trans — straight chains from industrial hydrogenation; mimic saturated packing but uniquely raise LDL and lower HDL; banned in Canadian food production since 2018.

10
[4]
Construct a one-page nutrition pamphlet (text only) titled "Five Foods That Provide Plant Calcium" for a teen audience. Include calcium quantity per serving and a short explanation of why teens need 1 300 mg/day.
Answer Key

1. Fortified soy milk (250 mL = 300 mg). 2. Calcium-set tofu (½ cup = 250 mg). 3. Cooked kale (1 cup = 180 mg). 4. White beans (1 cup = 160 mg). 5. Almonds (30 g = 75 mg) or fortified orange juice (250 mL = 300 mg). Teens need 1 300 mg/day because adolescence is the peak window for building bone mass; bones gain calcium fastest at 12–18 years. Bone mass set during adolescence largely determines adult bone density and fracture risk later in life.

11
[4]
Compare and contrast macronutrients and micronutrients in a clearly labelled 3-row table. Include: examples, role, daily quantity needed.
Answer Key

Macronutrients: carbohydrate, lipid, protein. Role: energy supply (4-4-9 kcal/g) and structural building blocks. Daily quantity: tens to hundreds of grams. Micronutrients: vitamins (13) and minerals (~16). Role: enable enzyme action, structure (Ca in bones), and signalling; do not supply kilocalories. Daily quantity: milligrams (most minerals, vitamin C, niacin) to micrograms (B12, folate, vitamin D, iodine).

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[4]
Case Study — A teenager's lunchbox. Tariq packs: a white-bread peanut-butter sandwich, an apple, 500 mL juice. He never eats fish, dairy, or vegetables. Evaluate the nutritional adequacy and suggest three specific improvements.
Answer Key

Strengths: peanut butter provides protein and unsaturated fat; apple provides fibre and vitamin C. Weaknesses: white bread (refined), 500 mL juice (high in sugar, no fibre), no vegetables, missing dairy/fortified-plant calcium and vitamin D, no source of omega-3, low fibre. Improvements: (1) swap white bread for whole-grain; (2) replace half the juice with water and add raw carrots/peppers; (3) add 250 mL fortified soy milk or yogurt for calcium and vitamin D; (4) consider canned tuna or hemp seeds for omega-3.

13
[4]
Case Study — Iron-deficiency anaemia. A 16-year-old menstruating teen reports tiredness; bloodwork shows low haemoglobin. Explain why menstruating teens are at risk for iron-deficiency anaemia and recommend a one-day meal plan (no supplements) that meets her 15 mg iron RDA. Pair iron-rich foods with vitamin C to maximise absorption.
Answer Key

Menstrual losses (~1 mg iron/day average) compound rapid teen growth needs, so the RDA rises to 15 mg. Sample day: Breakfast — iron-fortified oatmeal (3.5 mg) with strawberries (60 mg vitamin C). Snack — orange + 30 g pumpkin seeds (2 mg). Lunch — lentil & spinach soup (6 mg) with whole-grain bread; tomato salad (vitamin C). Dinner — 90 g lean beef (3 mg heme) with broccoli (60 mg vitamin C) and brown rice. Approximate total: ~15 mg iron with vitamin C pairing — meets RDA.

14
[4]
STSE — The 2018 trans-fat ban. Explain (i) why partial hydrogenation was used industrially; (ii) the health argument for the 2018 Canadian ban; (iii) the limits of the ban (what trans fat remains).
Answer Key

(i) Partial hydrogenation converts liquid vegetable oil to a solid spread by adding H across some double bonds; produces trans configurations that pack tightly. Industry valued long shelf life, low cost, and spreadable texture for margarine, baked goods, deep-fryer oils. (ii) Trans fats uniquely raise LDL and lower HDL — far worse than saturated fat for cardiovascular disease. Health Canada modelled prevented heart-attack and stroke deaths in the hundreds per year; the ban took effect 17 September 2018, prohibiting partially hydrogenated oils in food sold in Canada. (iii) Natural ruminant trans fats remain in beef and dairy (small amounts, less harmful in the quantities typically consumed). The ban applies to manufactured foods in Canada only; imported foods must also comply.
