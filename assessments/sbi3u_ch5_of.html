Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Name the three plant tissue systems and state one function of each.
Answer Key

(1) Dermal: outer covering — protection, prevention of water loss (cuticle), gas exchange through stomata, secretion of trichomes. (2) Ground: bulk of the plant — photosynthesis (parenchyma), flexible support (collenchyma), rigid support (sclerenchyma), storage. (3) Vascular: transport — xylem (water + minerals upward) and phloem (sugars source-to-sink).

2
[2]
Which is the principal cell type of secondary xylem ("wood")?
Answer Key

C. Wood is secondary xylem made mostly of tracheids and vessel elements (and some xylem fibres/parenchyma). Sieve-tube elements and companion cells are in phloem.

3
[3]
List two differences between monocots and eudicots, choosing from: leaf venation, flower part number, vascular bundle arrangement, cotyledon number.
Answer Key

(1) Leaf venation: monocots — parallel; eudicots — netted (reticulate). (2) Flower parts: monocots — multiples of 3; eudicots — multiples of 4 or 5. (3) Vascular bundles: monocots — scattered; eudicots — arranged in a ring. (4) Cotyledons: monocots — 1; eudicots — 2. (Any two.)

4
[3]
Describe the cohesion-tension theory of water transport in xylem. Identify (a) the driving force, (b) the property of water that allows the column to remain continuous, (c) the cell type in xylem that conducts water.
Answer Key

(a) Driving force: transpiration at the leaves — water evaporates from mesophyll cells and exits stomata, creating tension that pulls water up. (b) Water has strong cohesion (H-bonds between water molecules) and adhesion (H-bonds between water and cell-wall cellulose) — these keep the column intact and attached to xylem walls under tension. (c) Tracheids and vessel elements conduct water; both are dead at maturity, so they offer no cytoplasmic resistance.

5
[2]
Which plant hormone is most closely associated with stomatal closure during drought?
Answer Key

C (ABA). ABA is the "stress hormone" — under drought, ABA accumulates and triggers guard-cell solute efflux, closing the stomata and reducing water loss.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[4]
A potometer is used to measure water uptake by a leafy shoot. Design an experiment to compare transpiration rates at three different humidities (20%, 50%, 80%). State the variables, replicates, and the predicted pattern.
Answer Key

IV: relative humidity (20%, 50%, 80% — achieved with a sealed chamber and a humidifier or dry desiccant). DV: water uptake rate (mm or mL per minute, recorded as the meniscus moves along the potometer's capillary). Replicates: at least 3 shoots per humidity, and at least 3 trials per shoot. Controlled variables: temperature, light intensity, air movement (no wind), leaf area, time of day. Predicted pattern: transpiration rate is highest at 20% humidity (steep gradient drives water vapour out) and lowest at 80% humidity (shallow gradient). Plot rate vs humidity; expect a negative correlation.

7
[4]
A tree stump shows wider growth rings during one decade than in the surrounding decades. Suggest two possible biological or environmental reasons and explain how dendrochronology can be used to reconstruct climate.
Answer Key

Possible reasons for wider rings: (1) more favourable growing conditions — adequate rainfall, longer growing season, warmer temperatures; (2) reduced competition (a neighbouring tree fell, freeing light and nutrients); (3) fewer pests or pathogens during that decade; (4) increased atmospheric CO2 in modern times accelerates growth. Dendrochronology reconstructs climate by collecting long ring-width time series from many trees of overlapping ages (cross-dated by matching distinctive rings). Where wider rings correlate with weather records, those rings record warmer/wetter years; the same correlation lets researchers infer climate centuries before instrumental records (e.g., the Medieval Warm Period and Little Ice Age have been confirmed by tree-ring data).

8
[4]
In a controlled experiment, a plant is grown horizontally (the stem laid sideways). After several days, the shoot tip curves upward while the roots curve downward. (a) Name the response. (b) Identify the hormone responsible and describe how it redistributes in each organ. (c) Why do shoots and roots respond differently to the same hormone?
Answer Key

(a) Gravitropism — shoots show negative gravitropism (away from gravity), roots show positive gravitropism (toward gravity). (b) Auxin. In a horizontal shoot, auxin accumulates on the lower side, elongating those cells more and curving the shoot upward. In a horizontal root, auxin also accumulates on the lower side, but in roots high auxin inhibits cell elongation, so the upper side elongates more and the root bends downward. The sensing mechanism uses statoliths (dense starch-filled organelles) that sediment in cells of the root cap. (c) Roots and shoots have different sensitivities to auxin: shoots elongate fastest at concentrations that already inhibit root elongation — so the same gradient produces opposite curvatures.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[4]
Describe (with optional diagram) the structure of a typical flower. Include sepals, petals, stamens (filament + anther), and the carpel (stigma + style + ovary). State the role of each part.
Answer Key

A typical flower consists of four whorls of modified leaves. Sepals (calyx): outermost; protect the bud. Petals (corolla): often coloured/scented; attract pollinators. Stamens (male reproductive parts): each has a filament topped by an anther where pollen (male gametophyte) is produced. Carpel/pistil (female): the stigma is the sticky upper surface that receives pollen; the style connects to the ovary below; the ovary contains one or more ovules; each ovule contains the embryo sac (female gametophyte) with the egg and polar nuclei. After pollination, fertilization within the ovule produces a seed; the surrounding ovary develops into a fruit.

10
[5]
Explain the pressure-flow model of phloem transport. Use the terms source, sink, sieve-tube element, companion cell, active loading, osmosis, and pressure gradient.
Answer Key

At the source (e.g., a leaf actively photosynthesising), companion cells actively load sucrose into the adjacent sieve-tube elements using ATP. The high solute concentration draws water from neighbouring xylem into the phloem by osmosis, raising hydrostatic pressure. At the sink (e.g., a growing fruit or root), sucrose is actively unloaded into surrounding cells for use or storage; water exits the sieve tubes back into nearby xylem; pressure falls. The resulting pressure gradient (high at source, low at sink) drives bulk flow of phloem sap from source to sink. Because sources and sinks change with the season — leaves load to roots in summer; roots release to buds in spring — phloem transport is bidirectional over time, unlike xylem transport (always up).

11
[4]
Compare and contrast sexual and asexual (vegetative) reproduction in plants. Include one example of each, and one advantage and one disadvantage of asexual reproduction.
Answer Key

Sexual reproduction: gametes from two parents combine to form genetically unique seeds (e.g., apple from cross-pollinated flower). Offspring are genetically diverse. Asexual / vegetative reproduction: offspring are clones of a single parent — strawberry runners (stolons), potato tubers, garlic bulbs, ginger rhizomes, rooted cuttings. Advantage of asexual: fast, predictable, preserves a successful genotype, no need for a mate or pollinator. Disadvantage: no genetic variation, so populations are vulnerable to new pests or pathogens (Irish potato famine, banana fungal disease). Most plants use both modes depending on conditions.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[4]
Application — Cacti and water: Desert cacti show characteristic adaptations for water conservation. Identify two such adaptations and explain how each reduces water loss without preventing photosynthesis altogether.
Answer Key

(1) Thick waxy cuticle: minimises evaporation from epidermis. (2) Stomata closed during the day, open at night (CAM metabolism): CO2 taken in cool, humid nights and stored as malate; photosynthesis uses stored CO2 during the day with stomata closed → drastically reduced water loss for a given carbon gain. (3) Reduced leaves (often spines): minimises surface area for transpiration; spines also deter herbivores and shade the stem. (4) Photosynthesis in fleshy stems: green stem replaces leaf as photosynthetic organ. (5) Shallow extensive roots: rapid absorption from light rains.

13
[4]
STSE — Crop rotation: A farmer alternates corn (a grass, heavy nitrogen user) with soybeans (a legume, nitrogen fixer) over successive years. Explain (a) the biological basis for this rotation and (b) two practical benefits.
Answer Key

(a) Soybeans (and other legumes) form symbiotic root nodules with nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium bacteria. These bacteria convert atmospheric N2 (unusable by plants) into NH3/NH4+ (usable), enriching soil nitrogen for the next crop. Corn, in contrast, depletes nitrogen rapidly. Alternating the two replenishes nitrogen between corn crops. (b) Practical benefits: (1) reduced synthetic fertilizer needs — lower input cost and reduced run-off pollution; (2) interrupts pest and disease life cycles — pests adapted to corn cannot persist on soybeans, and vice versa, lowering pesticide need; (3) different root architecture improves soil structure and reduces erosion.

14
[4]
STSE — GM crops: Bt corn carries a bacterial gene that produces a toxin lethal to certain caterpillar pests. List one ecological benefit and one ecological concern for large-scale Bt corn deployment.
Answer Key

Benefit: reduced insecticide spraying because the corn produces its own pest-specific toxin → less off-target killing of pollinators and beneficial insects; lower pesticide exposure for farm workers; reduced agricultural run-off into waterways. Concern: insect populations can evolve resistance (directional selection on Bt-resistance alleles), making the toxin less effective over time; refuges of non-Bt corn must be planted to slow resistance. Other concerns: gene flow to wild relatives; potential effects on non-target Lepidoptera that consume corn pollen (e.g., monarch butterfly debate of the early 2000s — careful studies showed real-world risk is low but non-zero); market and patent issues for farmers.
