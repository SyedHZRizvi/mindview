Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Define each: (a) emerging adulthood (Arnett); (b) signature strengths (VIA); (c) the dual continua model (Keyes).
Answer Key

(a) Emerging adulthood (Arnett, 2000) — a life-stage roughly 18 to 29 characterised by identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and a sense of possibilities. (b) Signature strengths (VIA) — the top five of 24 character strengths from the Peterson and Seligman framework, deliberate use of which predicts higher reported well-being. (c) Dual continua model (Keyes, 2002) — mental health and mental illness as two distinct dimensions rather than opposite ends of one scale; you can be flourishing with a managed diagnosis or languishing without one.

2
[2]
Erikson's "intimacy vs isolation" stage is the developmental task of:
Answer Key

B. Intimacy vs isolation is Erikson's sixth stage — early adulthood — following identity vs role-confusion. Failure to form committed relationships, in Erikson's frame, leads to isolation.

3
[2]
In 2–3 sentences, distinguish terminal from instrumental values (Rokeach).
Answer Key

Terminal values are desired end-states of existence — e.g., a comfortable life, family security, freedom, inner harmony, true friendship, wisdom. Instrumental values are preferred modes of conduct that get you to those end-states — e.g., honesty, courage, responsibility, ambition. Rokeach's original survey listed eighteen of each; they form complementary but distinct categories.

4
[3]
Define each: (a) SMART goals; (b) implementation intentions (Gollwitzer); (c) flexible explanatory style (Seligman).
Answer Key

(a) SMART goals — Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound. Best fitted to 1-year horizons; loose-SMART at 5 years; description-of-future-state at 10 years. (b) Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999) — if-then plans that pre-decide a specific response in a specific situation (e.g., "If it is 9 a.m. Monday, then I will be at my desk reading Chapter 4"); research shows roughly doubled follow-through. (c) Flexible explanatory style (Seligman) — the capacity to distinguish controllable from uncontrollable factors in real time, neither over-internalising blame nor over-externalising responsibility.

6
[3]
Identify and briefly describe three of Arnett's five features of emerging adulthood.
Answer Key

Any three of: (1) Identity exploration — trying on roles, values, commitments. (2) Instability — frequent changes in residence, partners, jobs. (3) Self-focus — focus on one's own development without the constraints of adolescence or full adult commitment. (4) Feeling in-between — neither fully adolescent nor fully adult. (5) A sense of possibilities — wide-open expectations of future, including unrealistic optimism. Marks: 1 each.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
7
[5]
"Articulated values are what you tell yourself you care about. Revealed values are what your time and money say you care about."
Analyse the implication of this distinction for a graduating senior planning the year after OSSD. In 5–6 sentences, explain why the gap matters and describe the practical exercise that surfaces it.
Answer Key

A strong answer notes: (1) the articulated value list is often shaped by social desirability and inherited family scripts; (2) the revealed pattern is empirical — what your time and money allocate to over a representative week or month; (3) the gap is the work — either revise the value list (entertainment really is high) or revise the allocation; (4) for a graduating senior, the gap matters because every post-OSSD choice (program, city, partner, work) implicitly bets on values; without articulating-vs-revealed clarity, you make those bets on someone else's values; (5) the practical exercise: track all dollars and waking hours for a week, then categorise against the value list you would write. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 explanation, 1 exercise description, 1 connection to transition decisions.

8
[5]
Apply locus-of-control and resilience constructs: Maya, a first-year university student, receives a 52% on her first major essay. Identify the over-internal, over-external, and flexible responses she could have. In 5–6 sentences, explain why the flexible response is most useful.
Answer Key

Over-external: "The professor hates me; the topic was unfair; you can't succeed here." Outcome: anger, no improvement plan, motivation drop. Over-internal: "I'm not smart enough; I don't belong here." Outcome: shame, possible withdrawal, no improvement plan. Flexible: "Some of this is on me (started late, no office hours, no peer reader); some is the course (first-year writing has a steeper learning curve); some I can't control (topic outside my interest). What can I change for next time? Start earlier, attend office hours, find a peer reader, use the writing centre." The flexible response combines locus-of-control flexibility, resilience (recovery rather than breakdown), and growth-mindset (difficulty as information). Marks: 1 over-external, 1 over-internal, 2 flexible, 1 synthesis.

9
[5]
Critique the claim: "Emerging adulthood (Arnett's concept) describes the universal life-experience of all 18–29-year-olds in Canada today." In 5–6 sentences, identify two valid concerns about the universality claim.
Answer Key

Valid concerns include: (1) Class and economic constraint: a year of exploration funded by family looks very different from a year of exploration paying rent on minimum wage; the stage is more available to economically privileged young people. (2) Geography: rural and remote young Canadians often face different timelines because economic opportunities and partner pools are more constrained. (3) Indigenous, immigrant, and refugee young adults may follow paths shaped by family obligation, immigration status, or community responsibility that do not match the Arnett pattern. (4) Young parents and early entrants to skilled trades may move into adult commitments at 18–22 in ways the model under-describes. (5) The original Arnett research drew heavily on middle-class American samples. The honest claim is that emerging adulthood describes one common path now, not the universal path. Marks: 1 per valid concern, plus 1 for nuanced synthesis.

Part C: Communication [15 marks]
10
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) responding to the claim: "Goal-setting at 18 should be done in three horizons — 1-year, 5-year, 10-year — rather than as a single set of goals." Use specific evidence from the unit; embed at least one named framework or researcher.
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) a clear thesis (agree, disagree, or qualify); (2) specific reference to SMART (Doran 1981) or implementation intentions (Gollwitzer 1999) or three-horizon framework; (3) explanation of why SMART works at 1-year but over-specifies at 10-year; (4) consistency-check argument (each level rolls up to the next); (5) acknowledgment of revision and quarterly review; (6) closing claim about the relationship between document and practice. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

11
[5]
Write a coherent 6–8 sentence answer describing the four-part self-advocacy script for asking for mental-health help. Use a concrete sample situation (a first-year student noticing two weeks of sleep difficulty) to illustrate.
Answer Key

The four parts: (1) Specific observable symptom — e.g., "I've been waking at 4 a.m. with anxious thoughts." (2) Duration — "for the past two weeks." (3) Impact — "my schoolwork is suffering; I missed two of six lectures last week." (4) Specific ask — "I would like to talk to someone." A sample assembled script: "Hi, I'm a first-year student. For about two weeks I've had difficulty sleeping (waking at 3 or 4 a.m. with anxious thoughts) and I've missed two of my last six lectures. School isn't going as well as I'd like. I'd like to book a counselling appointment." Marks: 1 per part (4); 1 for the assembled coherent example.

[4]
Write a 4–5-sentence personal reflection on one signature strength you identified through this unit. Pair it with one specific concrete moment from your past four years when you used it well.
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) a specific named strength (from VIA, CliftonStrengths, or a self-identified strength with rationale); (2) a concrete situational anecdote with date, place, and action; (3) reflection on what the strength produced; (4) implication for post-OSSD year. The strength-without-evidence response should not earn full marks. Marks: 1 named strength, 2 evidence, 1 reflection.

Part D: Application [15 marks]
13
[8]
Apply the three-horizon goal framework to your own post-OSSD path. Produce:
(a) a 10-year vision (3–4 sentences describing a future state at age 28) — 2 marks
(b) a 5-year goal (a paragraph of loose-SMART milestones for age 23) — 2 marks
(c) a 1-year goal (a SMART specification for August 2027) — 2 marks
(d) a one-sentence demonstration that each level rolls up to the next — 2 marks
Marking Rubric

(a) Look for: concreteness without over-specification; flexible-enough wording to survive uncertainty; reference to work, location, relationships, and at least one quantified marker. (b) Look for: 4–6 milestones that are testable; should bridge 1-year and 10-year. (c) Look for: strict-SMART (specific outcome, measurable target, attainable, relevant, time-bound); should be testable monthly or quarterly. (d) Look for: explicit demonstration of consistency — the 1-year goal serves the 5-year, which serves the 10-year. Marks as above.

14
[7]
Application — Personal Synthesis: Write a 6–8-sentence reflection that integrates at least three Unit 1 concepts (e.g., values, signature strengths, locus of control, growth mindset, mental-health literacy) to articulate one specific behaviour you will change in the first month after OSSD ends. Be specific about the behaviour, the rationale (linking back to concepts), and the planned implementation intention.
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) at least three Unit 1 concepts named correctly; (2) a specific behaviour identified (not "I'll try harder"); (3) a clear conceptual rationale linking values/strengths/growth-mindset/locus to the behaviour; (4) one explicit if-then implementation intention; (5) a sentence acknowledging the difference between intention and follow-through (e.g., quarterly review, accountability partner). Strong responses move the abstract framework into specific lived practice. Marks: 1 per concept named correctly (3), 1 for specific behaviour, 1 for implementation intention, 1 for review-and-follow-through, 1 for communication conventions.
