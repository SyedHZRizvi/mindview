Topic 4.1–4.2 — Budget and Credit
Question 1
The 50/30/20 budget rule allocates:
Solution: The Warren/Tyagi 50/30/20 rule (All Your Worth, 2005): 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings/debt repayment beyond minimums. The percentages are starting points; high-cost cities bend the needs share upward.
Question 2
As of October 2024, Ontario's general minimum wage is:
Solution: Ontario's minimum wage rose to $17.20/hour on 1 October 2024 and indexes each October. A 40-hour week at minimum is roughly \(\$17.20 \times 40 = \$688\) gross; about $30,000 net per year.
Question 3
"$5,000 carried on a credit card at 20% APR for 5 years, paying $200/month, costs the borrower about $4,500 in interest before the balance reaches zero."
The biggest single lever for an 18-year-old building a credit score (300–900 Canadian range) is:
Solution: Payment history accounts for ~35% of the score; credit utilisation ~30%; length of history ~15%; new credit ~10%; mix ~10%. A single 30+ day missed payment can drop a 750 score by 80–110 points and stays on file for six years.
Question 4
As of April 2023, federal Canada Student Loans (the federal portion of OSAP):
Solution: The federal government eliminated interest on Canada Student Loans permanently as of April 2023. Ontario's provincial portion still accrues interest at the prime rate after the 6-month grace period.
Topic 4.3–4.4 — Renting and Insurance
Question 5
Under Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act 2006, a landlord may collect as a deposit:
Solution: The RTA limits residential deposits to one month's rent (as a last-month's-rent deposit). Damage deposits and "key deposits beyond replacement cost" are illegal in Ontario residential tenancies.
Question 6
Ontario's 2025 Rent Increase Guideline (the cap on annual rent increases for units first occupied before 15 November 2018) is:
Solution: The 2025 Rent Increase Guideline is 2.5%. The Ontario government publishes a new figure each summer for the following calendar year. Units first occupied after 15 November 2018 are exempt from the cap.
Question 7
Tenant insurance for an Ontario adult typically costs:
Solution: Tenant insurance is typically $15–$30/month for $30,000 contents + $1M liability. Many Ontario landlords require it as a lease condition. The math favours having it overwhelmingly — a single liability claim from water damage can exceed $10,000.
Question 8
Most 18-year-olds in Ontario do NOT need life insurance because:
Solution: Life insurance covers the income loss to dependents. With no dependents, no need. The product is aggressively marketed to young adults who do not need it; the honest rule is: insure when someone's livelihood depends on you.
Topic 4.5–4.6 — Taxes, Banking, OHIP, Transit
Question 9
The TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account) annual contribution limit for 2026 is:
Solution: TFSA 2026 contribution room is $7,000. Unused room accumulates. Growth and withdrawals are tax-free forever — the most powerful tax shelter for young Canadians.
Question 10
For an 18-year-old in a 0%–20% effective tax bracket, the recommended investing-account hierarchy is:
Solution: TFSA first because contributions made when you owe no tax deliver the full tax-free-growth benefit. RRSP later when you are in a meaningful tax bracket. Employer matching, where offered, is the exception — almost always worth taking.
Question 11
A CAA estimate for the annual cost of owning a small car in Ontario (depreciation + insurance + fuel + maintenance + parking) for a relatively new vehicle driven 15,000 km is approximately:
Solution: CAA estimates a small new car in Ontario at $10,000–$13,000/year all-in. A TTC pass is $1,872/year. The $8,000 annual difference invested at 7% for 40 years grows to about $170,000 — one of the largest single financial decisions for a young adult.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, use compound interest to explain why saving $1,000 at age 18 is much more valuable than saving $1,000 at age 30. Assume a 7% real return.
Sample: Compound interest follows \(A = P(1+r)^n\). $1,000 saved at 18 with 47 years to age 65 at 7% becomes \(\$1{,}000 \times 1.07^{47} \approx \$24{,}000\). $1,000 saved at 30 with 35 years to 65 becomes \(\$1{,}000 \times 1.07^{35} \approx \$10{,}700\). The 12-year head start more than doubles the eventual amount, because the additional twelve compounding years are at the most productive end of the curve.
