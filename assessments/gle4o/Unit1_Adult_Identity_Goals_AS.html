Topic 1.1–1.2 — Emerging Adulthood and Values
Question 1
Jeffrey Arnett's 2000 concept of "emerging adulthood" refers to:
Solution: Arnett's 2000 American Psychologist article identified emerging adulthood as a new life-stage (roughly 18–29) with five features: identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and a sense of possibilities. It complements rather than replaces Erikson.
Question 2
Erikson's stage of "identity vs role-confusion" maps onto:
Solution: Erikson's fifth stage, identity vs role-confusion, is the late-adolescent task. The early-adult task (intimacy vs isolation) follows.
Question 3
"Articulated values are what you tell yourself you care about. Revealed values are what your time and money say you care about."
According to this distinction, the most useful exercise in values clarification is:
Solution: The articulated-vs-revealed values distinction is empirical: comparing the value list to actual time and money allocation surfaces the gap. The gap is the work.
Question 4
Rokeach's distinction between terminal and instrumental values is:
Solution: Terminal values are end-states (e.g., a comfortable life, family security, freedom, wisdom); instrumental values are modes of conduct (e.g., honesty, courage, responsibility) that get you there.
Topic 1.3–1.4 — Strengths and Goal Horizons
Question 5
The VIA Character Strengths Survey identifies:
Solution: VIA (Peterson and Seligman, 2004) is the free 24-strengths-under-6-virtues system. CliftonStrengths is the 34-theme Gallup tool — a separate framework.
Question 6
"Growth areas" are best understood as:
Solution: The growth-area framing invites investment rather than defence. The mature student decides which gaps matter for their chosen path and which to work around — both legitimate choices when made consciously.
Question 7
A 10-year vision is best written as:
Solution: SMART is right for 1-year goals but over-specifies at the 10-year horizon. A description of a future state — concrete enough to picture, flexible enough to revise — does the work.
Question 8
The "consistency check" in three-horizon goal-setting refers to:
Solution: If the 1-year goal does not trace upward to the 5-year and 10-year, the system isn't working. The vertical consistency makes the framework useful.
Topic 1.5–1.6 — Locus of Control and Mental Health
Question 9
Julian Rotter's "locus of control" (1966) is best understood as:
Solution: Locus of control is a continuum, not a dichotomy. The healthy adult position is flexible — internal where things are controllable, external where genuinely not.
Question 10
Corey Keyes's "dual continua model" (2002) of mental health holds that:
Solution: Keyes's two-axis model means building flourishing is everyone's task (regardless of diagnosis status), and managing illness is a separate task when needed.
Question 11
Good2Talk (1-866-925-5454) is:
Solution: Good2Talk is free, 24/7, and specifically for Ontario and Nova Scotia post-secondary students aged 17+. ConnexOntario (1-866-531-2600) covers all ages province-wide.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, describe the components of an effective self-advocacy script for asking for help with a mental-health concern.
Sample: An effective self-advocacy script names a specific observable symptom (e.g., "trouble sleeping for two weeks, waking at 4 a.m."), a duration ("for three weeks"), an impact ("schoolwork is suffering"), and a specific ask ("I would like to talk to someone"). This four-part structure gives a counsellor, doctor, or peer-support worker enough information to act and signals you are paying attention to your own state rather than waiting for crisis.
