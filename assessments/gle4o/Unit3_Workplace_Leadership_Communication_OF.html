Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) SBI model; (b) BATNA (Ury); (c) psychological safety (Edmondson).
Answer Key

(a) SBI — Situation, Behaviour, Impact (Center for Creative Leadership); a structure for specific, non-judgmental feedback; SBI+Q adds a closing Question. (b) BATNA — Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement; pre-negotiation identification of what you do if no deal is reached; gives leverage and exit. (c) Psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) — shared team belief that interpersonal risks (asking a stupid question, admitting a mistake) will not be punished; Google Aristotle Project found it the strongest team-effectiveness predictor.

2
[2]
Heen and Stone (2014) distinguish three feedback types. The category most often misread by the receiver as a rating is:
Answer Key

B. The most common feedback miscommunication is coaching-heard-as-evaluation. The receiver hears a rating; the giver meant growth support. Naming the category at the start of the conversation reduces the mismatch.

3
[2]
In 2–3 sentences, distinguish positions from interests in negotiation (Fisher and Ury).
Answer Key

A position is what someone says they want ("I want a 15% raise"). An interest is the underlying need ("I want to keep up with cost of living and to feel my contributions are recognised"). Positions collide; interests can be reconciled because most negotiations have under-explored options that satisfy interests on both sides.

4
[3]
Define each: (a) emotional granularity; (b) amygdala hijack; (c) Adam Grant's "giver" pattern.
Answer Key

(a) Emotional granularity — using precise emotion vocabulary (frustrated vs angry vs resentful) rather than default-level words (bad, stressed); research shows it reduces reactive behaviour. (b) Amygdala hijack — the limbic system processes emotional information ~0.1s faster than the prefrontal cortex; consequence is that we feel before we think, and responses launched in those 0.1s are often ones we later regret. The discipline is the pause. (c) Giver pattern (Grant, 2013) — people who consistently make small bounded investments in others' success outperform takers and matchers over 5-year career horizons; the network compounds.

5
[2]
Cialdini's six principles of influence include all of the following EXCEPT:
Answer Key

C. Cialdini's six are: reciprocity, commitment-and-consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity. Coercion is not influence; it is force.

6
[3]
In 3–4 sentences, describe two Hofstede cultural dimensions and how they might shape a workplace conversation.
Answer Key

Examples: (1) Power distance — Canada scores low (relative comfort with peer-like exchange with managers); many East Asian and Latin American cultures score higher (greater deference to senior). A Canadian-default-low-power-distance worker may read silence as agreement from a higher-power-distance colleague when it is dissent waiting to be invited. (2) Individualism vs collectivism — Canada scores high individualism; East Asian cultures generally score collectivism. The Canadian-default of personal accomplishment in interviews may read as immodest to a Japanese colleague; the Japanese-default of team-attribution may read as evasive to a Canadian. Awareness reduces misreading. Marks: 1 each dimension, 1 conversation-shaping example.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
7
[5]
"You need to be more proactive." — supervisor's feedback to Marc, six months into his first office job.
Analyse this feedback. In 5–6 sentences, identify: (a) why the feedback is poorly delivered; (b) what Marc could ask to convert it into actionable SBI form; (c) the four-step response Marc should follow.
Answer Key

(a) The feedback is non-SBI: it is a label, not a description of situation, behaviour, and impact. It invites defence. (b) Marc should ask: "Can you give me a specific example of a moment when more proactivity would have helped?" — converting the label to behaviour. (c) Four-step response: (1) understand before defending — listen to the example; (2) separate signal from delivery — the label is poor but the underlying observation may be valid; (3) find the kernel — the 10%+ that is true; (4) decide what to do — "Thanks. I want to think about this. Could we set a follow-up in two weeks where I can show what I've changed?" Marks: 2 delivery critique, 1 conversion question, 2 four-step response.

8
[5]
Apply the Harvard interest-based negotiation method to this roommate dispute: "Aman wants quiet after 10 p.m. for early-morning lab; Diego practises guitar in the evening because his classes run 6–9 p.m." In 5–6 sentences, identify positions, interests, and at least three options that satisfy interests on both sides.
Answer Key

Positions: Aman wants "no guitar after 10"; Diego wants "guitar whenever I want in my own space." Interests: Aman's interest is sleep on a 6 a.m. lab-day schedule; Diego's interest is musical practice and unwinding after evening classes. Options: (1) Diego practises 6:30–8:30 p.m. before Aman's bedtime; (2) Diego uses an unplugged electric guitar (much quieter) after 10; (3) Aman uses earplugs and a white-noise app; (4) Diego books a campus practice room two nights a week; (5) they swap rooms (Diego to the one farther from the bedroom wall). The settlement might be a combination of 1, 2, and 4. The point: six options where the positional fight had two. Marks: 1 positions, 2 interests, 2 options (1 per option, up to 3).

9
[5]
Critique the claim: "Adult workplaces in 2026 prefer 'directness' as the universal communication norm." In 5–6 sentences, identify two valid concerns with the claim, drawing on Hofstede, Erin Meyer, or generational differences.
Answer Key

Valid concerns: (1) Directness is culturally specific — Dutch and German conventions value high-directness; Japanese, Korean, and many Latin-American conventions value indirect signalling that preserves face. The "directness is universal" claim assumes one cultural default. (2) Even within Canada, directness operates differently across generations — Gen Z workers often expect more frequent feedback than Boomers were trained to give. (3) Directness without psychological safety produces defensiveness, not learning. (4) Linda Babcock's gender-and-negotiation research finds women using "assertive" tactics face more pushback than men using the same tactics; "directness" is gendered in its reception. The mature claim is: directness has a place, but the skill is calibrating directness to the audience and culture, not asserting it universally. Marks: 2 each valid concern + 1 synthesis.

Part C: Communication [15 marks]
10
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) responding to the claim: "Leading without authority is the most important leadership skill for someone in the first three years of their career." Use specific evidence; reference at least one named framework (Cialdini, Edmondson, Abbajay, Grant).
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) clear thesis; (2) reference to Cialdini's influence principles or Edmondson's psychological safety or Abbajay's managing-up or Grant's giver pattern; (3) explanation of why first-three-year workers rarely have formal authority; (4) the compounding-credibility argument (reliability + small visible help + low-ego improvement); (5) acknowledgment of the alternative-argument (some roles do reward formal-credential authority); (6) closing claim about the practical implication. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

11
[5]
Write a 4–5-sentence professional email requesting a vacation week off from a supervisor, demonstrating the principles (subject line, ask in sentence 1, register match, polite close, evidence you have done the legwork).
Marking Rubric

Strong response: Subject: "Vacation request — August 11–15" or similar. Body: "Hi [Name], I'd like to take vacation August 11–15 (Mon–Fri). I've checked the team calendar — no client meetings that week and [colleague] has agreed to cover the [task]. Could you let me know by Thursday so I can book? Thanks, [Name]." Marks: 1 subject, 1 ask in S1, 1 evidence of legwork, 1 polite close, 1 register match.

12
[4]
Write 4–5 sentences of feedback in SBI form to a hypothetical peer who interrupted you during a team presentation. Keep the feedback specific, non-judgmental, and ending with a question.
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) Situation — "yesterday's team presentation"; (2) Behaviour — "you cut in twice during my slide on the timeline"; (3) Impact — "the client asked us to re-explain, and I felt my expertise on the timeline was undermined"; (4) Question — "What was going on? Is there a better way for us to coordinate next time?" Look for private-not-public delivery, and tone calibrated to the relationship. Marks: 1 each component (S, B, I, Q).

Part D: Application [15 marks]
13
[8]
Apply Unit 3 frameworks to a realistic scenario. You are six months into a co-op placement. Your supervisor is overwhelmed by client demands and has been short with the team. You believe a small process change (a shared client-status spreadsheet) would help. Without formal authority, design your move:
(a) the manage-up assessment: what are your supervisor's pressures? (2 marks)
(b) the credibility check: what have you been doing for six months that earns you the right to propose? (2 marks)
(c) the proposal: how do you frame the spreadsheet to your supervisor without overclaiming? (2 marks)
(d) the response if your supervisor says no: how do you handle it? (2 marks)
Marking Rubric

(a) Look for: identification of measurable supervisor pressures (number of clients, deadlines, feedback from her supervisor). (b) Look for: track record — consistent follow-through, small visible help, no mistakes-that-matter. (c) Look for: low-ego framing ("would this be useful, or shall I drop it?"); evidence of having drafted the spreadsheet; offer to maintain it. (d) Look for: graceful acceptance, no public criticism, return to the proposal in 2 months with different framing or evidence. Marks as above.
