Topic 5.1–5.2 — Voting and Volunteering
Question 1
In Canada, the three levels of elections that an 18-year-old votes in are:
Solution: The three levels are federal, provincial, and municipal. Each has separate jurisdiction (federal: immigration/defence; provincial: education/health-care; municipal: transit/zoning).
Question 2
"47% turnout among voters aged 18–24 vs 75% among voters aged 65–74" — Elections Canada, 2021.
The structural consequence of this youth-turnout gap is:
Solution: Politicians prioritise the voters who reliably vote. The largest single action an 18-year-old can take to shape policy is reliable election turnout — federal, provincial, and municipal.
Question 3
"Voluntourism" critique refers to:
Solution: Critics (Biddle, Papi-Thornton) note that short international trips often displace local labour and produce more value for the volunteer than the community. Sustainable contribution requires longer commitment, alignment with community-led organisations, and skills that fit the need.
Question 4
The healthier pattern for first-year adult volunteering is:
Solution: Time-limited defined-scope first commitments produce data — what energised you, what drained — that calibrates the second commitment. Open-ended starts often lead to burnout.
Topic 5.3–5.4 — Lifelong Learning and AI Labour Market
Question 5
The 70-20-10 model of adult learning (Center for Creative Leadership) holds that adults learn roughly:
Solution: The 70-20-10 split is the rough guideline. The numbers are contested but the proportions point at where adult learning happens — most of it is on-the-job experiential and from interactions, not from formal study.
Question 6
Deliberate practice (Anders Ericsson) emphasises that:
Solution: Ericsson distinguished deliberate practice (focused, edge-of-ability, with feedback) from rote repetition. The "10,000 hours" rule (popularised by Malcolm Gladwell) is a rough heuristic; Ericsson's actual claim was about quality of practice.
Question 7
Charles Handy's "portfolio career" (1989) refers to:
Solution: Handy's "Age of Unreason" introduced the portfolio-career concept. The 2026 Canadian labour market — average tenure ~2.8 years for under-30 workers, 15–20% in gig economy, AI reshaping task content — has made the model more relevant.
Question 8
The pattern in occupational research about AI reshaping work (e.g., WEF Future of Jobs 2024) is:
Solution: AI is reshaping task content within jobs faster than it eliminates jobs. The strategic implication: invest in the parts of your skill set that AI does not yet do well — original argument, judgment, integration, relational work.
Topic 5.5–5.6 — Networks and "Enough"
Question 9
Mark Granovetter's 1973 finding "the strength of weak ties" claims that:
Solution: Granovetter's paper showed that information about jobs reaches people most often through weak ties. LinkedIn's 2022 natural experiment confirmed the pattern. The implication: investing in network size and diversity matters for career mobility.
Question 10
A "personal board of directors" (Kram 1985, mentor-network model) refers to:
Solution: The network-of-mentors model has largely replaced the one-mentor-for-life model. A personal board of three to five people from diverse backgrounds gives you specific advice across the dimensions of your life.
Question 11
The "hedonic treadmill" (Brickman and Campbell, 1971) describes:
Solution: The hedonic treadmill is the empirical finding that material gains adapt away. Above a moderate income threshold (~$90,000–$100,000 household), incremental income produces diminishing returns to well-being. The implication for "enough" thinking is structural, not moralistic.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain what "the Tail End" (Tim Urban, 2014) thought-experiment reveals about how an 18-year-old should think about time with parents and close friends.
Sample: Urban's exercise: estimate annual in-person days with specific people, multiply by remaining years (parents' likely lifespan, your geographic trajectory). Most people discover they have already spent the majority of their lifetime in-person time with parents and childhood friends by their mid-twenties, especially once moves for school and work are factored in. The reframing changes attitude toward present-day visits and calls: the calls home and visits during breaks are not optional politeness; they are most of the time you have left with the specific people who matter.
