Topic 3.1–3.2 — Email and Feedback
Question 1
The most important sentence of a professional email is:
Solution: The subject line is the headline. Vague subjects get skipped; specific subjects get opened. The discipline is treating it like a news headline.
Question 2
"In yesterday's client call, you cut Aisha off twice when she was explaining the timeline; the client looked confused and we had to re-explain after the meeting."
This feedback uses the model:
Solution: SBI (Center for Creative Leadership) keeps feedback specific and non-judgmental: Situation, Behaviour, Impact. SBI+Q adds a closing Question to open dialogue.
Question 3
Heen and Stone (2014) distinguish three types of feedback that get tangled in conversation:
Solution: Heen and Stone's "Thanks for the Feedback" (2014) identifies three categories — Appreciation (being seen), Coaching (growth), Evaluation (rating). The giver and receiver often mean different categories with the same word.
Question 4
The mature adult response to feedback you disagree with is:
Solution: Adult feedback receipt: understand before defending, separate signal from delivery, find the kernel, decide what to do. You owe consideration, not agreement.
Topic 3.3–3.4 — EQ and Cross-Cultural Communication
Question 5
The four-domain model of emotional intelligence (Goleman) includes:
Solution: Goleman's four-domain EQ model (originally derived from Salovey and Mayer's 1990 work) is the workplace-default framework.
Question 6
"Name it to tame it" (Lieberman et al., 2007) refers to the finding that:
Solution: The Lieberman fMRI research showed that labelling emotion activates prefrontal regulation and reduces amygdala reactivity. The practical move: name what you are feeling specifically before responding.
Question 7
Hofstede's "power distance" dimension measures:
Solution: Power distance is one of Hofstede's six cultural dimensions; it measures default acceptance of hierarchical authority. Awareness of it reduces cross-cultural misreading.
Question 8
The most-skipped negotiation question in adult conversations is:
Solution: Fisher and Ury's Harvard method hinges on the position-to-interest move. Asking "why does this matter to you?" surfaces the interest under the stated position.
Topic 3.5–3.6 — Negotiation and Leadership
Question 9
BATNA stands for:
Solution: Ury's BATNA concept: pre-negotiation identification of what you will do if no deal is reached. Without BATNA you have no leverage and no exit.
Question 10
Edmondson's "psychological safety" (1999) is best defined as:
Solution: Psychological safety is about predictable safe interpersonal-risk-taking. Google's Aristotle Project (2015) found it was the strongest predictor of team effectiveness.
Question 11
"Managing up" (Abbajay, 2018) involves three core moves:
Solution: Managing up is professional, not sycophantic. It is the discipline of being a low-friction high-value report — someone who reduces the manager's load and brings solutions.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain why the Harvard Negotiation method (Fisher and Ury) emphasises "interests" over "positions."
Sample: A position is what someone says they want ("I want a 15% raise"); an interest is the underlying need ("I want to keep up with cost of living and feel recognised"). Positions collide while interests can often be reconciled because each side can identify multiple options that satisfy interests on both sides. The Harvard method's leverage is the move from positions ("I want this, you want that") to interests ("why does each of us want what we say we want") which usually opens options the positional fight cannot see.
