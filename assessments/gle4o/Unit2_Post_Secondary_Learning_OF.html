Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) SQ3R (Robinson, 1946); (b) Cornell notes (Pauk); (c) zettelkasten (Luhmann).
Answer Key

(a) SQ3R — Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review; reading method by Francis P. Robinson (1946); active recall structure increases retention ~40–80% over straight reading. (b) Cornell notes — three-zone page (right-hand notes, left-hand cue column, bottom summary); designed for spaced retrieval at three timescales. (c) Zettelkasten — Luhmann's "slip-box"; one note per concept with unique ID and links; tool for long-term cross-course concept synthesis.

2
[2]
A Red Seal trade apprenticeship in Ontario typically requires (best single answer):
Answer Key

B. Trade-specific hour requirements vary; typical 6,000–9,000 on-the-job hours plus three or four 6–12-week in-school technical blocks plus a provincial Certificate of Qualification exam. The Red Seal endorsement is interprovincial. Skilled Trades Ontario is the provincial regulator since 2022.

3
[2]
In 2–3 sentences, distinguish skimming, scanning, and deep reading.
Answer Key

Skimming locates overall structure — appropriate for SQ3R Survey step and end-of-term review. Scanning locates a specific fact (definition, quotation) — appropriate for reference. Deep reading is slow, line-by-line, with annotation — appropriate for primary sources, mathematical proofs, and texts the assessment will hinge on. Skill is applying the right register to the right text.

4
[3]
Define each: (a) implementation intentions (Gollwitzer); (b) timeboxing; (c) Getting Things Done (Allen).
Answer Key

(a) Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999) — if-then plans pre-deciding a specific response in a specific situation; research shows roughly doubled follow-through. (b) Timeboxing — assigning each hour of the day to a specific block on the calendar; surfaces under-estimation of task time. (c) Getting Things Done (Allen, 2001) — capture, clarify, organise, reflect, engage; weekly review is the key practice.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
7
[5]
"Use ChatGPT to critique my outline, check grammar in three paragraphs, and suggest one counter-argument. All argument, evidence selection, and 95%+ of the prose are mine."
Analyse this AI-use disclosure. In 5–6 sentences, identify why it is consistent with most Ontario university academic-integrity policies, and identify one risk the student should still manage.
Answer Key

The disclosure is policy-consistent because: (1) it acknowledges AI use specifically; (2) it identifies what the AI did (outline critique, grammar check, counter-argument suggestion); (3) it describes what the student's contribution is (argument, evidence selection, prose ownership); (4) the AI is used as collaborator, not substitute. The remaining risk: the disclosure assumes the instructor's syllabus permits AI with disclosure; if the syllabus says "AI prohibited," even this disclosure does not save the student. Other risks: AI-hallucinated citations may have been incorporated and need verification; AI grammar suggestions may have shifted the student's voice. Marks: 2 policy consistency, 2 risk identification, 1 conventions.

8
[5]
Apply executive-function frameworks. Andre is a first-year Engineering student at Waterloo. His Friday calculus problem set was estimated at 2 hours but actually took 4 hours; he is now an hour behind on the Tuesday Physics midterm preparation. In 5–6 sentences, describe how he should respond — using implementation intentions, timeboxing, and the Sunday weekly review.
Answer Key

Strong response: (1) Recognise that the under-estimation is itself useful data — typical first-year students under-estimate by 50–100%, and recalibrating against reality is half the work. (2) Re-timebox the next 4 days: now that we know calculus is twice as slow, the implication is reduced time for other study blocks. (3) Implementation intentions: "If 7 p.m. Monday, then read Physics Chapter 6 for the midterm; if 10 a.m. Wednesday, then write the lab report at the library." (4) For Sunday review: log the under-estimation and the cause (the implicit-differentiation rules he had not understood); book office hours Monday at 11 to address the gap. (5) Acknowledge that protecting sleep is the higher priority than completing all originally planned work. Marks: 1 recalibration, 1 timeboxing, 1 implementation intentions, 1 review, 1 sleep priority.

9
[5]
Critique the claim: "AI tools should be entirely banned from undergraduate work in 2026." In 5–6 sentences, identify one strong argument for the ban and one strong argument against.
Answer Key

For ban: AI use substitutes for the cognitive work that university assessments are designed to develop and measure; if students do not write the essay themselves they do not learn to write; instructors cannot reliably distinguish AI from human prose with current detector technology, so the only enforceable rule is total prohibition; the credential value of the degree depends on students having done the work. Against ban: AI tools are now ubiquitous workplace tools and avoiding them in undergrad leaves graduates under-prepared; students can use AI as collaborator (outline critique, grammar check, counter-argument generation) without substituting it for their thinking; a per-instructor permission model with disclosure preserves the learning while acknowledging the tool reality; total bans are unenforceable in practice and create perverse incentives. Strong responses recognise both arguments are real and that the per-instructor permission model is a defensible compromise. Marks: 2 ban argument, 2 anti-ban argument, 1 synthesis.

11
[5]
Write a coherent 6–8 sentence answer describing the SQ3R reading method using a concrete example — a 40-page chapter assigned for Tuesday's tutorial.
Answer Key

Steps: (1) Survey (~5 min) — chapter title, headings, opening and closing paragraphs, figures, bold terms. (2) Question (~5 min) — convert each section heading into a question. (3) Read (~70 min) — read each section actively looking for the answer to its question; close the book at the end of each section. (4) Recite (within Read step) — answer the question in your own words. (5) Review (~15 min) — look back at all questions and answers, write a one-paragraph summary. Total ~105 minutes vs ~90 minutes straight reading, but Tuesday's seminar arrives with usable knowledge instead of vague familiarity. Marks: 5 (1 per step + 1 example concreteness).

12
[4]
Write a 4–5-sentence email to the writing centre at a hypothetical Ontario university requesting an appointment for help with a first essay outline. Use professional register and be specific about your need.
Marking Rubric

Look for: (1) clear subject line ("Writing-centre appointment request — first essay outline review"); (2) opening greeting appropriate to register; (3) specific ask in sentence one ("I'd like to book a 30-minute appointment to discuss the outline of my first PSY101 essay"); (4) context that gives the tutor what they need (course, due date, current draft state); (5) polite close. Marks: 1 subject, 1 ask, 1 context, 1 close + 1 register match.

📝 Communication Category Rubric (instructor-graded)

Per Ontario's Growing Success (2010) Achievement Chart, Communication is one of the four equally-weighted categories (25% of the course mark). This rubric is applied to every student-written response (short-answer, extended-response, essay, lab report, deputation, etc.) on this assessment.

Criterion Level 1 — Limited (50–59%) Level 2 — Some (60–69%) Level 3 — Considerable (70–79%) Level 4 — Thorough (80–100%)
Expression and organisation of ideas Ideas expressed with limited clarity; little organisation. Ideas expressed with some clarity; some organisation. Ideas expressed clearly and organised effectively. Ideas expressed with clarity, precision, and a high degree of organisation.
Communication for different audiences and purposes Limited awareness of audience/purpose; tone often inappropriate. Some awareness of audience/purpose; tone occasionally inappropriate. Consistently appropriate tone, register, and address to audience. Tone, register, voice, and address are precisely matched to audience and purpose.
Use of conventions, vocabulary, terminology Frequent errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation; little discipline-specific vocabulary. Some errors that occasionally impede meaning; emerging use of discipline-specific vocabulary. Few errors; consistent use of discipline-specific vocabulary. Virtually error-free; precise and varied use of discipline-specific vocabulary.

Level 3 represents the Provincial Standard. Use the most-recent and most-consistent evidence across multiple Communication tasks (this assessment, prior FOR work, written responses on AS quizzes, oral presentations) when assigning a course-level Communication mark. Growing Success, 2010, p. 17.
