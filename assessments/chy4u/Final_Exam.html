[2]B · Renaissance
The intellectual programme at the core of the Italian Renaissance — focused on Latin, Greek, history, and moral philosophy — is called:
Answer Key

B. Humanism — Petrarch, Valla, Pico, Erasmus.

2
[2]B · Reformation
Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses (1517) most directly attacked:
Answer Key

B. Tetzel's indulgence sales in Saxony.

3
[2]B · Columbian Exchange
Approximately how many enslaved Africans were embarked for transatlantic transport 1501–1866?
Answer Key

~12.5 million embarked, ~10.7 million survived the Middle Passage.

4
[2]C · Absolutism
Louis XIV's relocation of the French court to Versailles from 1682 was politically intended to:
Answer Key

Domesticate the French aristocracy by absorbing nobles into elaborate court ceremonial under royal supervision, removing them from provincial power bases.

5
[2]C · Constitutional
The 1689 English Bill of Rights established what major principle?
Answer Key

No taxation, no standing army, no suspension of statutes without parliamentary consent. Freedom of speech in Parliament. Protestant succession.

6
[2]C · Enlightenment
Match the thinker to their major work: (a) Locke; (b) Montesquieu; (c) Rousseau; (d) Wollstonecraft.
Answer Key

(a) Two Treatises of Government (1689); (b) The Spirit of the Laws (1748); (c) The Social Contract (1762); (d) A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792).

7
[2]D · French Revolution
The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was adopted in:
Answer Key

B.

8
[2]D · Haitian
Haiti was proclaimed independent on:
Answer Key

1 January 1804, by Jean-Jacques Dessalines, after thirteen years of revolution against French, Spanish, and British forces.

9
[2]D · Industrial
James Watt's 1769 patent improved the:
Answer Key

Steam engine (separate-condenser design); first deployed in mine pumping, then in factory spinning, then in transport.

[2]D · Imperialism
The Berlin Conference (1884–1885) set rules for European partition of Africa — but no delegates were invited from:
Answer Key

No African delegates were invited. Convened by Bismarck at Leopold II's request.

11
[2]E · WWI
The MAIN long-term causes of WWI are:
Answer Key

Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism — plus the short-term July Crisis trigger (assassination of Franz Ferdinand 28 June 1914).

12
[2]E · Russia
The Bolshevik seizure of power in Petrograd took place in:
Answer Key

October-November 1917 (the "October Revolution"; 25-26 October Old Style / 7-8 November New Style). The February Revolution earlier in 1917 had overthrown the Tsar.

13
[2]E · Versailles
Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles is known as:
Answer Key

The "war guilt clause" — assigning responsibility for the war and its damage to Germany and her allies. Basis for the ~132 billion gold marks reparations bill of 1921.

14
[1]E · Holocaust
Approximately how many Jewish people were murdered in the Holocaust?
Answer Key

~6 million Jews — two-thirds of European Jewry — plus 5+ million non-Jewish victims (Roma, disabled, gay men, Polish elites, Soviet POWs).

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
15
[8]Document-Based Question
Passage A: "Article 1. Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions can be based only on common utility… Article 17. Since property is an inviolable and sacred right, no one shall be deprived thereof…" — Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, August 1789
Passage B: "I had thought you would have been pleased to see the African race admitted to such a great honor… But far from it, the colonists of all parties unite to oppose us. Will it be necessary to be born white in order to enjoy that liberty which our laws bestow on all?" — Vincent Ogé, free man of colour, to the Constituent Assembly, May 1790
Analyse the tension between these two sources. (a) What does Passage A claim universally? (b) What contradiction is Passage B exposing? (c) How did the French Revolution and Napoleon ultimately respond? 8-10 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) Universal natural rights, equality before the law, equal protection. (b) The Declaration's universalism was selectively applied — free people of colour in the French colonies were denied political rights, and enslaved Africans were entirely outside its protection. Ogé was tortured to death in February 1791. (c) The National Convention abolished slavery in all French colonies in February 1794 — the first such European act; Napoleon restored it in 1802 to retake Saint-Domingue. The Haitian Revolution's success (1791–1804) was the most decisive resolution from below. Final French abolition came in 1848. Marks: 2 (a), 3 (b), 3 (c).

16
[6]Cause & Consequence
"The Protestant Reformation was a religious revolution made possible by political and technological conditions." Argue for or against. Identify at least three converging causes. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong responses cite multi-causation: Luther's theology (content); printing press (scale — To the Christian Nobility sold 4,000 copies in 18 days); German princes' political and financial interests (protection — Frederick the Wise sheltered Luther; territorial princes could claim church lands); Habsburg distraction with Ottoman wars (Suleiman besieged Vienna 1529); Catholic Church's own corruption. Jan Hus's similar theology in 1415 failed for lack of these conditions. 6 marks: 2 for evidence, 2 for cause-analysis, 2 for synthesis.

17
[6]Continuity & Change
"What changed and what stayed the same in women's lives between 1789 and 1945?" 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

Changes: extension of suffrage (NZ 1893, Canada 1918, UK 1918/1928, US 1920, France 1944); access to higher education and the professions; legal property rights for married women; factory and clerical work expansion; world-war participation. Continuities: gender pay gap; primary responsibility for domestic labour; restrictions on bodily autonomy; political under-representation; sexual violence inadequately addressed. The 1945 starting point of full legal equality is far from 1789's nearly total subordination, but social structures remained durably gendered. 6 marks: 3 changes, 3 continuities.

18
[5]Historical Significance
Apply the Seixas-Morton significance criteria to the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

(1) Importance at time: foundational; signed by 48 nations 10 December 1948. (2) Depth/durability: basis of subsequent international human-rights law (ICCPR, ICESCR 1966; UN Genocide Convention 1948; many national constitutions including Canada's Charter 1982). (3) Quantity affected: directly all UN member states' citizens; indirectly all humanity. (4) Broader trends: marks the post-WWII shift toward inter-human, not only inter-state, international law. Highly significant by all four criteria. Canadian John Humphrey co-drafted. 5 marks.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
19
[10]Comparative Essay
Write one full essay-length response (10-15 sentences) on the following: "Compare the responses of Qing China and Tokugawa/Meiji Japan to Western pressure in the 19th century. Why did Japan adapt successfully while China declined into 'unequal treaties' and eventual dynastic collapse?"
Answer Key

Strong responses identify the asymmetric trajectories: China — Opium Wars 1839-1842 and 1856-1860; Unequal Treaties (Nanjing 1842, Tianjin 1858); Taiping Rebellion 1851-1864 (20-30 million dead, deadliest war of the 19th c.); slow Self-Strengthening; Hundred Days' Reform 1898 failed; Qing collapse 1911. Japan — Perry 1853-1854; Meiji Restoration 1868; abolition of samurai 1873; Meiji Constitution 1889; defeat of China 1895, Russia 1905; annexation of Korea 1910. Causes of divergence: Japan's smaller population (~30m vs China's ~400m); more cohesive elite (Satsuma-Chōshū coalition); defensible island geography; pre-1868 rangaku tradition imported European science; Meiji oligarchs' strategic clarity; timing — Japan modernised in the window before colonialism locked Asia in. Marks: 2 for thesis, 4 for evidence (each side), 3 for analytical synthesis, 1 conventions.

20
[8]PEEL Paragraph
Write a single, well-structured PEEL paragraph (8-10 sentences) on the claim: "The Cold War ended not because the West 'won' but because the Soviet leadership chose not to use force." Include specific dates and decisions.
Answer Key

Strong responses: P — thesis stated; E — Gorbachev's restraint in Polish Solidarity 1988-1989, refusal to send tanks to East Germany October 1989, acceptance of German reunification 1990; contrast with East German uprising 1953, Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968 when Moscow intervened; E — Soviet economic weakness did not require non-intervention (Brezhnev managed weakness with repression); declassified documents (Cold War International History Project); L — back to thesis. Counter-evidence: Western pressure (Reagan defence build-up); Eastern European agency (Solidarity, Charter 77); synthesis: Gorbachev's restraint was necessary, not sufficient. 8 marks: 2 Point, 3 Evidence, 2 Explanation, 1 conventions.

[4]Thesis
Rewrite this weak thesis: "Many things changed during the long 20th century."
Answer Key

Sample: "Hobsbawm's 'short 20th century' (1914-1991) was structured by three sequential crises — the world wars 1914-1945, the Cold War 1947-1991, and the post-1989 dissolution — each generating institutional and ideological responses (the League, the UN, the EU, the WTO) that shape the world historians of the 2020s still operate in." Marks: specific (1), debatable (1), engages periodisation (1), reasonable scope (1).

Part D — Application [25 marks]
23
[8]Ethical Dimension
Apply the historical-thinking concept of ethical dimension to a 20th-century case of state violence — choose one: the Holocaust, the Armenian Genocide, the Rwandan Genocide, the Holodomor, or Canadian residential schools. Identify what evidence supports the ethical judgement, what perpetrator and bystander responsibilities exist, and what institutional responses emerged. 8-10 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong responses select one case and provide specific evidence: dates, perpetrators, victims, scale, sources (e.g., for the Holocaust: Wannsee minutes, Nuremberg trial records, survivor testimony, demographic records; for Rwanda: ICTR proceedings, Dallaire's Shake Hands with the Devil, BBC archive). Identify bystander responsibility (Allied inaction during the Holocaust; UN under-resourcing of UNAMIR in Rwanda; Canadian church and state collaboration in residential schools). Institutional responses: UDHR 1948, Genocide Convention 1948, ICTY/ICTR/ICC, Canada TRC 2008-2015 and 94 Calls to Action. Marks: 2 for case description, 3 for evidence, 2 for ethical judgement, 1 for institutional response.

24
[6]Connection past/present
Apply your knowledge: How does the historian's toolkit (source criticism, corroboration, multi-causation, ethical reasoning) apply to contemporary debates about (a) misinformation in social media or (b) climate-change responsibility? Choose one. 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) For misinformation: source-criticism (who created this content, when, why? what is its provenance?); corroboration (do independent sources confirm the claim?); multi-causation (algorithmic amplification, individual choices, platform incentives, foreign interference all contribute); ethical reasoning (what duties do citizens, platforms, regulators have?). (b) For climate responsibility: causation (industrial-revolution-onward cumulative emissions; historical responsibility concentrated in industrialised North Atlantic, increasingly China); evidence (IPCC reports; emission data; impact assessments); ethical reasoning (intergenerational justice, distributive justice — emissions concentrated in rich countries, consequences in poor); historical perspective (1990s climate-change framing differed from 2020s urgency). 6 marks: 2 each for two-three skills; 2 for specific example application.

25
[6]Comparative
Apply the comparative method to two revolutions of the long 19th century (1789–1914). Compare the French Revolution (1789-1799) and the Russian Revolution (1917) — what was similar and what was different? 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

Similarities: both overthrew anciens régimes; both proceeded in phases (constitutional → radical → consolidation); both produced revolutionary terrors (1793-1794 Reign of Terror; Red Terror and Stalinist purges); both transformed national legal codes; both ended in personal rule (Napoleon; Stalin); both shaped 19th/20th-century political vocabulary; both faced foreign intervention. Differences: ideological foundations (Enlightenment natural-rights vs Marxism-Leninism); class composition (French bourgeoisie + sans-culottes; Russian industrial proletariat + Bolshevik party); peasant question (French peasants gained land, Russian peasants reabsorbed by collectivisation 1928-1933); international ambitions (Napoleonic Wars; the Comintern). Marks: 3 similarities, 3 differences.

26
[5]STSE / Real-World
Apply the curriculum to the 21st century: how do you think the historian of 2100 will periodise the early 21st century (2000-present)? What event(s) will likely be remembered as the period's defining feature(s)? Justify your answer. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

Plausible candidates: the climate crisis (the long emission story; the 2015 Paris Agreement; the 1.5°C threshold likely crossed in the 2020s); the rise of Asia (China overtaking the US as largest economy in some measures by mid-century); the digital revolution (smartphone 2007; AI from 2022); 9/11 and post-9/11 wars; the 2008 financial crisis; COVID-19; the Russian invasion of Ukraine 2022; the unfinished decolonisation agenda. A defensible answer identifies one likely "defining" candidate (climate, plausibly), acknowledges that contemporary historians cannot have full perspective, applies significance criteria, and ties to broader course themes. 5 marks: 2 for candidate, 2 for justification using significance criteria, 1 for honest epistemic humility about contemporary judgement.
