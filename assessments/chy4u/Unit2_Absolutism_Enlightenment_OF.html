Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
Define absolutism and name two rulers commonly cited as exemplars in 17th-18th century Europe.
Answer Key

Absolutism: theory and practice that sovereignty resides solely in the monarch, unmediated by representative institutions. Exemplars: Louis XIV of France (r.1643–1715), Peter the Great of Russia (r.1682–1725), Frederick William the Great Elector of Brandenburg, Charles XI of Sweden, or the Habsburg emperors.

2
[2]
Identify the year of the Glorious Revolution and name the joint monarchs who replaced James II.
Answer Key

1688. William III of Orange (Dutch stadtholder) and his wife Mary II (James II's Protestant daughter) accepted the throne jointly on conditions set out in the 1689 Bill of Rights.

3
[2]
Name three foundational works of the Enlightenment and their authors.
Answer Key

Acceptable: Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689); Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748); Voltaire's Candide (1759); Rousseau's The Social Contract (1762); Diderot/d'Alembert's Encyclopédie (1751–1772); Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776); Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792); Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764).

4
[2]
In which Caribbean colony was the wealthiest plantation economy of the late 18th century located, and which European power held it?
Answer Key

C — French Saint-Domingue. Roughly 40% of world sugar and 60% of world coffee, ~500,000 enslaved Africans, ~30,000 whites. The colony's revolution (1791–1804) produced Haiti.

5
[3]
Define each: (a) Cohong / Canton system, (b) sakoku, (c) rangaku.
Answer Key

(a) Cohong (Canton system, 1757–1842): Qing restriction of European trade to Guangzhou through 13 licensed Chinese merchant houses. (b) Sakoku: Tokugawa "closed country" policy (from 1635) restricting foreign contact. (c) Rangaku: "Dutch studies"; Japanese scholarly engagement with European medical and scientific texts via Dutch traders at Dejima.

6
[2]
Name two reforms of Joseph II of Austria (r.1780–1790).
Answer Key

Acceptable: Toleranzpatent of 1781 (religious freedom for non-Catholic Christians and expanded Jewish civil rights); abolition of personal serfdom (1781); dissolution of contemplative monasteries; codification of law; German as administrative language; reduction of judicial torture; promotion of basic literacy (compulsory primary schooling continued from Maria Theresa's 1774 reform).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
7
[5]
"L'État, c'est moi" — "I am the State." (Attributed to Louis XIV, though almost certainly apocryphal; first appears in early 19th-century writings.)
Analyse the historical-significance question: should this often-quoted phrase be used in a Grade 12 essay? Use source-criticism principles in your answer. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

The phrase is not contemporary; it was first attributed to Louis XIV by Pierre-Édouard Lemontey (1818) and popularised in the 19th century. There is no document from Louis's reign in which he is recorded saying this. A historian using it for "what Louis believed" is using fabricated evidence. However, the phrase captures something of the 19th-century image of absolutism, and may be useful for discussions of later historiography. The discipline is to label it accurately: "the much-later attributed phrase" rather than "as Louis said." This is exactly the kind of source-criticism that distinguishes Level 4 work. 5 marks: 1 for noting non-contemporary attribution, 2 for the source-criticism implications, 1 for appropriate uses, 1 for communication.

8
[5]
Compare and contrast the political trajectories of France and England between 1640 and 1715. What were they each moving toward, and why did they diverge? 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

France moved toward absolute monarchy: the Fronde failure, Louis XIV's personal rule from 1661 without a first minister, Versailles, mercantilism, suppression of Huguenots in 1685. England moved toward constitutional monarchy: Civil War 1642–1651, Charles I executed 1649, Restoration 1660, Exclusion Crisis, Glorious Revolution 1688, 1689 Bill of Rights. Causes of divergence: difference in parliamentary tradition (English Parliament had medieval roots stronger than the French Estates-General which had not met since 1614); different religious settlements; different impacts of the Civil War on royal-noble relations; the personality and circumstances of monarchs. 5 marks: 2 for naming each trajectory, 2 for explaining divergence, 1 for communication.

9
[3]
"The 18th century is the century in which the Atlantic slave trade reached its peak — the same century that produced the Declaration of Independence and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen." How should a historian resolve this contradiction? 3-5 sentences.
Answer Key

The strongest answers refuse easy resolution: the contradiction was real and visible at the time (Samuel Johnson 1775: "How is it that we hear the loudest yelps for liberty among the drivers of negroes?"). The universal-rights vocabulary was new and limited in original application; it was also the language abolitionists later used (Equiano, Wilberforce, Wollstonecraft). The contradiction was resolved only by further struggle through 19th-century abolition movements. The ethical-dimension move is to hold both ledgers — rhetoric and trade-volumes — together without flattening either. 3 marks: 1 for naming the contradiction, 1 for the visibility-at-the-time, 1 for the long-term resolution through struggle.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[6]
Write one PEEL-structured analytical paragraph (6-8 sentences) on the following claim: "The Enlightenment's universalist rhetoric of natural rights was deeply contradicted by its tolerance for, and partial complicity in, transatlantic slavery." Include one specific Enlightenment thinker and one specific aspect of the slave economy.
Answer Key

Strong responses: P — natural-rights rhetoric and the slave system contradicted; E — a specific thinker (Voltaire invested in the French slave trade; Locke held shares in the Royal African Company; Hume's racist footnote in "Of National Characters"); E — interpretation showing how the contradiction reveals the limits of who counted as "men" in 1789 universalism; L — connection back to thesis. 6 marks: 1 Point, 2 Evidence, 2 Explanation, 1 Link/conventions.

11
[3]
Rewrite this weak thesis into a strong one: "The Enlightenment was a movement of ideas in the 18th century."
Answer Key

Sample: "The Enlightenment's defining innovation was not any single doctrine but its application of an empirical, communicative method — pamphlets, salons, the Encyclopédie, learned societies — that produced political theory, jurisprudence, and economics as separable disciplines capable of challenging traditional authority." Look for: specific claim (1), debatable (1), engages content beyond a generality (1).

12
[3]
In one short paragraph (4 sentences), compare Hobbes's and Locke's theories of legitimate authority.
Answer Key

Both argued government emerges from a social contract escaping the "state of nature." For Hobbes (Leviathan, 1651), human nature is competitive and violent ("solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"), so the contract surrenders all rights to an absolute sovereign whose authority cannot be questioned. For Locke (Two Treatises, 1689), people are by nature rational and equal, and the contract creates a government bound to protect natural rights (life, liberty, property); when government violates these, the people may dissolve it. Hobbes legitimates absolutism; Locke legitimates constitutional government and the right of revolution. 3 marks: 1 each for Hobbes, Locke, and the contrast.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[5]
Apply the historical-thinking concept of continuity and change to Catherine the Great's reign (1762–1796). What was Russia like at her accession, and at her death? 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

At accession (1762): Russia was a serf-based agrarian autocracy with limited urban culture and a Westernising elite still digesting Peter the Great's reforms. Changes Catherine instituted or accelerated: territorial expansion (Crimea 1783, Polish partitions 1772/1793/1795); Westernising court culture (correspondence with Voltaire and Diderot, the Hermitage collection); the 1767 Legislative Commission and the Nakaz; restriction of torture; modernised provincial administration (1775). Continuities: serfdom intensified rather than weakened (esp. after Pugachev 1773–1775); autocratic political structure preserved; the Russian Orthodox Church remained subordinated to state. A strong response holds both threads and acknowledges that the Enlightenment correspondence and the worsening serfdom co-existed. 5 marks: 2 for changes, 2 for continuities, 1 for synthesis.

14
[4]
"There is nothing more to be said about religion in the Empire: there cannot be too much of it nor too little. The question now is solely about the people…" — Voltaire, in a letter from c.1750s, characterising his outlook
Apply source-analysis to evaluate Voltaire's stance toward religious authority. (a) What does the passage suggest about Voltaire's view on religion's social role? (b) How would Voltaire's life experience (his exile to England 1726-1729, the Calas affair 1762-1765) shape his views? 4-6 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) Voltaire is shifting attention from religious controversy to the welfare of "the people" — a secularising move characteristic of moderate Enlightenment thought. He is not an atheist; he is a deist who wants religion's social role limited. (b) His three-year exile to England exposed him to British religious toleration and parliamentary government; the Calas affair (in which a Protestant was judicially murdered) drove him to publish the Treatise on Toleration (1763), one of the period's most influential arguments against religious persecution. 4 marks: 2 for (a), 2 for (b).

15
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: Drawing on Unit 2, how do the constitutional principles of the 1689 English Bill of Rights and the Lockean theory of consent still shape Canadian government today? 3-4 sentences.
Answer Key

The 1689 Bill of Rights is one of the constitutional documents inherited by Canada through the Constitution Act 1867 and constitutional convention; parliamentary supremacy, no taxation without consent, and judicial independence are baseline Canadian constitutional features. The Lockean idea of government by consent informs the Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982). The British monarch still acts as Canadian head of state through the Governor-General, a constitutional inheritance. 3 marks: 1 for constitutional principles, 1 for Charter connection, 1 for specific Canadian example.
