Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define the "M-A-I-N" causes of WWI, giving one example of each.
Answer Key

Militarism (arms races, especially Anglo-German naval); Alliances (Triple Alliance: Germany/Austria-Hungary/Italy; Triple Entente: France/Russia/Britain); Imperialism (colonial competition, esp. in Africa and the Balkans); Nationalism (particularly Slavic nationalism threatening Austria-Hungary). 3 marks: 1 for naming, 2 for examples.

2
[2]
Identify two major reasons for the Russian Revolution of February 1917.
Answer Key

Food shortages and inflation in Petrograd; military defeats and casualties; army mutinies; the Tsar's loss of legitimacy; women textile workers' International Women's Day strike (23 February Old Style); collapse of urban order. Any two well-stated.

3
[2]
Identify three major terms of the Treaty of Versailles imposed on Germany.
Answer Key

Acceptable: loss of ~13% of territory (Alsace-Lorraine to France, Polish Corridor); military limited to 100,000 with no conscription, no air force, no submarines; Rhineland demilitarised; loss of overseas colonies; war-guilt clause (Article 231); reparations (132 billion gold marks 1921); exclusion from League of Nations.

4
[2]
The 1935 Nuremberg Laws:
Answer Key

B. The Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honor (September 1935) implemented the systematic Nazi racial-legal framework. Kristallnacht followed in 1938; the Holocaust began in 1941.

5
[2]
Identify (a) the major Soviet victory of February 1943 that turned the Eastern Front and (b) the major Allied landings in June 1944.
Answer Key

(a) Battle of Stalingrad (August 1942 – February 1943); destruction of the German Sixth Army (~300,000 casualties). (b) D-Day (6 June 1944), Operation Overlord, the Normandy landings (Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, Sword beaches; Canada's Juno).

6
[2]
Approximately how many Soviet citizens died in WWII (military + civilian)?
Answer Key

C — approximately 27 million Soviet citizens (civilians + military). The Eastern Front was where Germany lost more than 80% of its military casualties; Western popular memory often underweights this.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
7
[5]
"On July 28, 1914, … Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia… On the 30th, Russia ordered general mobilization. Germany followed suit, with a declaration of war on Russia on August 1 and on France on August 3. German troops invaded Belgium on August 4 to outflank French defences; Britain declared war on Germany the same day." — Standard textbook summary
Using this passage as a starting point: identify three "decision points" where a different choice by a major actor might have prevented the general war. Briefly explain each. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

Possible decision points: (1) Germany's "blank check" of 5-6 July 1914 to Austria-Hungary; without German support, Vienna might have negotiated. (2) Russian general mobilisation on 30 July; partial mobilisation could have given diplomacy more time. (3) The German Schlieffen Plan's commitment to invading Belgium; alternatives existed (defensive posture in the west, two-front war on a smaller scale). (4) British declaration to defend Belgian neutrality; though grounded in the 1839 treaty, British neutrality might have changed continental calculations. The historical-thinking move is to recognise contingency without dismissing structural causes — multiple decisions converged on war. 5 marks: 1 for each of 3 decisions, 2 for analytical depth.

8
[4]
Was the Treaty of Versailles "too harsh"? Argue for or against, using specific evidence and engaging counter-evidence. 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

Either position defensible. "Too harsh" (Keynes 1919): Article 231 humiliating; 132 billion gold marks reparations; territorial losses; combined effect impoverished Germany and fed Nazi grievance. "Not harsh enough" (MacMillan 2002, Marks): Germany retained sovereignty, was not occupied or divided; reparations were repeatedly reduced; ~50 billion gold marks actually paid; Germany re-emerged as great power within 15 years. The strongest answers acknowledge both ledgers and argue that the problem was unenforceability rather than inherent harshness. 4 marks: 2 for evidence, 2 for engaging counter-evidence.

9
[4]
Why did Germans vote for the Nazi Party in large numbers in 1932? Suggest three converging causes. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

(1) Economic collapse: unemployment exceeded 30%; Weimar coalition governments paralysed. (2) Fear of communism: KPD also growing; middle-class voters feared revolution. (3) Mass propaganda: Hitler's flying campaigns, rallies, exploitation of newsreel and radio. (4) Versailles grievance: NSDAP denounced reparations and lost territories. (5) Antisemitism: Nazi narrative blaming "Jewish capital" and "Jewish Marxism." (6) Conservative miscalculation: Papen and others believed they could use Hitler. 4 marks: 1 each for three causes, 1 for synthesis.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[6]
Write one PEEL paragraph (6-8 sentences) on the claim: "The Holocaust required not only Hitler's ideology but also a modern bureaucratic state, industrial technique, and the wartime collapse of normal moral restraint." Use specific evidence (a place, a number, an institution).
Answer Key

Strong responses: P — the Holocaust as a multi-factor event; E — Einsatzgruppen mass shootings 1941 (~1.5 million Soviet Jews); Wannsee Conference 20 January 1942; Auschwitz-Birkenau (~1.1 million murdered); use of railways, bureaucratic categorisation, IBM punch-card tabulation; the Nazi state's full machinery; E — interpretation drawing on Browning's Ordinary Men, Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust; L — back to thesis. 6 marks: 1 Point, 2 Evidence, 2 Explanation, 1 Link/conventions.

11
[3]
Rewrite this weak thesis: "WWI was bad."
Answer Key

Sample: "WWI was the catastrophe from which most of the 20th century's other disasters flow — the Russian Revolution, the Treaty of Versailles, the rise of fascism, the Second World War, and the Holocaust all become unintelligible without it; George Kennan called it 'the great seminal catastrophe' of the century with reason." Look for: specific claim, debatable, named connections.

12
[3]
In a short paragraph (4 sentences), compare Stalinist USSR and Nazi Germany. What did they share and where did they differ?
Answer Key

Shared: single party-state; mass terror; propaganda apparatus; cults of personality; persecution of "enemies of the people"; large camp systems (Gulag, KZ). Differences: ideological foundation (Marxism-Leninism vs racial nationalism); class targeting vs racial targeting; the Nazi state's Holocaust (industrial extermination of a defined ethnic-religious group) had no exact Soviet parallel, even allowing for Stalinist genocide accusations (Holodomor). Marks: 2 for shared, 1 for differences.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[5]
Apply the historical-thinking concept of ethical dimension to the US atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945). Set out the strongest argument for "justified" and the strongest argument for "not justified," then take a position. 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

"Justified" case: Japan unlikely to surrender without invasion; Operation Downfall would have killed hundreds of thousands of Americans and millions of Japanese; Tokyo firebombing (March 1945) had already killed ~100,000 civilians, so atomic weapons were continuous with conventional bombing; bombings shortened the war. "Not justified" case: Japan was already seeking surrender through Soviet channels; the bombings partly aimed to impress the USSR for postwar negotiations; Hiroshima and Nagasaki were predominantly civilian; no demonstration on an uninhabited area; ~200,000 civilian deaths, mostly women, children, elderly, were disproportionate. A defensible Level 4 answer takes a position and grounds it in evidence and ethical principle (just-war tradition, doctrine of double effect). 5 marks: 2 each side, 1 for the chosen position.

14
[4]
Apply source analysis to the Armenian Genocide (1915–1923). What kinds of primary sources exist for studying it, and what makes corroboration of the death toll (~1–1.5 million) defensible? 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

Primary sources include: Ottoman government deportation orders; US Ambassador Henry Morgenthau's despatches; German military officer reports (Germany was allied with the Ottomans); missionary records; survivor testimonies; demographic records before and after (Armenian population dropped from ~2 million in 1914 to ~400,000 in 1923 in surviving territory); post-war Ottoman court-martial proceedings (1919-1920). Corroboration across these independent sources, with very different biases, converges on the documented genocide. The Republic of Turkey continues to dispute the genocide label politically, but the historical consensus is firm. 4 marks: 2 for sources, 2 for corroboration logic.

15
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: How does the historical-thinking concept of significance apply to the United Nations and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)? Briefly explain. 3-4 sentences.
Answer Key

By Seixas-Morton criteria: (1) Importance at the time — the founding generation widely supported the UN and UDHR; (2) Depth and durability — the UDHR has been the basis of subsequent international human-rights law (ICCPR, ICESCR, Genocide Convention) and shapes constitutions globally; (3) Quantity affected — directly all UN member states' citizens, indirectly all humanity; (4) Revealing of trends — marks the transition from inter-state to also inter-human international law. Canadian John Humphrey was a major co-drafter. 3 marks: 1 per several criteria.
