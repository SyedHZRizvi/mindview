Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
List the six Seixas-Morton historical-thinking concepts.
Answer Key

Historical significance; cause and consequence; continuity and change; historical perspective (perspective-taking); the ethical dimension; evidence. 3 marks for all six.

2
[3]
Define each: (a) primary source, (b) secondary source, (c) corroboration.
Answer Key

(a) Primary: document, image, object, or testimony created at or close to the time of the event studied. (b) Secondary: later interpretation by a historian or other analyst. (c) Corroboration: confirmation of a claim by independent multiple sources with different biases. 1 mark each.

3
[2]
Define presentism and explain why historians try to avoid it.
Answer Key

Presentism: anachronistic projection of present-day values, concepts, and standards onto past actors and contexts. Historians avoid it to maintain "historical perspective" — understanding past worldviews on their own terms — which is necessary for both analytical accuracy and fair judgement. Avoiding presentism does not mean refusing ethical judgement; it means making such judgements informed by context.

4
[2]
Provide one MLA-9 Works Cited entry for the following: imaginary book titled "Power and Sugar: The Caribbean 1670–1830" by Robert Williams, published 2018 by Yale University Press in New Haven.
Answer Key

Williams, Robert. Power and Sugar: The Caribbean 1670–1830. Yale University Press, 2018. (Yale University Press is the publisher; location is now optional in MLA 9; italicised book title; period after each unit.) Full marks for correct format; partial credit for partial correctness.

5
[3]
Define each cognitive bias and give one example of how it might affect history-writing: (a) hindsight bias, (b) survivorship bias, (c) confirmation bias.
Answer Key

(a) Hindsight bias: exaggerating the inevitability of past events because we know how they ended (e.g., writing as if Hitler's rise was inevitable in 1928, when most contemporaries saw it as marginal). (b) Survivorship bias: studying only what survived (e.g., studying medieval women's lives mostly through nuns' writings, missing illiterate peasants). (c) Confirmation bias: seeking evidence that supports prior beliefs (e.g., writing about the French Revolution as confirming one's prior political views). 1 mark each.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[6]
"With a Heart full of sorrow we report that Mr. Lincoln died this morning at twenty-two minutes past seven o'clock." — Telegram from Edwin Stanton, Secretary of War, to Major-General John Dix, New York, 15 April 1865, 7:22 AM
Analyse this primary source. (a) What is the provenance? (b) What does the source directly tell us? (c) What can it not tell us about the assassination? (d) What two other sources would you want to corroborate or extend it? 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) Edwin Stanton, Lincoln's Secretary of War, sending an official telegram from the cabinet officer at Lincoln's deathbed (the Petersen House) to General Dix in New York. (b) The time of death (7:22 AM, 15 April 1865) and the official-government recognition. (c) Cannot tell us what happened in Ford's Theatre the night before; who planned the assassination; what Lincoln's last words were; how the country would respond. (d) Sources to corroborate: Mary Todd Lincoln's later testimony; doctors' clinical notes (Dr. Charles Leale); shorthand notes of cabinet meetings; eyewitness accounts at Ford's Theatre; the trial of the conspirators (1865). 6 marks: 1 for provenance, 1 for explicit content, 2 for limitations, 2 for corroborating sources.

7
[4]
Take a specific event from CHY4U (for example: the storming of the Bastille, 14 July 1789). Apply all four Seixas-Morton significance criteria — importance at the time, depth/durability, quantity affected, reveals broader trends — and reach a judgement about its overall significance. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

For the Bastille example: (1) Importance at the time — high; Parisian crowd seized political initiative; King ordered troops to withdraw the next day. (2) Depth/durability — high; 14 July became and remains French national day; the act of popular insurrection became a permanent revolutionary template. (3) Quantity affected — symbolic, then enormous (the French Revolution affected France's 28 million and rippled across Europe). (4) Broader trends — reveals the shift of sovereignty from king to people; the role of urban crowds in modern politics. Overall judgement: highly significant by all four criteria, though many of its consequences depended on later events (Tennis Court Oath, August Decrees). 4 marks: 1 per criterion well-applied.

8
[3]
"Was the Industrial Revolution inevitable?" Briefly evaluate this counterfactual question. Is it a legitimate historical question, and what would a defensible answer look like? 3-5 sentences.
Answer Key

Counterfactuals are useful but limited: they help test causal claims (if we removed factor X, would Y have followed?), but the counterfactual world is unknowable in detail. A defensible answer: the Industrial Revolution emerged from a contingent convergence of factors (British coal geography, the 1689 constitutional settlement, agricultural improvement, colonial-derived capital and demand, the printing-press and patent systems). Remove any major factor and industrialisation might still have happened — somewhere, eventually — but where, when, and on what terms might have differed substantially. The strongest answer recognises both contingency and structural pressure. 3 marks: 1 for noting counterfactuals' utility, 1 for limits, 1 for a defensible response.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Imagine you are writing a research essay on a CHY4U topic of your choice. Write the opening paragraph: an introduction containing (a) a hook or framing context (1-2 sentences), (b) a clear thesis statement (1 sentence), (c) a preview of the essay's structure (2-3 sentences). Total: 4-6 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong introductions: (a) Hook engages reader and introduces topic; (b) thesis is specific, debatable, named; (c) structure preview gives reader a roadmap. 6 marks: 2 hook, 2 thesis, 2 structure. Marks deducted for weak thesis or unfocused opening.

10
[3]
Identify three common plagiarism risks for the research essay and how to avoid each.
Answer Key

(1) Direct copying of sources without quotation marks — always quote and cite. (2) Paraphrasing too close to original wording — paraphrase requires substantial rewording AND citation. (3) Mosaic plagiarism — stitching together phrases from multiple sources without attribution. (4) AI-generated text and citations — AI is forbidden by most boards for graded work; verify any citation. (5) Using a friend's paper or a paid service — automatic academic-integrity failure. 3 marks: 1 each for three risks well-explained.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Apply the historical-thinking concepts to evaluate a contemporary news claim. Choose any recent event (a war, a court ruling, a climate event, an election). Identify (a) what primary sources exist; (b) what biases might colour the major secondary commentary; (c) what corroboration you would want before treating the claim as established; (d) which Seixas-Morton concept is most useful here. 6-8 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong responses select a specific contemporary event and apply all four elements. The skill being assessed is transfer: the same source-criticism, corroboration, and significance criteria apply to contemporary events as to 1517 or 1789. Marks: 1 each (a), (b), (c), 2 for (d).

13
[4]
Apply source-criticism to a hypothetical primary source: "Letter from a fictional 17th-century European missionary in China, 1640, describing the Qing conquest of Beijing." What four questions would you ask before using this letter as evidence about the 1644 conquest? 4-6 sentences.
Answer Key

(1) Date — 1640 letter cannot describe a 1644 event (the Qing took Beijing 1644); the date is impossible for the claimed content. (2) Identity and position of the missionary — Jesuit? Where stationed? What languages did he know? (3) Purpose and audience — was the letter intended for Vatican, missionary order, secular patrons? (4) What did he see directly versus hear second-hand? (5) Has this letter been authenticated by historians? The first issue alone disqualifies the source for its claimed content — a basic chronology check is the first move. 4 marks.

14
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: How are the inquiry skills from CHY4U useful outside history class — in evaluating news, social media, or political claims? Give one specific example. 3-4 sentences.
Answer Key

Source criticism transfers directly: provenance (who created this?), purpose (why?), corroboration (who else confirms?), context. Specific example might include evaluating a tweet about an election, a TikTok claim about a science study, or a news headline. The point is that "history" is not a static record but a method, and the method generalises. 3 marks: 1 transfer, 1 example, 1 communication.
