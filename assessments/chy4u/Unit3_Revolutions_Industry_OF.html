Identify and date: (a) the storming of the Bastille; (b) the execution of Louis XVI; (c) Napoleon's defeat at Waterloo.
Answer Key

(a) 14 July 1789. (b) 21 January 1793. (c) 18 June 1815. 1 mark each.

[2]
Name the main leader of the Haitian Revolution before his capture by the French, and the date of Haitian independence.
Answer Key

Toussaint Louverture (captured 1802, died in French prison April 1803). Jean-Jacques Dessalines proclaimed Haitian independence on 1 January 1804.

3
[2]
List three British technological innovations of the early Industrial Revolution.
Answer Key

Acceptable: Watt's steam engine (1769); Arkwright's water frame (1769); Crompton's spinning mule (1779); Cartwright's power loom (1785); Stephenson's railway locomotive (1814–1825); Bessemer process for steel (1856); the puddling and rolling processes; gas lighting.

[2]
German unification was completed in:
Answer Key

B. The Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871) was the third of Bismarck's wars of unification (after Denmark 1864 and Austria 1866). Proclamation 18 January 1871.

5
[2]
Name the major Meiji-era institution-building reforms enacted in or around 1873.
Answer Key

Abolition of the samurai class (1873); universal military conscription (1873); compulsory primary education (1872, fully implemented from 1873). Modern legal codes, the 1889 Meiji Constitution, the modernised navy followed.

6
[2]
Identify (a) the year Britain abolished the slave trade (not slavery itself) and (b) the year Britain abolished slavery in most of its empire.
Answer Key

(a) 1807 (Slave Trade Act, banning British ships from carrying enslaved people). (b) 1833 (Slavery Abolition Act, effective 1834, with apprenticeship until 1838). The US ended chattel slavery 1865 (13th Amendment); Brazil 1888.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
7
[5]
"Article 1. Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions can be based only on common utility… Article 17. Since property is an inviolable and sacred right…" — Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (August 1789)
Analyse this primary source. (a) Identify two Enlightenment thinkers whose ideas are visible in these articles. (b) Identify one tension between Articles 1 and 17 — particularly with respect to slavery. (c) When and how was the tension partially resolved? 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) Locke (natural rights to life, liberty, property; consent of governed); Rousseau (the social contract, common utility); Montesquieu (limited government). Mary Wollstonecraft would extend "Men" to women in her 1792 Vindication. (b) Article 1 declares men born free; Article 17 declares property inviolable. Enslaved people in French colonies were legally property — both articles could not be honoured for both planters and the enslaved. (c) The National Convention abolished slavery in February 1794; Napoleon restored it in 1802; final French abolition came in 1848. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was the most decisive resolution from below. Marks: 2 (thinkers), 2 (tension), 1 (resolution).

8
[4]
Why did the Haitian Revolution succeed while hundreds of other American slave revolts failed? Suggest four converging causes. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

(1) Demographic ratio: ~17:1 enslaved to white in Saint-Domingue. (2) Leadership: Toussaint Louverture's military and political skill. (3) Disease: ~50,000 French soldiers died of yellow fever and malaria 1802–1803. (4) Timing: French Revolution's 1794 abolition gave revolutionary legitimacy; Napoleon's preoccupation with European wars; Anglo-French rivalry created openings. (5) Solidarity: enslaved and free people of colour mobilised together; Vodou networks provided organisation. Marks: 1 per cause, up to 4.

9
[4]
Why did the 1848 revolutions fail in most of continental Europe? Provide three structural reasons. 4-6 sentences.
Answer Key

(1) Coalition fragmentation: liberals and nationalists allied with workers in spring, split when workers demanded social reforms (June Days, 4,000 dead in Paris). (2) Royal armies remained loyal; once monarchs recovered nerve they used them. (3) Divided nationalisms with overlapping territorial claims (Czech, German, Hungarian, Italian). (4) Rural conservatism: peasants withdrew once feudal obligations were abolished. (5) International intervention (Russia helped Habsburgs crush Hungary 1849). Marks: 1 each for three reasons, 1 for synthesis.

Write one PEEL paragraph (6-8 sentences) on the following claim: "The Industrial Revolution transformed Britain's material life but kept much of its social hierarchy intact." Include specific evidence (a date, a quantity, or a named act).
Answer Key

Strong responses: P — material transformation but social-hierarchy persistence; E — specific evidence such as factory acts (1833, 1847), Reform Acts (1832, 1867), urbanisation rates (Britain became majority-urban by 1851), per-capita-income growth, persistence of aristocratic landholding into the 20th century; E — interpretation linking facts; L — back to thesis. 6 marks: 1 Point, 2 Evidence, 2 Explanation, 1 Link/conventions.

11
[3]
Rewrite this weak thesis: "The new imperialism was bad for African people."
Answer Key

Sample: "The new imperialism transferred political sovereignty across Africa from local rulers to European powers in less than 30 years, with extreme variation in violence — from the Belgian Congo's perhaps 10 million dead under Leopold II's rubber regime to relatively bureaucratic French Senegal — and consistently extractive economic structures that pre-empted indigenous development." Look for: specific claim (1), engages variation (1), debatable (1).

12
[3]
In a short paragraph (4 sentences), contrast Meiji Japan's response to Western pressure with Qing China's. Why did Japan adapt more rapidly?
Answer Key

Both faced "opening" by Western powers around mid-century (Perry's squadron 1853; Opium Wars 1839–1842, 1856–1860). Japan's Meiji oligarchs (1868) launched coordinated industrial, military, and educational modernisation; China's reform efforts (Self-Strengthening, Hundred Days 1898) were slower and overcome by foreign pressure, internal rebellion (Taiping 1851–1864), and provincial fragmentation. Likely factors: Japan's smaller population, more cohesive elite, defensible geography, and timing within the window of European modernisation. 3 marks: 1 each for Japan's path, China's path, and the contrast.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[5]
Apply the historical-thinking concept of ethical dimension to King Leopold II's Congo Free State (1885–1908). What ethical judgement can a historian defensibly make, and on what evidence? 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

The Congo Free State was a personal possession of King Leopold II (Belgian sovereign acting as a private actor) administered by his Force Publique. Rubber-quota enforcement included widespread mutilation (cut hands as proof of executions). Estimates of excess deaths from violence, starvation, and disease range from 5 to 10 million (Adam Hochschild's King Leopold's Ghost, 1998). Documentation: E. D. Morel's investigative journalism, Roger Casement's 1904 British consular report, and Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) recorded the atrocities. The international Congo Reform Association mobilised between 1903 and 1913 was the world's first major human-rights campaign of the 20th century. Belgium took over administration in 1908; Leopold's culpability is settled. By any defensible standard — including standards articulated at the time by African and European critics — the regime committed crimes against humanity. The ethical-dimension move is to make this judgement evidence-based and to acknowledge that contemporaries (not only later historians) named it. Marks: 2 for evidence, 2 for judgement, 1 for communication.

14
[4]
Apply the historical-thinking concept of continuity and change to women's lives in industrialising Britain between 1780 and 1900. 5-7 sentences.
Answer Key

Changes: women entered the factory and textile workforce in large numbers (Lancashire mills majority female); urbanisation; legal property rights for married women (Married Women's Property Acts 1870, 1882); access to higher education (London, Cambridge from 1869); the early suffrage movement (Lydia Becker, Millicent Fawcett). Continuities: women could not vote in parliamentary elections until 1918 (full equality 1928); paid less than men for equivalent work; primary responsibility for domestic labour; limited access to most professions; legal subordination within marriage. A defensible answer holds both, recognising substantive change but also durable structures. 4 marks: 2 changes, 2 continuities.

15
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: The Industrial Revolution began the long process of fossil-fuel-driven economic growth that the climate crisis now demands we constrain. Briefly discuss what historians' tools (causation, continuity-change, ethical dimension) contribute to thinking about climate policy today. 3-4 sentences.
Answer Key

Causation: historians can trace cumulative emissions back to specific national industrialisation patterns (Britain 1780s, US 1860s, China 1980s), informing debates about historical responsibility. Continuity-change: emissions have been continuous over 240 years while political consciousness of the problem is much newer. Ethical dimension: intergenerational justice (decisions now affect people not yet born), distributive justice (emissions concentrated in rich countries; consequences fall heavily on poor ones). These are historical-thinking moves applied to a present problem. 3 marks: 1 for each concept's application.
