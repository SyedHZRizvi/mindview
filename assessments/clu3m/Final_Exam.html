Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[3]B · Heritage
Define each: (a) rule of law; (b) common law; (c) stare decisis.
Answer Key

(a) Rule of law: government and individuals equally subject to publicly known law, applied predictably (Reference re Secession of Quebec). (b) Common law: judge-made law developed through case-by-case decisions; binding on lower courts via stare decisis. (c) Stare decisis: "let the decision stand" — lower courts bound by higher-court rulings on the same point of law.

2
[3]B · Constitution
Identify what each does: (a) Constitution Act, 1867; (b) Constitution Act, 1982; (c) section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
Answer Key

(a) Created Canada in 1867; ss. 91–92 divide federal/provincial legislative powers. (b) Patriated the Constitution from the UK Parliament; entrenched the Charter; included a new amending formula. (c) Recognises and affirms "the existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada."

3
[3]C · Charter
List the four fundamental freedoms in section 2 of the Charter and give one example of each.
Answer Key

s. 2(a) conscience and religion (wearing a religious symbol); s. 2(b) thought/belief/opinion/expression including the press (publishing a critical editorial); s. 2(c) peaceful assembly (a public protest); s. 2(d) association (joining a union).

4
[3]D · Crime
Define each: (a) actus reus; (b) mens rea; (c) hybrid offence.
Answer Key

(a) The guilty act — physical conduct, circumstances, consequence; must be voluntary. (b) The guilty mind — the mental element (intention, knowledge, recklessness, wilful blindness, criminal negligence). (c) Dual-procedure offence; Crown elects to proceed summarily or by indictment.

5
[3]E · Civil
List the four elements of a negligence claim.
Answer Key

(1) Duty of care (Anns/Cooper); (2) breach of standard of care; (3) causation ("but for"); (4) damages not too remote and compensable.

6
[2]C · Charter
Under section 33 of the Charter, a notwithstanding declaration expires after:
Answer Key

C. 5 years; can be re-enacted, but the legislature must do so each time, forcing renewed political accountability.

7
[2]A · Inquiry
The IRAC framework stands for:
Answer Key

B. IRAC = Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion — the canonical legal-analysis framework.

8
[3]D · Sentencing
Identify three purposes of sentencing under s. 718 of the Criminal Code.
Answer Key

Any three of: denunciation; deterrence (general/specific); rehabilitation; separation from society; reparation; promotion of responsibility.

9
[3]B · Indigenous Law
Briefly explain what R v Gladue [1999] and section 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code require of sentencing judges.
Answer Key

Section 718.2(e): all available sanctions other than imprisonment should be considered, with particular attention to Indigenous offenders. Gladue interpreted this to require consideration of (a) unique systemic/background factors affecting Indigenous offenders (residential schools, intergenerational trauma, displacement); (b) sentencing approaches consistent with the offender's heritage. Ipeelee [2012] reaffirmed and applied this to all offences.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
10
[8]C/D · Cross-Strand IRAC
Constable Ngo pulls Marcus over at 11 pm for a brief traffic check. The officer smells alcohol, arrests Marcus for impaired driving (s. 320.14 Criminal Code), places him in the cruiser, and immediately begins questioning him about how much he had to drink. Marcus has not been told he can call a lawyer. Marcus answers and inculpates himself.
Apply IRAC. Identify Charter section(s) breached, the leading case(s), and the likely s. 24(2) remedy.
Answer Key

Issue: did the police breach s. 10(b) by questioning Marcus before informing him of his right to counsel and giving him a reasonable opportunity to contact one? Rule: s. 10(b) requires (i) informational duty, (ii) implementational duty (reasonable opportunity), (iii) cessation of questioning until consult — R v Manninen [1987], R v Bartle [1994]. Application: Officer Ngo skipped (i) and (iii). Breach established. s. 24(2) Grant: seriousness of breach high (squarely on officer); impact significant (self-incriminating statements); offence not violent. Conclusion: exclusion of statements likely. Crown still has breath samples and observations. Marks: 1 issue, 2 rule (sections + case), 3 application + Grant, 2 conclusion + remedy.

11
[6]C · Charter / Section 1
Suppose Ontario passes a law banning all advertising of vaping products by retailers. A vaping retailer challenges the law as a s. 2(b) Charter violation.
Apply the Oakes test to assess whether the law is justified under s. 1. Address each of the five steps.
Answer Key

(1) Prescribed by law — yes, a statute. (2) Pressing and substantial — yes, protecting youth and public health is pressing (analogous to RJR-MacDonald, JTI-Macdonald). (3) Rational connection — yes, advertising restrictions plausibly reduce uptake. (4) Minimal impairment — depends on scope; a blanket ban on all advertising may be broader than necessary if alternatives (age-gated, factual-only) could achieve much of the goal — could fail here. (5) Proportionality of effects — public-health benefit weighed against commercial-expression cost (latter is core but less central to s. 2(b)). Likely upheld with possible minimal-impairment risk. Marks: 1 step × 5 + 1 conclusion.

12
[6]D · Sentencing
Devon, age 24, has pleaded guilty to break-and-enter into a dwelling house (s. 348 Criminal Code, indictable, max life). He has no record. He has begun voluntary opioid-addiction treatment. The victim was not home; theft of $1,200 in electronics; no violence.
Analyse the sentencing options under s. 718 and 718.2. What sentence is most consistent with the Criminal Code's principles? Justify with reference to specific principles.
Answer Key

s. 718 purposes: denunciation (dwelling entry serious); deterrence (general yes; specific addressed by treatment); rehabilitation (strongly weighted — age, no record, voluntary treatment); reparation possible. s. 718.1 proportionality: high gravity (dwelling) but moderated blameworthiness (addiction + treatment). s. 718.2: aggravating — dwelling, victim anxiety; mitigating — no record, treatment, age, plea. s. 718.2(d)(e): restraint — consider non-imprisonment options. Likely: conditional sentence under s. 742.1 (where statutory eligibility) of 12–18 months with strict conditions + probation + restitution. Marks: 2 principles identified, 2 application, 2 specific outcome + reasoning.

13
[5]A/B · Constitutional Interpretation
A new federal statute purports to regulate the sale of bicycles to minors, requiring a federal permit and creating a federal offence.
Apply a pith-and-substance / division-of-powers analysis (s. 91/92 Constitution Act, 1867). Is the statute likely valid? Why or why not?
Answer Key

Pith and substance: dominant purpose appears to be consumer/youth protection in retail sale of a chattel — a property-and-civil-rights matter under s. 92(13), provincial. The federal "criminal law" head (s. 91(27)) requires prohibition + penalty + valid criminal-law purpose (peace, order, security, health, morality) — but a permit-and-permission regime for a non-dangerous product is regulatory not criminal. Likely the federal statute is ultra vires; provincial jurisdiction applies. Cf. Reference re Firearms Act [2000] which upheld federal firearms registration as criminal-law because firearms are dangerous to public safety. Bicycles do not carry that same characteristic. Marks: 1 pith-and-substance, 2 head-of-power analysis, 1 case reasoning, 1 conclusion.

[15]A/C/D · Major Legal Position Paper
Major Task — Write a legal position paper (500–700 words) on ONE of the following prompts:

Option 1: "Section 33 of the Charter — the notwithstanding clause — is essential to Canadian democracy because it preserves the dialogue between courts and legislatures. Pre-emptive use, however, threatens its dialogue function." Argue for or against this thesis. Use at least two real Canadian examples (e.g., Quebec Bill 21, Ontario Bill 28, Quebec sign-language law).
Option 2: "The Youth Criminal Justice Act appropriately balances accountability and rehabilitation for young offenders." Argue for or against, with reference to specific YCJA provisions and the principles in R v DB [2008] (reduced moral blameworthiness).
Option 3: "Access to justice in Canada is a crisis." Argue for or against, with specific reference to barriers, institutions, and possible reforms.

Include: introduction with thesis, 2–3 body paragraphs (each PEEL/IRAC structure), counter-argument paragraph, conclusion. Embed at least 3 legal authorities with proper attribution.
Marking Rubric

K/U (3): correct legal facts (case names, statute sections, principles). Thinking (4): clear thesis; argument grounded in legal evidence; specific examples; counter-argument addressed. Communication (5): clear introduction with thesis, well-organised paragraphs (PEEL/IRAC), embedded authorities with citation, conclusion synthesises rather than restates, conventions. Application (3): transfer of legal-analysis vocabulary; relevance of evidence to thesis. For Option 1 strong response cites Bill 21 (Hak), Bill 28, Ford v Quebec (AG), the dialogue/abdication debate, and Hogg's scholarly framing. For Option 2 strong response cites R v DB [2008], YCJA ss. 3, 6, 10, 39, 64, and ratio of presumption of youth sentence. For Option 3 strong response cites cost data, McLachlin's writings, OCLNP 2010 statistics, paralegals, community clinics, Vavilov framework.

15
[5]A · Citation & Communication
Identify and correct the citation/legal-vocabulary error in each sentence:
(a) "R v Oakes was decided by the Ontario Court of Appeal in 1986."
(b) "The defendant is presumed guilty until proven innocent in Canadian criminal law."
(c) "Section 33 of the Charter can be used to override any Charter section."
(d) "A 'tort' is a criminal wrong against the state."
(e) "Judges are bound by the obiter dicta of higher courts."
Answer Key

(a) Wrong court — R v Oakes was decided by the Supreme Court of Canada, [1986] 1 SCR 103. (b) The presumption is innocence until proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt (s. 11(d) Charter). (c) Section 33 can override ss. 2 and 7–15 only — NOT ss. 3–6 (democratic), 16–23 (language), or 28 (gender). (d) A tort is a civil wrong, actionable by an injured private party — not a criminal wrong. (e) Judges are bound by the ratio decidendi, not the obiter dicta (which are persuasive only).

16
[5]A · Plain Language
In plain language (4–6 sentences) for a friend who has never studied law, explain the difference between a criminal case and a civil case. Use concrete examples and accurate vocabulary.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) parties — criminal: Crown (state) vs accused; civil: plaintiff vs defendant. (2) purpose — criminal: punish wrongdoing against society; civil: compensate the injured party. (3) burden of proof — criminal: beyond a reasonable doubt; civil: balance of probabilities. (4) remedies — criminal: imprisonment, fine, probation; civil: damages, injunction, specific performance. (5) Both can arise from the same event (e.g., impaired-driving collision: criminal charge + civil lawsuit). Marks: 1 each.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
17
[8]E · Civil Application
Helena, 47, was a senior marketing manager for 9 years at a mid-sized Ontario firm. Her contract has no enforceable termination clause (failed under Waksdale). One Monday she is told her position is "eliminated"; the employer offers 8 weeks' pay in lieu — the ESA minimum.
Apply employment law to Helena's situation. Identify (a) her ESA entitlements; (b) her common-law entitlements (Bardal factors); (c) her duty to mitigate; (d) likely range of reasonable notice in months.
Answer Key

(a) ESA: 9 years of service → 8 weeks' notice/pay (s. 57). If employer payroll > $2.5M, also 9 weeks' severance (s. 64) = 1 week per year. (b) Common-law Bardal: age 47 (mid-career; difficult to re-enter); 9 years' service; senior/specialised role; availability of similar positions. Typical range: 10–14 months. (c) Duty to mitigate: Helena must reasonably search for comparable employment; earnings during the notice period reduce damages; documentation matters. (d) Likely outcome: 10–12 months reasonable notice + ESA-mandated severance; total compensation could be $110,000–$140,000 less mitigation earnings. Marks: 2 ESA, 2 common-law, 2 mitigation, 2 specific range with reasoning.

18
[6]A · Statutory Interpretation
A municipal by-law says: "No person shall ride a 'wheeled conveyance' on the boardwalk between 8 am and 8 pm." A list of examples in the by-law gives "bicycles, scooters, and skateboards." Three people are charged: (a) someone in a wheelchair; (b) a parent pushing a baby stroller; (c) someone on roller-skates.
Apply Driedger's modern principle and at least one canon of construction (e.g., ejusdem generis). For each person, are they guilty? Why?
Answer Key

Text: "wheeled conveyance" ordinarily means a device with wheels used for transportation. Context: the listed examples (bicycles, scooters, skateboards) are recreational/transport devices typically operated by a rider. Purpose: safety of pedestrians on the boardwalk. Ejusdem generis: "wheeled conveyance" narrowed by the recreational/transport class. (a) Wheelchair — a mobility device, not within the class; statutory interpretation should also favour Charter-consistent reading (s. 15 equality + Human Rights Code) — not guilty. (b) Baby stroller — not within the class (transport of a non-passenger, slow speed) and excluding mobility-related devices is consistent with purpose — not guilty. (c) Roller-skates — within the class of recreational/transport devices, ridden by a person at speed; guilty. Marks: 1 framework, 2 ejusdem generis application, 1 each (a)(b)(c) plus Charter values.

[5]B/C · Indigenous Rights & Charter
An Indigenous community has begun running a traditional dispute-resolution circle for minor criminal matters with the consent of the accused and the community.
Apply your knowledge of Indigenous legal traditions, the heritage of Canadian law, and ss. 718.2(e) Criminal Code + section 35 Constitution Act. In 5–7 sentences, explain how this circle fits within the framework of Canadian law, identify one legal benefit, and identify one legal complication.
Answer Key

Strong responses: traditional circles are an Indigenous legal practice with deep roots (oral tradition, restorative orientation, relational obligation). They fit within Canadian law through (a) s. 718.2(e) requiring courts to consider Indigenous sentencing approaches, (b) R v Gladue requiring consideration of culturally appropriate sanctions, (c) s. 35 protections, and (d) post-2008 TRC Calls to Action. Benefits: lower re-offence, healing-focus, community accountability, ownership. Complications: jurisdictional overlap with Criminal Code; charter rights (e.g., right to counsel, right to a public trial); potential lack of recognition by the Crown; resourcing. Marks: 1 heritage, 1 statutory basis, 1 benefit, 1 complication, 1 communication.
