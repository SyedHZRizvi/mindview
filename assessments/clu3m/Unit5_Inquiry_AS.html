Topic 5.1–5.2 — Inquiry & Case Briefs
Question 1
Which of the following is a primary legal source?
Solution: The Criminal Code is a statute — a primary source (binding law). Wikipedia, op-eds, and textbooks are secondary (commentary or summary, useful as starting points but not binding). Other primary sources include the Constitution, regulations, and case decisions.
Question 2
In a case brief, the ratio decidendi is:
Solution: Ratio decidendi = the binding legal principle (the rule the court actually applied). Obiter dicta = remarks made in passing, persuasive only. Distinguishing them is essential for understanding what a case stands for in future litigation.
Question 3
Which database is the free standard Canadian legal-information resource?
Solution: CanLII (canlii.org) is the free database for Canadian case law, statutes, and many tribunal decisions. The Supreme Court of Canada also hosts its decisions on its own site. Paid databases (LexisNexis, Westlaw) are also available but CanLII is the free standard.
Topic 5.3–5.4 — IRAC and Statutory Interpretation
Question 4
In the IRAC framework, the four steps in order are:
Solution: IRAC = Issue (the legal question), Rule (the controlling principle with its source), Application (apply the rule to the specific facts), Conclusion (the answer to the issue). Variations include IREAC, CREAC, FIRAC — the core logic is the same.
Question 5
Driedger's "modern principle" of statutory interpretation requires reading words in:
Solution: Driedger's modern principle, adopted in Rizzo & Rizzo Shoes [1998]: "the words of an Act are to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, the object of the Act, and the intention of Parliament." Text + context + purpose, together.
Question 6
A regulation says: "It is an offence to operate a 'vehicle' in the park without a permit." The list of examples in the same Act gives "cars, motorcycles, and other vehicles."
Which interpretive canon helps you decide whether a baby stroller is a "vehicle"?
Solution: Ejusdem generis ("of the same kind") — the general word "vehicles" is interpreted in light of the listed examples (motorised conveyances). A baby stroller is not motorised; it would likely fall outside. A horse is harder. The canon narrows the general word.
Topic 5.5–5.6 — Mooting and Access to Justice
Question 7
A "factum" in legal practice is:
Solution: A factum is the formal written brief filed in appellate proceedings: Part I (overview + facts), Part II (issues), Part III (argument — usually 20–30 pages), Part IV (order requested), Part V (list of authorities, table of contents). Mooters prepare a factum before oral argument.
Question 8
"Access to justice" in Canadian law refers most directly to:
Solution: Access to justice is the principle that the legal system should be practically available to all. Former Chief Justice McLachlin called it one of the most serious challenges facing the rule of law. Barriers include cost, geography, language, complexity, and legal literacy. Solutions include legal aid, community legal clinics, paralegals, pro bono, self-help resources, and simplified procedures.
Question 9
Which of the following is a tool of statutory interpretation?
Solution: Noscitur a sociis — "a word is known by the company it keeps" — is a canon of statutory interpretation. Words take on meaning from surrounding words. The other options are different doctrines: habeas corpus (right to challenge detention), mens rea (guilty mind), stare decisis (binding precedent).
Question 10
Effective oral appellate advocacy generally begins with:
Solution: A roadmap is the conventional opening of appellate argument: "My Lords, I will address two issues today: first … second …". It orients the bench, sets your structure, and ensures key arguments are made early in case of interruption.
