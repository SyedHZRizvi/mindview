Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
List the four fundamental freedoms in section 2 of the Charter and give one brief example of each.
Answer Key

(a) s. 2(a) — conscience and religion (e.g., wearing a religious symbol); (b) s. 2(b) — thought, belief, opinion, and expression, including the press (e.g., publishing a critical newspaper editorial); (c) s. 2(c) — peaceful assembly (e.g., a public protest); (d) s. 2(d) — association (e.g., joining a union or political party).

2
[3]
Identify three rights protected by sections 7–14 of the Charter (legal rights) and briefly explain each.
Answer Key

Three of: s. 7 life, liberty, security of the person + principles of fundamental justice; s. 8 protection from unreasonable search and seizure; s. 9 protection from arbitrary detention; s. 10(b) right to retain and instruct counsel on arrest; s. 11(b) trial within a reasonable time; s. 11(d) presumption of innocence; s. 12 protection from cruel and unusual punishment.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) enumerated grounds; (b) analogous grounds; (c) the duty to accommodate.
Answer Key

(a) Enumerated grounds = the grounds explicitly listed in s. 15(1) of the Charter: race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, mental or physical disability. (b) Analogous grounds = personal characteristics, often immutable, found by courts to be analogous to enumerated grounds — sexual orientation, marital status, citizenship, Aboriginal off-reserve residence. (c) Duty to accommodate = obligation under the Human Rights Code (and analogously under s. 15) to take reasonable steps to accommodate protected groups to the point of undue hardship (BCGSEU/Meiorin).

4
[2]
Section 33 of the Charter — the notwithstanding clause — has a sunset clause. How long is the maximum duration of a single declaration?
Answer Key

C. Section 33(3) provides that a notwithstanding declaration expires after 5 years; it can be re-enacted, but the re-enactment is a fresh political act each time, forcing accountability.

5
[2]
Name the section of the Charter that "guarantees the rights and freedoms set out in it subject only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society."
Answer Key

Section 1. This is the "reasonable limits" or "justification" clause; the Supreme Court's structured test for s. 1 was set out in R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
In 2023, a fictional municipality of "Lakeside" passes a by-law prohibiting any "political assembly of more than 10 persons" in the municipal park between 6 pm and 10 pm. The municipality cites concerns about noise complaints.
Conduct a Charter analysis: (a) identify the Charter sections engaged; (b) apply the Oakes test to determine whether the by-law is justified under s. 1.
Answer Key

(a) Sections engaged: s. 2(b) freedom of expression; s. 2(c) peaceful assembly. (b) Oakes: (i) prescribed by law — yes, a municipal by-law; (ii) pressing and substantial — noise control is legitimate but a numerical and time cap targeted only at political assemblies (not, say, weddings or concerts) is suspect; (iii) rational connection — questionable that 10 people is the noise threshold; (iv) minimal impairment — fails: less restrictive measures (decibel limits, viewpoint-neutral assembly rules) would achieve the same end; (v) proportionality — the cost to political speech significantly outweighs the marginal noise benefit. The by-law likely fails Oakes. Marks: 1 sections engaged, 1 each Oakes step.

7
[5]
Marwa, a Muslim woman who wears a hijab, applies for a teaching position in Quebec. Quebec's Bill 21 prohibits public-sector workers in positions of authority from wearing religious symbols at work. Bill 21 invokes section 33 of the Charter.
Analyse Marwa's situation. Address (a) which Charter rights are engaged; (b) the effect of the s. 33 declaration; (c) any remaining constitutional avenues.
Answer Key

(a) Rights engaged: s. 2(a) freedom of religion; s. 15 equality (disproportionate impact on religious-minority and women employees). (b) Effect of s. 33: the Quebec statute operates "notwithstanding" sections 2 and 7–15 — Marwa cannot succeed on a s. 2(a) or s. 15 challenge as long as the override is in force (5-year sunset). (c) Remaining avenues: s. 33 does NOT cover s. 28 (gender-equality guarantee) — the Quebec Court of Appeal in Hak 2024 QCCA 154 considered this; minority-language education and democratic-rights sections also fall outside the override. Marwa might also challenge under the Quebec Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms (provincial human rights statute), s. 35 if applicable, and bring political/public-accountability pressure. Marks: 1 each section engaged, 2 s. 33 effect, 2 remaining avenues.

8
[3]
Explain how Vriend v Alberta [1998] used the technique of "reading in" to remedy a Charter breach. Why was this remedy chosen instead of striking down the statute?
Answer Key

Alberta's IRPA failed to list sexual orientation among the prohibited grounds of discrimination — under-inclusive. The Court found this under-inclusion violated s. 15. Rather than strike down the entire IRPA (which would leave everyone — including currently-protected groups — without any protection), the Court "read in" sexual orientation as a protected ground. Reading in repaired the under-inclusion while preserving the rest of the protection. Strike-down would have produced a worse outcome — the alternative remedy was Charter-required. Marks: 1 under-inclusion identified, 1 reading in described, 1 why preferred to striking down.

Write a one-page (300–500 word) op-ed explaining either: (a) why section 33 of the Charter is necessary, OR (b) why it is dangerous. Address counter-arguments.
Marking Rubric

K/U (1) — accurate description of s. 33 (5-year sunset, restricted scope, explicit invocation required). Thinking (2) — clear argument grounded in evidence; specific examples (Bill 21, Bill 28). Communication (2) — coherent structure (claim → evidence → counter-argument → conclusion), conventions. Application (1) — connects to a current Canadian context. Strong responses on either side acknowledge the dialogue/abdication debate explicitly.

10
[6]
Write a clear, plain-language explanation (one paragraph) for a Grade 9 student of the five steps of the Oakes test. Number them.
Marking Rubric

Look for accurate identification of: (1) prescribed by law — the limit must be in legislation, regulation, or a clear common-law rule; (2) pressing and substantial objective — the goal must be important; (3) rational connection — the means must actually advance the goal; (4) minimal impairment — least restrictive reasonable option; (5) proportionality of effects — benefits outweigh costs. Marks: 1 per step + 1 for clear plain-language adaptation.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[6]
Constable Singh stops Marcus at 11 pm for a traffic check, smells alcohol on his breath, arrests him for impaired driving, and immediately begins questioning him in the police cruiser. Marcus has not yet been told about his right to a lawyer.
Apply the Charter to this scenario. Identify the Charter section(s) breached, the leading case, and the likely remedy under s. 24(2).
Answer Key

Sections breached: s. 10(b) — Constable Singh has not informed Marcus of his right to counsel and is questioning him before he has had a reasonable opportunity to contact a lawyer. R v Manninen [1987] establishes the informational and implementational duties. Section 24(2) Grant framework: (1) seriousness of the Charter-infringing conduct (failure to read 10(b) rights is serious); (2) impact on the accused's rights (significant — self-incriminating statements); (3) society's interest in adjudication on the merits (modest where the breach is procedural and other evidence — breath samples, observations — is available). Likely remedy: exclusion of the statements made in the cruiser. Marks: 2 section identification, 2 leading case (Manninen), 2 s. 24(2) analysis.

12
[6]
STSE / Real-World Application: Explain in 6–8 sentences how the Charter has changed Canadian society since 1982. Use at least three concrete examples (cases or laws) and discuss one perspective (Indigenous, immigrant, LGBTQ2S+, disability, religious-minority) on the Charter's effect.
Answer Key

Strong responses include specific examples like: striking down the 1969 abortion provisions in R v Morgentaler [1988]; legalising same-sex marriage following provincial appellate decisions (Halpern 2003) and the Civil Marriage Act 2005; Carter v Canada [2015] on medical assistance in dying; Vriend [1998] reading in sexual orientation; R v Big M Drug Mart [1985] on freedom of religion. Perspective: e.g., LGBTQ2S+ — Charter has been a primary engine of equality; Indigenous — Charter rights complement but are sometimes in tension with s. 35; religious minorities — Charter has both enabled accommodation (e.g., Multani) and faced restrictions (Bill 21). Marks: 3 for three concrete examples, 2 for perspective, 1 communication.
