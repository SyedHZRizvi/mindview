Topic 2.1–2.2 — Architecture and Fundamental Freedoms
Question 1
The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms applies primarily to:
Solution: Section 32 provides the Charter applies to Parliament, the federal government, the legislatures, and the provincial governments. It does not directly bind private parties — those situations are usually governed by the Ontario Human Rights Code or other statutes (RWDSU v Dolphin Delivery).
Question 2
Section 2 of the Charter protects all of the following EXCEPT:
Solution: Section 2 lists conscience/religion (2(a)), expression (2(b)), peaceful assembly (2(c)), and association (2(d)). It does not protect a right to bear arms — Canada has no constitutional analogue to the US Second Amendment.
Question 3
A teacher is convicted under s. 319(2) of the Criminal Code for wilful promotion of hatred. He argues s. 319(2) infringes section 2(b) of the Charter.
In R v Keegstra [1990], the Supreme Court held that s. 319(2):
Solution: Hate speech is "expression" under Irwin Toy — so s. 319(2) infringes s. 2(b). However, the Court applied the Oakes test and found the limit reasonable: pressing objective (protecting groups, social cohesion), rationally connected, minimally impairing (high mens rea + s. 319(3) defences), and proportionate.
Topic 2.3–2.4 — Legal & Equality Rights
Question 4
Section 10(b) of the Charter requires that on arrest or detention, police must:
Solution: Section 10(b) requires (i) informational duty (inform), (ii) implementational duty (give a reasonable opportunity to contact counsel), and (iii) cessation of questioning until the detainee has had that opportunity — as set out in R v Manninen [1987] and R v Bartle [1994].
Question 5
In R v Jordan [2016] 1 SCR 631, the Supreme Court set presumptive ceilings for trial within reasonable time of:
Solution: Jordan set presumptive ceilings of 18 months for provincial-court trials and 30 months for superior-court trials, measured from charge to end of trial. Defence delay is subtracted; exceptional circumstances can justify exceeding the ceiling.
Question 6
In Vriend v Alberta [1998], the Supreme Court remedied a section 15 violation by:
Solution: The Court "read in" sexual orientation as a protected ground in Alberta's IRPA. Reading in is a judicial technique used where a statute is under-inclusive — adding protection rather than striking down what other groups already have.
Topic 2.5–2.6 — Section 1 Limits and Notwithstanding
Question 7
The Oakes test requires the government to prove (in order):
Solution: Chief Justice Dickson in Oakes set out: (1) prescribed by law, (2) pressing and substantial objective, (3) rational connection, (4) minimal impairment, (5) proportionality of effects. The Crown bears the burden at every step.
Question 8
Section 33 of the Charter (the notwithstanding clause) allows Parliament or a legislature to override:
Solution: Section 33 can override sections 2 and 7 through 15 only. It cannot override democratic rights (3–6), mobility (6 — partly), official languages and minority-language education (16–23), or the gender-equality guarantee in s. 28. It also has a 5-year sunset clause.
Question 9
Which case is the leading authority on section 1 of the Charter (the reasonable-limits clause)?
Solution: R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103 set out the structured test that has governed s. 1 analysis since 1986 across the common-law world.
Question 10
A high-school student wears a T-shirt to school with the message "Climate Strike — Walk Out Now." The school's dress code bans "political messaging."
Identify the Charter section most directly engaged:
Solution: Wearing a slogan T-shirt is expression conveying meaning, which under Irwin Toy [1989] is protected by s. 2(b). A school board is "government" for Charter purposes. The board would then have to justify the limit under s. 1 (likely failing minimal impairment).
