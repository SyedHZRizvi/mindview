Topic 1.1–1.2 — What is Law & Ancient Roots
Question 1
Which best describes the principle of the "rule of law"?
Solution: The rule of law means everyone — including the government — is subject to publicly known law, applied predictably. It does not require that laws be morally correct, only that they be applied equally; the Supreme Court treated this as an unwritten constitutional principle in Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998].
Question 2
The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE) is historically significant primarily because:
Solution: Hammurabi's significance is in form, not content. It was written, public, and applied to categories — so citizens could know in advance what conduct attracted what penalty. The principle of promulgation (publication so the law is knowable) descends from this innovation.
Question 3
The Magna Carta (1215) contributed which key principle to Canadian law?
Solution: Clause 39 of the Magna Carta required that no free person be detained "except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land." This is the deep ancestor of habeas corpus (Charter s. 10(c)) and trial by jury (s. 11(f)).
Topic 1.3 — Indigenous Legal Traditions
Question 4
Which statement about Indigenous legal traditions is most accurate?
Solution: Indigenous legal traditions are many — distinct legal orders developed by different nations over thousands of years. They have their own sources (oral tradition, ceremony, story), processes (often restorative), and pedagogies. Modern Canadian law increasingly recognises them (e.g., Gladue principles, Tsilhqot'in Nation, Bill C-92).
Question 5
R v Gladue [1999] established that sentencing judges must:
Solution: Gladue interpreted s. 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code to require sentencing judges to consider (a) the unique systemic/background factors affecting Indigenous offenders (residential schools, trauma, dislocation), and (b) sentencing approaches consistent with the offender's heritage. It is a corrective for systemic over-incarceration, not a "discount."
Topic 1.4–1.5 — Constitution & Sources
Question 6
Sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867 do what?
Solution: Sections 91 and 92 are the federal/provincial division-of-powers provisions. S. 91 lists federal heads (criminal law, banking, postal service, "Peace, Order, and good Government"); s. 92 lists provincial heads (property and civil rights, hospitals, municipalities, administration of justice in the province).
Question 7
What does "patriation" refer to in the Constitution Act, 1982?
Solution: Until 1982, Canada needed the UK Parliament's assent to amend the Constitution. Patriation moved that authority to Canada via the new amending formula (the "7/50 rule" generally), and the Constitution Act, 1982 simultaneously entrenched the Charter and recognised Aboriginal and treaty rights in s. 35.
Question 8
The doctrine of stare decisis means:
Solution: Stare decisis is the common-law doctrine that lower courts are bound by higher-court decisions on the same point of law in the same jurisdiction. It produces predictability and consistency. The Supreme Court can overrule its own prior decisions but rarely does.
Topic 1.6 — Courts & Legal Actors
Question 9
Which court is the final appellate court in Canada?
Solution: The Supreme Court of Canada (since 1949, when appeals to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council ended) is Canada's final court of appeal. It has 9 justices, including 3 from Quebec. Appeals generally require leave (permission), granted in only ~12% of leave applications.
Question 10
In the citation "R v Brown, 2022 SCC 18," what does "R" stand for?
Solution: "R" stands for Regina (Queen) or Rex (King) — the Crown, who prosecutes criminal cases. The citation "2022 SCC 18" is a neutral citation: year, Supreme Court of Canada, and the 18th decision released that year.
