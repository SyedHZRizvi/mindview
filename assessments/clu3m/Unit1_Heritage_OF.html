Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define the "rule of law" and identify the case in which the Supreme Court of Canada recognised it as an unwritten constitutional principle.
Answer Key

The rule of law means government and individuals are equally subject to publicly known law, applied predictably; no one is above the law and no one can be punished except for a breach of established law. The Supreme Court recognised it as one of four unwritten constitutional principles in Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 217 (alongside federalism, democracy, and protection of minorities).

2
[2]
Sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867 do what?
Answer Key

B. Section 91 lists federal heads of power (criminal law, banking, navigation, postal service, POGG); section 92 lists provincial heads (property and civil rights, hospitals, municipalities, administration of justice in the province).

3
[3]
Name and briefly describe three contributions to the heritage of Canadian law from before 1867.
Answer Key

Acceptable contributions (any three): Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE) — first public, written legal code; Mosaic Code — moral content + evidentiary practice (multiple witnesses); Roman law — codified in Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis; ancestor of civil-law systems; Magna Carta (1215) — constrained the king; ancestor of habeas corpus and jury trial; Indigenous legal traditions — distinct legal orders with oral, restorative, relational features; British common law — case-by-case judge-made precedent.

4
[3]
Distinguish (a) common law, (b) civil law, and (c) statute law as sources of Canadian law.
Answer Key

(a) Common law — judge-made law developed case-by-case; binding via stare decisis; English heritage; used in all provinces except Quebec for private law. (b) Civil law — codified law in the Civil Code of Quebec; reasoning deductive from general code provisions; descends from Roman law through the Napoleonic Code; used in Quebec for private matters. (c) Statute law — legislation passed by Parliament or a legislature, then signed into force; dominates modern law; displaces inconsistent common law where it conflicts.

5
[2]
Distinguish ratio decidendi from obiter dicta.
Answer Key

Ratio decidendi = the binding legal principle of a case (the rule the court actually applied to reach its decision). Obiter dicta = remarks made in passing that are not strictly part of the decision; persuasive only, not binding. Distinguishing the two is essential to figuring out what a case actually stands for.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Ontario passes a statute imposing fines on retailers who sell tobacco to anyone under 19. A retailer challenges the statute, arguing tobacco regulation is "criminal law" under s. 91(27) of the Constitution Act, 1867 — exclusively federal.
Analyse whether the Ontario statute is within provincial constitutional authority. Use a pith-and-substance approach.
Answer Key

Step 1 — Pith and substance: what is the law really about? The dominant purpose is consumer protection and public health (preventing youth tobacco use); the dominant effect is regulating who may purchase a product (an age-based restriction with administrative penalty). Step 2 — Head of power: criminal law (s. 91(27)) requires prohibition + penalty + valid criminal-law purpose. Provincial property-and-civil-rights authority (s. 92(13)) covers regulation of who may transact and on what terms; s. 92(7) (hospitals/health) and s. 92(16) (matters of a local nature) are supportive. Step 3 — Outcome: the statute sits naturally within provincial property-and-civil-rights and matters-of-a-local-nature authority. The fines are regulatory not criminal. The Supreme Court's "double aspect" doctrine permits both federal and provincial action on related matters (e.g., the federal Tobacco Act co-exists with provincial sales-age laws). Marks: 1 pith-and-substance identification, 2 head-of-power analysis, 1 doctrine, 1 conclusion.

7
[4]
A 17-year-old in Ontario reads about the Supreme Court of Canada's decision in R v Gladue [1999] 1 SCR 688 and concludes that "Indigenous offenders get lighter sentences."
Explain why this conclusion misunderstands Gladue. Give two reasons grounded in the case itself.
Answer Key

The conclusion is wrong because: (1) Gladue directs sentencing judges to consider the unique systemic and background factors affecting Indigenous offenders (residential schools, intergenerational trauma, displacement, poverty) — these are contextual sentencing factors required by s. 718.2(e), not a discount. (2) Gladue also requires consideration of sanctions consistent with the offender's heritage and community (restorative options, healing circles) — these are not necessarily "lighter," only different. (3) Indigenous offenders made up 5% of the adult population but 32%+ of federal inmates; Gladue is a corrective for systemic over-incarceration. (4) R v Ipeelee [2012] reaffirmed and strengthened these principles. Marks: 2 for each substantive reason.

8
[4]
Apply the doctrine of stare decisis to the following scenario: A 2024 Ontario small-claims judge is asked to decide whether a 2018 Ontario Court of Appeal decision on a tort matter is binding on her. Explain.
Answer Key

The 2024 small-claims judge is bound by the 2018 Ontario Court of Appeal decision on the same point of law, assuming the facts are substantially similar. Small Claims Court is a branch of the Superior Court of Justice; the Court of Appeal for Ontario sits above the Superior Court in the same hierarchy. Stare decisis binds lower courts to higher-court decisions in the same jurisdiction. The judge can distinguish (if facts differ materially) but cannot disregard. Decisions of higher courts in other provinces are persuasive but not binding. Marks: 1 binding statement, 1 hierarchy, 1 distinguishing option, 1 persuasive-vs-binding distinction.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write a short paragraph (5–7 sentences) explaining to a Grade 9 student what the Constitution Act, 1982 did. Include at least three specific changes it introduced and use accurate legal vocabulary.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) explanation of patriation — moving constitutional amending authority from the UK Parliament to Canada; (2) the new amending formula (7/50 rule and special unanimity); (3) entrenchment of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (Part I); (4) recognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights in section 35; (5) section 52(1) declaring the Constitution the supreme law. Bonus: mention that Quebec did not sign but the Constitution still applies in Quebec (Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998]). Marks: 1 patriation, 1 amending formula, 1 Charter, 1 s. 35, 1 supremacy clause, 1 vocabulary and conventions.

10
[6]
Read this case citation: R v Oakes, [1986] 1 SCR 103. Explain in detail what each element of the citation tells you about the case (who, what court, when, where to find it).
Answer Key

R = Regina, indicating a criminal case in which the Crown is a party (here, the Crown is the appellant). v = versus. Oakes = the named party (here, the respondent David Oakes). [1986] = year of decision (square brackets indicate the year is needed to identify the volume — typical for cases reported in court-curated reporters). 1 = volume number. SCR = Supreme Court Reports (Canada's official Supreme Court reporter). 103 = first page. To find it: search "R v Oakes 1986 SCR 103" on CanLII, the SCC website, or any legal database. The citation also tells you the case is a Supreme Court of Canada decision, therefore binding on all lower courts in Canada. Marks: 1 each of R/v/Oakes/year/volume/SCR/page (capped at 6).

Part D: Application [12 marks]
11
[6]
In 2024, a small village in Ontario passes a by-law requiring shopkeepers to pay a $25 "doorway-cleaning fee" each year. A shopkeeper refuses, arguing the by-law is invalid.
Apply Canadian law to determine whether the by-law is valid. Identify the source of the municipality's authority, the constitutional issue (if any), and one practical defence the shopkeeper might raise.
Answer Key

A municipality is a creature of provincial statute. Ontario's Municipal Act, 2001 grants municipalities authority to pass by-laws for local matters. A "doorway-cleaning fee" arguably falls within municipal authority over local public works (s. 11 of the Municipal Act). Constitutional issues: the by-law operates under provincial property-and-civil-rights authority (s. 92(13) Constitution Act, 1867) and matters of a local nature (s. 92(16)). It does not appear to invade federal jurisdiction. Defence: the shopkeeper could argue (a) the by-law was not properly passed under the Municipal Act; (b) it is ultra vires the municipality's enabling powers; (c) it is unreasonable or discriminatory; (d) it is a disguised tax (municipalities have limited taxation authority). Most likely the by-law is valid if properly enacted; the shopkeeper's best argument is procedural irregularity. Marks: 2 source of authority, 2 constitutional placement, 2 defence + reasoning.

12
[6]
STSE / Real-World Application: The Supreme Court of Canada in Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia [2014] 2 SCR 257 recognised Aboriginal title to a specific tract of land for the first time. In 6–8 sentences, explain why this decision matters for the heritage of Canadian law. Use at least two of the concepts of legal thinking (legal significance, continuity and change, cause and consequence, legal perspective).
Answer Key

Strong responses use the concepts deliberately. Legal significance: Tsilhqot'in is the first Supreme Court declaration of Aboriginal title to specific land, completing a doctrinal path from Calder [1973] and Delgamuukw [1997]. Continuity and change: change — the Court recognised an Indigenous nation's pre-existing legal understanding of land as constitutionally protected under s. 35; continuity — the case fits within the long s. 35 jurisprudential trajectory. Cause and consequence: the decision has reshaped Indigenous-Crown negotiations across BC and beyond and influenced the legal recognition of Indigenous legal traditions. Legal perspective: the case took the Tsilhqot'in's own legal understanding of their territory as evidence — a shift from settler-only constructions of land law. Marks: 2 for legal significance, 2 for continuity/change or cause/consequence, 1 for perspective, 1 for communication.
