Topic 4.1–4.2 — Tort Law and Contracts
Question 1
The four elements of a negligence claim in Canadian tort law are:
Solution: The four elements: (1) duty of care (Anns/Cooper framework), (2) breach of the standard of care, (3) causation (factual "but for" + legal), (4) damages that are not too remote and are compensable. Plaintiff bears the burden on each element on a balance of probabilities.
Question 2
Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] is famous for establishing:
Solution: Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle" — "you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour." Established that manufacturer-to-consumer duty of care exists without contractual privity. Foundation of modern negligence law.
Question 3
The five elements of a valid contract under common law are:
Solution: The five elements: (1) offer, (2) acceptance, (3) consideration, (4) capacity, (5) intention to create legal relations. Some contracts also require a particular form (writing under the Statute of Frauds). Vitiating factors (misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence, unconscionability) can render a contract voidable.
Topic 4.3–4.4 — Consumer & Employment
Question 4
Under Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, 2002, a consumer who signs a "direct agreement" at home can typically cancel without reason within:
Solution: Section 43 of the Ontario Consumer Protection Act, 2002 provides a 10-day cooling-off period for direct agreements (consumer contracts entered outside the supplier's place of business — e.g., door-to-door sales). The consumer can cancel for any reason within that period.
Question 5
Helena, 47, has been a senior marketing manager for 9 years at a mid-sized Ontario firm with no enforceable termination clause. She is dismissed without cause.
Her common-law entitlement to "reasonable notice" is governed by what test/factors?
Solution: Bardal v Globe & Mail Ltd. [1960] OWN 253: reasonable notice depends on the employee's age, length of service, character of employment (senior/specialised vs entry-level), and availability of similar employment. A senior manager at age 47 with 9 years of service typically receives 10–14 months reasonable notice.
Topic 4.5 — Family Law
Question 6
Under Ontario's Family Law Act, when a married couple separates, the spouse with the larger Net Family Property pays the other:
Solution: The equalisation payment is half the difference between the two spouses' NFPs. Each spouse calculates their NFP (assets minus debts minus pre-marriage property minus excluded property like inheritances), and the larger NFP pays half the difference. The matrimonial home is uniquely treated — its full value at separation enters NFP regardless of pre-marriage ownership.
Question 7
The federal Divorce Act provides that a divorce can be granted on the ground of "breakdown of marriage," which is proved by:
Solution: Section 8(2) of the Divorce Act lists three ways to prove breakdown: (a) one-year separation (now the standard "no-fault" ground), (b) adultery, (c) cruelty. The 1-year separation is overwhelmingly the route used in modern Canadian divorces.
Topic 4.6 — ADR & Tribunals
Question 8
Which of the following is NOT a standard form of Alternative Dispute Resolution?
Solution: A jury trial is litigation in the formal court system — NOT ADR. ADR is the family of processes (negotiation, mediation, arbitration) that resolve disputes outside formal trial procedure.
Question 9
Ontario's Small Claims Court handles civil claims up to:
Solution: Ontario Small Claims Court (a branch of the Superior Court of Justice) handles claims up to $35,000 (since 2020). Procedure is simplified; parties commonly self-represent or use paralegals. Above $35,000, claims proceed in the SCJ general civil track.
Question 10
Lia rents an apartment. Her landlord Marco refuses to fix the broken fridge. Lia withholds part of the rent. Marco applies to evict her.
Which forum has jurisdiction over this dispute?
Solution: Residential tenancy disputes in Ontario are within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Landlord and Tenant Board under s. 168 of the Residential Tenancies Act, 2006. The LTB can grant evictions, order rent abatement, order repairs, and award costs. Small Claims Court will decline jurisdiction for residential-tenancy matters.
