Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
A human cell entering meiosis has 46 chromosomes. How many chromosomes does each gamete have at the end of meiosis II?
Answer Key

B (23). Meiosis halves chromosome number from 46 (diploid) to 23 (haploid) in each of the four resulting gametes.

2
[3]
State THREE differences between mitosis and meiosis.
Answer Key

(1) Mitosis: one division → 2 daughter cells; Meiosis: two divisions → 4 daughter cells. (2) Mitosis: daughters genetically identical (diploid); Meiosis: daughters genetically unique (haploid). (3) Mitosis: no homolog pairing or crossing over; Meiosis I: homologs pair and undergo crossing over. (4) Mitosis: somatic cells; Meiosis: germ cells (gamete formation). (Any three.)

3
[2]
Two heterozygous tall plants (Tt) are crossed. What is the expected phenotypic ratio in the offspring?
Answer Key

C (3:1). Tt × Tt gives genotypic ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt and phenotypic ratio 3 tall : 1 short (because T is dominant).

4
[3]
Distinguish complete dominance, incomplete dominance, and codominance. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

Complete dominance: heterozygote looks identical to homozygous dominant (Mendelian peas — purple Pp = purple PP). Incomplete dominance: heterozygote shows blended intermediate (snapdragon red × white → pink). Codominance: both alleles fully expressed in heterozygote (MN blood-group, ABO heterozygote IAIB shows both antigens).

5
[3]
Define non-disjunction. Explain why it leads to aneuploidy in offspring, with one human example.
Answer Key

Non-disjunction: failure of homologous chromosomes (meiosis I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II) to separate properly. This produces gametes with too many or too few chromosomes. Fertilisation with a normal gamete yields aneuploid zygotes — extra (trisomy) or missing (monosomy) chromosomes. Example: trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) results when a gamete carries two copies of chromosome 21; the zygote has 47 chromosomes with three copies of chromosome 21. Other examples: Turner syndrome (XO, monosomy X), Klinefelter syndrome (XXY).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[4]
In Labrador retrievers, coat colour is controlled by two genes. Black (B) is dominant to brown (b), and a separate "expression" gene E (dominant) allows pigment to be deposited; ee dogs are yellow regardless of B/b. Two double-heterozygous dogs (BbEe × BbEe) are mated. What is the expected phenotypic ratio in the puppies? Show your reasoning.
Answer Key

This is epistasis (E is epistatic to B). Use the product rule. P(B_ | E present) = 3/4 black; P(bb | E present) = 1/4 brown. P(ee) = 1/4 yellow regardless. So: P(black) = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16; P(brown) = 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16; P(yellow) = 1 × 1/4 = 4/16. Final ratio = 9 black : 3 brown : 4 yellow. (Compare with classic 9:3:3:1; the 3+1 = 4 because yellow phenotype masks both B and bb.)

7
[5]
A man and woman, neither showing haemophilia, have an affected son and an unaffected daughter. The maternal grandfather was also affected. (a) State the inheritance pattern. (b) Give the genotype of every named family member using XH/Xh notation. (c) What is the probability that a future daughter will be a carrier?
Answer Key

(a) X-linked recessive. (b) Maternal grandfather: XhY (affected). Mother: XHXh (carrier — must have inherited Xh from affected father). Father: XHY (unaffected). Affected son: XhY. Unaffected daughter: 1/2 chance XHXH, 1/2 chance XHXh (status uncertain without testing). (c) Cross XHXh × XHY. Daughters: 1/2 XHXH (normal) and 1/2 XHXh (carrier). Probability a future daughter is a carrier = 1/2 = 0.5.

8
[4]
In tomatoes, red fruit (R) is dominant to yellow (r), and tall vine (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). A double-heterozygous plant (RrTt) is test-crossed to a homozygous-recessive plant (rrtt). What proportion of offspring would be (a) red and dwarf, (b) yellow and tall?
Answer Key

Test cross: RrTt × rrtt. Gametes from RrTt: RT, Rt, rT, rt — each at 1/4. Gametes from rrtt: rt. Offspring genotypes: 1/4 RrTt (red tall), 1/4 Rrtt (red dwarf), 1/4 rrTt (yellow tall), 1/4 rrtt (yellow dwarf). (a) Red dwarf = 1/4 = 25%. (b) Yellow tall = 1/4 = 25%. Test crosses yield a 1:1:1:1 ratio when independent assortment holds.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[4]
Explain Mendel's law of segregation and law of independent assortment, including the cellular events in meiosis that physically produce each law.
Answer Key

Law of segregation: each gamete receives only one allele for each gene. The cellular event is the separation of homologous chromosomes in anaphase I — each daughter cell receives one homolog (carrying one allele). Law of independent assortment: alleles of different genes are inherited independently. The cellular event is the random orientation of each homologous pair on the metaphase I spindle, so the chromosomes from different pairs are distributed independently into gametes. With n pairs there are \(2^n\) combinations. Independent assortment requires that the genes be on different chromosomes (or far apart on the same chromosome).

10
[4]
Explain how X-linked recessive disorders produce a characteristic pedigree pattern. Describe at least three clues you would look for in a pedigree to identify X-linked recessive inheritance.
Answer Key

X-linked recessive disorders: (1) Far more affected males than females — males are hemizygous, so a single Xh is enough; females need XhXh. (2) An affected male never transmits the trait to his sons (he passes Y, not X) but always to his daughters (who become at least carriers). (3) Affected females must have an affected father AND a mother who is at least a carrier (so are rare). (4) Carrier females (XHXh) pass the allele to half their sons (affected) and half their daughters (carriers). The trait can "skip" generations, appearing in maternal grandsons after a carrier mother. Examples: red-green colour blindness, haemophilia A, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

11
[4]
Describe gel electrophoresis: how it works, what it separates by, and one practical application.
Answer Key

Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size. DNA samples are loaded into wells in an agarose gel submerged in buffer. An electric field is applied; the negatively charged DNA (due to phosphate backbone) migrates toward the positive electrode. Smaller fragments move through the gel pores faster; larger fragments lag. After staining, the result is a ladder of bands. By comparing migration distances with a known-size ladder, the size of each fragment is read off. Applications: DNA fingerprinting (forensic identification, paternity testing), diagnosing genetic diseases (e.g., sickle-cell), separating PCR products in COVID-19 testing.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[4]
Application — Blood-transfusion case: A type-O patient receives type-A blood in an emergency. (a) Predict the clinical outcome and explain using ABO genetics and immunology. (b) Which is the universal-donor red-cell blood type?
Answer Key

(a) Type-O blood (genotype ii) has no A or B antigens on red cells but the recipient's plasma contains both anti-A and anti-B antibodies. Transfusing type-A red cells (with A antigen) into a type-O recipient triggers a violent immune-mediated agglutination and haemolytic transfusion reaction: clumping of red cells, kidney failure, possible death. (b) Type-O red cells are the universal donor (no A/B antigens) — although ideally a matched type is given because antibodies in the donor's plasma can still cause minor reactions; for plasma, type-AB is the universal donor.

13
[4]
STSE — Prenatal screening: A 40-year-old pregnant woman is offered non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT). (a) Why does maternal age matter for trisomy 21 risk? (b) Compare NIPT to amniocentesis in terms of accuracy and risk.
Answer Key

(a) The risk of trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) increases with maternal age because oocytes have been arrested in meiosis I since fetal development; over decades, the spindle and cohesin proteins that hold homologs together degrade, raising the rate of meiosis I non-disjunction. Risk: ~1/1500 at age 20, ~1/100 at age 40, ~1/30 at age 45. (b) NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing): analyses cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood, accuracy >99% for common trisomies (21, 18, 13), no risk to the pregnancy. Amniocentesis: samples amniotic fluid (cells from the fetus), highly accurate karyotype-based diagnosis, but invasive — approximate 0.1–0.5% miscarriage risk. NIPT is usually a screening test; positive NIPT results are typically confirmed by amniocentesis.

14
[4]
STSE — DNA fingerprinting in forensics: Police find a hair at a crime scene. The DNA profile is generated by PCR and gel electrophoresis. The bands match suspect A and not suspects B, C, or D. Discuss (a) the strength of this evidence and (b) one ethical concern about genetic databases.
Answer Key

(a) A match across many independent loci (STRs — short tandem repeats) gives very high statistical confidence, often quoted as random-match probabilities of 1 in a billion or rarer. The evidence is strong but not absolute: contamination, mishandling, partial profiles, and shared profiles in identical twins or close relatives can complicate interpretation. Other physical and circumstantial evidence is usually required for conviction. (b) Ethical concerns: (i) privacy — DNA contains far more information than is needed for identification, including disease-risk alleles; (ii) consent — police databases may include people never convicted, or family members whose DNA was uploaded to a commercial genealogy service; (iii) over-representation of certain communities in databases reflects systemic biases in policing; (iv) future misuse — DNA could be used to identify protest participants or refugees against their wishes. Strong governance and limits on data retention are needed.
