Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [12 marks]
1
[3]
List the five stages of the geographic inquiry process in order.
Answer Key

(1) Formulate questions. (2) Gather and organise information. (3) Interpret and analyse. (4) Evaluate and draw conclusions. (5) Communicate results. The cycle is iterative; new findings often loop back to question refinement and additional data gathering.

2
[2]
The four concepts of geographic thinking are:
Answer Key

A. Ontario CGW4U curriculum's four concepts. Each is a lens for framing inquiry questions and structuring analysis.

3
[3]
Define vector data and raster data in GIS, with one example of when each is preferable.
Answer Key

Vector: discrete features represented as points (cities), lines (roads, rivers), polygons (provinces). Preferred for clear boundaries and exact features. Raster: grid of cells, each holding a value. Preferred for continuous phenomena — elevation, temperature, NDVI, population density, satellite imagery.

4
[2]
Which classification scheme is generally best for skewed quantitative data on a choropleth map?
Answer Key

C. Natural breaks (Jenks) minimises within-class variance and is well suited to skewed distributions (most common in geographic data). Equal intervals work for uniform data; quantiles give equal visual emphasis but mask outliers.

5
[2]
Two thirds of CGW4U inquiry questions should engage at least how many concepts of geographic thinking?
Answer Key

B. A strong inquiry engages at least two of the four concepts; capstone inquiries often engage all four. Engaging only one yields descriptive (not analytical) work.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[5]
A student's draft question is "Is climate change bad?" Refine the question into a researchable geographic inquiry question, identify two concepts of geographic thinking it should engage, and list three data sources you would consult.
Answer Key

Refined question example: "How have the frequency and area-burned by wildfires in the Canadian boreal forest changed since 1990, and what adaptation strategies have Indigenous communities in northern Ontario implemented in response?" Concepts engaged: spatial significance, patterns & trends, interrelationships, geographic perspective (Indigenous, environmental). Sources: Canadian National Fire Database, NASA Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), peer-reviewed papers (e.g., Hanes et al., Canadian Journal of Forest Research), Indigenous Climate Hub reports, federal Canadian Wildland Fire Strategy 2019.

7
[4]
Given satellite pixel values: pixel A NIR = 0.60, RED = 0.10; pixel B NIR = 0.30, RED = 0.20; pixel C NIR = 0.10, RED = 0.15. Compute NDVI for each, classify each likely surface (vegetation, soil/built, water), and identify one limitation of relying on a single-date NDVI.
Answer Key

Pixel A: (0.60−0.10)/(0.60+0.10) = 0.50/0.70 ≈ 0.71 — dense vegetation. Pixel B: (0.30−0.20)/(0.30+0.20) = 0.10/0.50 = 0.20 — sparse vegetation or soil. Pixel C: (0.10−0.15)/(0.10+0.15) = −0.05/0.25 = −0.20 — water or built surface. Limitations: single-date NDVI cannot distinguish seasonal phenology (deciduous winter vs evergreen), can be obscured by cloud cover (Sentinel-2 5-day revisit reduces this), and atmospheric correction matters; time-series analysis is far more informative.

8
[3]
A student wants to map global GDP per capita on a single world map. Identify the best (a) map projection, (b) data classification, and (c) colour scheme, with one-sentence justification for each.
Answer Key

(a) Robinson or Equal Earth — preserves area better than Mercator, avoids exaggerating high latitudes. (b) Natural breaks (Jenks) — GDP per capita is skewed (a few very rich and many poor countries). (c) Sequential — light to dark in a single hue (e.g., light blue to dark blue) since GDP per capita is ordered; or diverging if anchored to the world median.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
In 250–350 words, write a thesis statement and three-claim outline for an inquiry on the question "Should Canada increase its peacekeeping contribution to UN missions?" Include a counter-argument.
Answer Key

Thesis example: "Canada should re-build its UN peacekeeping contribution selectively, focusing on training, intelligence, and policing missions rather than blue-helmet combat, because such contributions match contemporary peace-operation needs and Canadian capability while supporting multilateral diplomacy." Claim 1: current UN missions need specialist capabilities — engineering, medical, female peacekeepers. Claim 2: Canada's middle-power identity and diplomatic reach depend on visible UN engagement. Claim 3: a selective expansion is achievable within current defence budgets through repurposing. Counter-argument: UN missions have a mixed record (DRC, Mali, CAR), and combat casualties carry domestic political cost; rebuttal: training and capacity-building offer effectiveness with lower risk.

10
[4]
Draw or describe a labelled diagram of a choropleth map showing 5 classes of a quantitative variable. Include all required map elements.
Answer Key

Required elements: clear title (variable, place, time, e.g., "HDI by country, 2024"), legend with class breaks and colour swatches, north arrow, scale bar, data source ("UNDP HDI Report 2024"), projection note ("Robinson"), date of compilation, and credits. Five classes shaded in a sequential palette from light (low HDI) to dark (high HDI). Optional: inset for small island states or smaller regions for clarity.

11
[3]
Distinguish quantitative and qualitative data. Give one example of when a mixed-methods inquiry would be stronger than either alone.
Answer Key

Quantitative: numerical, suitable for statistical and cartographic analysis (e.g., census, GDP, satellite NDVI). Qualitative: interpretive, captures meaning, context, lived experience (e.g., interviews, oral histories, field notes). Example: an inquiry into climate-displaced communities in Bangladesh could quantify outmigration with census/IDMC data, then add interviews with displaced families to capture decision-making, gender impacts, and adaptation responses — neither dimension is sufficient alone.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Culminating inquiry plan — Critical minerals & Indigenous consent: Outline a complete inquiry plan including question, two relevant concepts of geographic thinking, three sources, one original map deliverable, and an argument with counter-argument.
Answer Key

Question: "How can Canada balance critical-minerals development with Indigenous consent and environmental protection between 2025 and 2035?" Concepts: spatial significance (deposit locations vs treaty territories); interrelationships (mining-ecosystem-community-economy links); geographic perspective (Indigenous, industry, environmental, federal). Sources: Natural Resources Canada Critical Minerals Centre; UNDRIP Act 2021; First Nations Major Projects Coalition reports; peer-reviewed Nature Energy/Resources Policy articles; case studies of the Ring of Fire and BHP Jansen. Map: choropleth of provincial mineral potential overlaid with First Nations territories (Mapbox or QGIS). Argument: Canada should adopt a federal Indigenous Consent Protocol with binding mediation, transparent royalty sharing, and environmental conditions. Counter-argument: economic urgency and global competition justify faster permitting; rebuttal — projects without consent face injunctions and capital flight, slowing development further.

13
[4]
Geospatial reasoning — Arctic sea ice: NSIDC reports September Arctic sea ice extent declined from ~7.6 million km² (1979–1990 average) to ~4.4 million km² (2020–2024 average). Compute percent loss. Identify two implications and one data caveat.
Answer Key

Percent loss = (7.6 − 4.4)/7.6 = 0.421 ≈ 42%. Implications: opens trans-Arctic shipping (Northern Sea Route, Northwest Passage) with geopolitical implications for sovereignty (Canada's Arctic claims, Russia's NSR strategy); ice-albedo positive feedback accelerates regional and global warming; ecological impacts on polar bears, seals, marine ecosystems and Inuit hunters. Data caveats: monthly variability is high; September is the seasonal minimum (extent peaks in March); satellite measurement methodology has been consistent only since 1979; thickness (volume) declines are even steeper than area; comparisons should use anomalies vs baselines (1981–2010 standard).

14
[3]
Ethics — Indigenous research: A student wants to interview a First Nations community about climate impacts. Identify three ethical principles or processes that should guide the work (OCAP, consent, reciprocity).
Answer Key

(1) Community consent: secure approval from band council or designated cultural authority; protocols vary by Nation. (2) OCAP principles (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession): the community controls how data are collected, stored, and used. (3) Reciprocity: results returned to the community in accessible form (e.g., plain-language summaries, public presentations); benefits beyond the researcher. (4) Compensation for elders' time. (5) Researcher reflexivity: positionality reflection on student's vantage point. (6) Ethics-board (REB) review for school or university research. Strong answers reference TCPS 2 Chapter 9 (Tri-Council Policy on Research Involving the First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Peoples of Canada).
