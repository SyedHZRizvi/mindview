Topic 1.1 — Measuring Quality of Life
Question 1
Which of the following is NOT a component of the Human Development Index (HDI)?
Solution: The HDI uses three dimensions: long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (schooling), and decent standard of living (GNI per capita). CO₂ emissions are tracked separately and appear in the Happy Planet Index, not HDI.
Question 2
Why is PPP (purchasing power parity) adjustment important when comparing GDP per capita between countries?
Solution: PPP allows comparison of living standards across countries by accounting for differences in the local cost of goods. Market exchange rates can mislead because non-traded goods (haircuts, housing) cost very different amounts in different economies.
Question 3
Calculate: A country has life-expectancy index 0.80, education index 0.70, and income index 0.65. What is its HDI (geometric mean, to two decimals)?
Solution: HDI = ∛(0.80 × 0.70 × 0.65) = ∛0.364 ≈ 0.714, rounded to 0.71. Geometric means penalise weakness in any one dimension more than arithmetic means do.
Topic 1.2 — Inequality & the Gini Coefficient
Question 4
A Gini coefficient of 0 represents:
Solution: The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 (perfect equality — Lorenz curve coincides with the line of equality) to 1 (perfect inequality — one person holds all income). Real countries cluster between 0.25 (Nordic) and 0.63 (South Africa).
Question 5
Wealth inequality is typically larger than income inequality because:
Solution: Wealth is a stock; even small differences in saving rates and investment returns compound over decades. Canadian wealth Gini ~0.72 vs income Gini ~0.31. Inheritance also concentrates wealth across generations.
Question 6
Calculate: In a village of 4 households earning $5, $15, $30, $50 (total $100), what is the cumulative income share of the bottom 50% of households?
Solution: Bottom 50% of households = poorest 2 (earning $5 and $15). Their combined income = $20. Cumulative share = 20/100 = 20%. The line of equality would put them at 50%; the gap reflects inequality.
Topic 1.3 — Demographic Transition
Question 7
A country has CBR = 35, CDR = 8, TFR = 4.5, and a wide-based population pyramid. It is most likely in DTM:
Solution: High CBR, falling CDR, high TFR, and a youth-heavy pyramid signal Stage 2 / early Stage 3. Natural increase = (35 − 8)/1000 = 2.7%/year, doubling in ~26 years.
Question 8
Replacement-level fertility (assuming no migration) is approximately:
Solution: About 2.1 — two to replace parents plus a small margin to compensate for mortality before reproductive age. Canada's TFR is ~1.4; the population grows mainly through immigration.
Topic 1.4 — Gender, Education, & Urbanization
Question 9
The Gender Inequality Index (GII) reflects three dimensions: reproductive health, empowerment, and ___.
Solution: The GII captures three dimensions: reproductive health (maternal mortality, adolescent birth rate), empowerment (parliamentary seats, secondary education by sex), and labour-market participation. The three dimensions are combined into a single index for cross-country comparison.
Question 10
A mega-city is conventionally defined as having a population of at least:
Solution: 10 million is the conventional UN threshold. Tokyo's metropolitan area has about 37 million, the largest. Other mega-cities include Delhi, Shanghai, São Paulo, Lagos.
Topic 1.5 — SDGs
Question 11
The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) consist of how many goals adopted in 2015?
Solution: The 2030 Agenda contains 17 SDGs with 169 targets. They succeeded the 8 Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015) and apply universally to all 193 UN member states.
Question 12
Calculate: If a city of 4.0 million in 2010 grew to 6.5 million in 2020, what was the average annual percentage growth rate (use exponential growth, round to one decimal)?
Solution: Using N_t = N₀e^(rt): r = (1/10) × ln(6.5/4.0) = (1/10) × ln(1.625) = (1/10) × 0.486 = 0.0486 ≈ 4.9% per year. Doubling time ≈ 70/4.9 ≈ 14 years.
