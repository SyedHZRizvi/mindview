Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define the four dimensions of globalization (economic, political, cultural, environmental) and give one example for each.
Answer Key

Economic: trade, investment, financial flows (e.g., CUSMA). Political: intergovernmental institutions, treaties (e.g., UN, WTO, Paris Agreement). Cultural: spread of media, food, language, fashion (e.g., K-pop in Toronto). Environmental: shared climate and ecosystems (e.g., transboundary air pollution, biodiversity loss).

2
[2]
The "most-favoured-nation" principle under WTO rules requires that:
Answer Key

A. MFN is the non-discrimination principle. Exceptions: regional FTAs and Generalised System of Preferences (GSP) for developing-country exports.

3
[3]
Distinguish the IMF from the World Bank in three ways: mandate, lending type, decision making.
Answer Key

Mandate: IMF — monetary stability, BoP crisis lending; World Bank — long-term development. Lending: IMF — short-term, conditional; WB — long-term project + policy loans. Decision making: both quota/share-weighted (US near-veto on major decisions); WB Board has 25 directors; IMF Executive Board 24.

4
[3]
Define and distinguish FDI, outsourcing, and offshoring. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

FDI: lasting cross-border investment (≥10% ownership), e.g., Honda building a plant in Alliston, ON. Outsourcing: contracting an outside firm (domestic or abroad), e.g., Bell outsourcing call centres. Offshoring: relocating own operations abroad, e.g., GM moving CAMI to Mexico. Outsourcing + offshoring overlap when work is contracted to overseas firms.

5
[2]
Critical-minerals supply chains for the energy transition are most concentrated in:
Answer Key

A. China controls ~60% of lithium refining, ~80% of cobalt refining, and over 90% of rare-earth processing. Mining is more distributed (Chile, Australia, DRC). The choke point is refining.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Country X can produce 5 tonnes of wheat or 1 tonne of beef per labour-day. Country Y can produce 3 wheat or 2 beef. (a) Which has comparative advantage in each? (b) State a mutually beneficial exchange rate (wheat per beef) at which both gain. (c) If both specialise fully and each has 100 labour-days, what is total combined output before and after specialisation?
Answer Key

(a) Country X opportunity cost: 1 beef = 5 wheat; Y: 1 beef = 1.5 wheat. Y has comparative advantage in beef (gives up less wheat). X's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 0.2 beef; Y's = 0.67 beef. X has comparative advantage in wheat. (b) Any rate strictly between Y's 1.5 wheat/beef and X's 5 wheat/beef makes both better off — e.g., 3 wheat per beef. (c) Without specialisation, if each splits time 50/50: X produces 250 wheat + 50 beef; Y produces 150 wheat + 100 beef; total 400 wheat + 150 beef. With full specialisation: X 500 wheat + 0 beef, Y 0 wheat + 200 beef; total 500 wheat + 200 beef. Specialisation yields more of both.

7
[4]
A 25% tariff is applied to steel imports from non-FTA countries. CIF (pre-tariff) prices: FTA partner = $1100/t; non-FTA = $900/t. Compare landed costs, and explain trade creation vs trade diversion.
Answer Key

FTA landed: $1100 × 1.00 = $1100/t. Non-FTA landed: $900 × 1.25 = $1125/t. FTA is cheaper. Without the FTA, imports would have come from the lower-cost non-FTA producer ($900 — efficient). The FTA diverts trade away from the lower-cost producer toward the higher-cost partner — trade diversion, welfare-reducing. Trade creation happens when an FTA enables imports that previously could not compete with domestic producers at all — welfare-increasing. Net effect of any FTA depends on relative magnitudes of these two effects.

8
[4]
Indonesia receives ~$15 billion in remittances and has GDP of ~$1320 billion. Tonga receives ~$220 million and has GDP of ~$0.5 billion. Compute remittance share of GDP for each and discuss the different macroeconomic vulnerabilities and benefits.
Answer Key

Indonesia: 15/1320 ≈ 1.1% of GDP — modest contribution; diversified economy. Tonga: 0.22/0.5 = 44% of GDP — extreme dependence. Tonga is vulnerable to host-country labour-market shocks (NZ, Australia, US lockdowns hit remittances), exchange-rate appreciation effects, and demographic "brain drain." Benefits in Tonga: household consumption, school enrolment, disaster resilience after cyclones (e.g., Tropical Cyclone Gita, 2018). Strong answers note Pacific Islands as a remittance-economy archetype.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
In 250–350 words, evaluate the claim that "globalization has been good for the world." Address at least two strengths and two limitations, and reach a defensible conclusion.
Answer Key

Strengths: lifted ~1 billion out of extreme poverty (China, India, Vietnam); reduced consumer prices in high-income countries; increased technology and ideas exchange; expanded international institutions for cooperation. Limitations: deindustrialisation in some regions (US Rust Belt, southern Ontario); concentration of profits in TNCs and elites; environmental costs (emissions, deforestation, shipping pollution); financial volatility (1997 Asian crisis, 2008 GFC); cultural homogenisation concerns. Strongest answers reach a defensible verdict (e.g., "net positive globally but unevenly distributed, requiring active redistribution and adjustment policy") rather than a flat for/against position. Reference Branko Milanovic's elephant graph or Dani Rodrik's "globalization trilemma."

10
[4]
Construct a 4-row table comparing CUSMA, CETA, CPTPP, and the WTO. Columns: Year in force, Geographic scope, Distinct feature.
Answer Key

CUSMA: 2020 / Canada, US, Mexico / sunset clause, increased auto regional content (75%), labour-value rule. CETA: 2017 (provisional) / Canada + EU / mostly duty-free goods, regulatory cooperation, ISDS replaced by Investment Court System. CPTPP: 2018 / 11 Pacific Rim + UK 2023 / progressive labour and environment chapters, suspended provisions from original TPP after US withdrawal. WTO: 1995 / 164 members / MFN, national treatment, dispute settlement (appellate body currently dysfunctional).

11
[4]
Sketch or describe the "smile curve" of value capture in a global value chain. Where do most jobs sit? Where does most profit sit?
Answer Key

U-shape: horizontal axis = stages of production (R&D → design → component manufacture → assembly → distribution → marketing → after-sales). Vertical axis = value added per unit. High at both ends (design / branding-services), low in the middle (assembly). Most jobs concentrate in middle stages; most profit at the ends. Implications: countries moving up the curve (China toward design and services; Korea, Taiwan in semiconductors) capture more value over time. Pure assembly economies are vulnerable to wage competition.

Part D: Application [13 marks]
12
[5]
Case study — EV battery supply chain & Canadian critical minerals: Canada is positioning itself as an integrated EV battery supplier. Evaluate the geographic advantages of Canada and identify two policy actions to capture more downstream value.
Answer Key

Advantages: nickel (Sudbury, Voisey's Bay), cobalt (Cobalt Camp ON), lithium (James Bay area, QC), graphite, copper, REEs (Saskatchewan, NWT). Clean electricity grid (~85% non-emitting). Trade access via CUSMA (qualifies for US IRA EV tax credits) and CETA. Skilled mining workforce. Policy actions: (1) federal Critical Minerals Strategy ($3.8B); (2) joint ventures with Korean/Japanese battery makers (LG, Stellantis Windsor, Volkswagen St Thomas, Northvolt Montérégie); (3) Indigenous consultation and benefit agreements (UNDRIP Act 2021); (4) cathode and battery cell manufacturing onshoring; (5) recycling infrastructure (Li-Cycle, Toronto-based). Trade-offs: speed of approvals vs ecological protection; subsidies vs market discipline.

13
[4]
Case study — Bangladesh garment industry: Apparel exports account for ~80% of Bangladesh's export earnings. After the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse (1134 dead), industry safety reforms emerged. Identify two reforms and one ongoing concern.
Answer Key

Reforms: (1) Bangladesh Accord on Fire and Building Safety (2013–) — legally binding agreement between brands and unions, with independent inspections; (2) Alliance for Bangladesh Worker Safety (2013–2018, US-led); (3) Bangladesh National Tripartite Plan of Action and increases in factory inspection capacity. Ongoing concerns: low minimum wages relative to living wage (~$113/month in 2024 vs estimated living wage $260); subcontracting to uninspected factories; worker representation and freedom-of-association gaps; climate vulnerability of factory zones (Dhaka flooding); environmental impact (textile dyeing pollutes rivers). Strong answers note brand due-diligence laws in EU and Canada as next-stage levers.

14
[4]
Issue-rich focus — Semiconductor geopolitics: Taiwan's TSMC produces ~60% of the world's chips and over 90% of the most advanced. Identify two strategic concerns and two policy responses (CHIPS Act, EU Chips Act, Canadian initiatives).
Answer Key

Concerns: (1) cross-strait political risk (China–Taiwan); (2) single-point-of-failure for critical electronics, automotive, defence; (3) Korean (Samsung) and Taiwanese leadership in advanced nodes (3 nm, 2 nm); (4) export controls (US ban on advanced equipment to China). Policy responses: US CHIPS and Science Act 2022 ($52B); EU Chips Act 2023 (€43B); Korean K-Chips Act; Japan METI subsidies for TSMC Kumamoto; Canadian Photonics Fabrication Centre and federal microelectronics initiatives. Trade-offs: subsidy races, public-money risks, and the geopolitical signal of "friend-shoring." Strong answers reference Chris Miller's Chip War (2022) framing.
