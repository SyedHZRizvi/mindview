Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Explain the greenhouse effect in 3–4 sentences. Why is it both natural and good (at the pre-industrial level) and dangerous (at the current human-amplified level)?
Answer Key

Sunlight passes through the atmosphere; Earth absorbs it and re-radiates infrared. Greenhouse gases (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, N₂O) absorb that infrared and re-emit it, warming the surface. Without it, Earth would average −18 °C; with the natural greenhouse, about +15 °C. The current human amplification (CO₂ from 280 → 422 ppm) adds energy faster than ecosystems can adjust, driving rising temperatures, sea levels, and extreme weather.

2
[2]
The natural carbon reservoir with the largest active stock is:
Answer Key

C. Ocean ~38 000 Gt C is the largest active reservoir. The lithosphere holds more but exchanges only on geologic timescales. Atmosphere ~875 Gt C; terrestrial biosphere ~2050 Gt C.

3
[3]
Define ecological footprint, biocapacity, and Earth Overshoot Day. State the world's approximate per-capita footprint and biocapacity.
Answer Key

Ecological footprint = area of biologically productive land and water required to support a lifestyle (gha/person). Biocapacity = productive area available. Earth Overshoot Day = date by which humanity has used all the resources the planet can regenerate that year (2024: 1 August). World averages: footprint ~2.6 gha, biocapacity ~1.6 gha, overshoot factor ~1.7.

4
[3]
Name three differences between the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015).
Answer Key

(1) Coverage: Kyoto only Annex I (industrialised) countries had binding targets; Paris has universal NDCs. (2) Targets: Kyoto top-down 5% below 1990; Paris bottom-up self-set NDCs with regular updates. (3) US participation: Kyoto signed but not ratified; Paris ratified (briefly withdrew 2017–2021). (4) Mechanism: Kyoto carbon-market mechanisms (CDM, JI); Paris emphasises transparency and stocktakes. (5) Goal: Kyoto modest absolute reduction; Paris well-below-2 °C, toward 1.5 °C.

5
[2]
Approximately what share of global fresh-water withdrawals goes to agriculture?
Answer Key

C. About 70% to agriculture, 20% industry, 10% domestic. The food–water nexus is among the highest-leverage climate-adaptation domains.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Country A emits 250 Mt CO₂/year and 8 Mt CH₄/year. Country B emits 200 Mt CO₂/year and 12 Mt CH₄/year. Using GWP-100 = 28 for methane, compute total CO₂e emissions for each, identify which country has higher total CO₂e, and explain how policy emphasis might differ.
Answer Key

Country A: 250 + (8 × 28) = 250 + 224 = 474 Mt CO₂e. Country B: 200 + (12 × 28) = 200 + 336 = 536 Mt CO₂e. Country B has higher CO₂e despite lower CO₂. A's policy should emphasise CO₂ (electricity, transport) reductions; B's policy must address methane (livestock, oil & gas, landfills). Because methane is short-lived (~12 years) and high-GWP, methane reductions yield large near-term climate benefits.

7
[4]
A country emits 800 Mt CO₂/year and pledges to reduce emissions by 40% from 2020 levels by 2035. If 2020 emissions were 750 Mt, what is the 2035 target and what annual reduction rate (assuming linear) is required?
Answer Key

2035 target = 750 × (1 − 0.40) = 450 Mt. Required reduction from current 800 Mt to 450 Mt = 350 Mt over 11 years. Linear annual reduction = 350/11 ≈ 31.8 Mt/year, or ~4% per year of the current level. Faster than typical OECD trajectories — requires aggressive electrification, building retrofits, and methane action.

8
[4]
A region's renewable water is 80 km³/year. Population in 2025 is 20 million, projected to reach 35 million by 2055 with no change in supply. Compute per-capita water in 2025 and 2055, classify each on the Falkenmark scale, and identify two demand-side responses.
Answer Key

2025: 80 × 10⁹/20 × 10⁶ = 4000 m³/person — abundant. 2055: 80 × 10⁹/35 × 10⁶ ≈ 2286 m³/person — adequate but trending toward stress. Demand-side: drip irrigation, leak reduction (24% loss is typical), water reuse / greywater, agricultural water pricing, reduced meat consumption (saves virtual water), urban density to lower per-capita landscape demand.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
In 250–350 words, evaluate the effectiveness of carbon pricing as a climate-policy tool. Address advantages, limitations, and a complementary policy.
Answer Key

Advantages: prices the external cost of emissions, harnesses market response, generates revenue (often rebated to households — Canadian Climate Action Incentive returns more than most households pay), is economy-wide. Limitations: politically difficult; can be regressive without rebates; price elasticity is low in short run; sectors with limited substitutes (cement, aviation, agriculture) need complementary policy; carbon leakage if trading partners lack pricing. Complementary: zero-emissions vehicle mandates, clean-electricity standards, building codes, methane regulations, green industrial policy (Inflation Reduction Act, Canadian Critical Minerals Strategy), border carbon adjustments (EU CBAM). Best evidence: carbon-pricing systems combined with regulation and public investment produce faster, more equitable decarbonisation than any single tool.

10
[4]
Construct a 3-row table comparing the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement, and the 2023 Global Stocktake. Columns: Year, Key feature, Status today.
Answer Key

Kyoto: 1997 / binding top-down targets for Annex I / expired (commitment periods 2008–2012, 2013–2020 Doha Amendment, limited uptake). Paris: 2015 / universal voluntary NDCs, 1.5/2 °C goal / in force, 200+ parties. Global Stocktake: 2023 / first comprehensive assessment of NDC ambition / called for tripling renewables, doubling efficiency, transition away from fossil fuels.

11
[4]
Sketch (or describe with labelled parts) the carbon cycle, including atmosphere, ocean, biosphere, lithosphere, and the major fluxes (photosynthesis, respiration, ocean exchange, fossil-fuel combustion, deforestation).
Answer Key

Atmosphere ~875 Gt C. Ocean (surface + deep) ~38 000 Gt C. Terrestrial biosphere ~550 Gt plants + ~1500 Gt soils. Lithosphere fossil pool ~5 000 000 Gt C. Fluxes (Gt C/yr): gross photosynthesis ~120; respiration + decomposition ~120; ocean ⇄ atmosphere ~90 each way; volcanic ~0.1; fossil-fuel combustion ~9.6; deforestation ~1.5. Approximately half of human emissions accumulate in the atmosphere; the rest is absorbed roughly equally by ocean and land sinks.

Part D: Application [13 marks]
12
[5]
Case study — Athabasca oil sands: Canada's oil-sands sector emits ~80 Mt CO₂e/year (about 11% of national emissions). Critically evaluate two pathways: (a) absolute emissions cap; (b) carbon-capture-and-storage (CCS) with continued production. Identify trade-offs and political feasibility.
Answer Key

(a) Emissions cap: forces phased reductions, aligned with 1.5 °C trajectories; politically difficult in Alberta/Saskatchewan; risks investment flight and stranded assets but signals long-term decarbonisation. (b) CCS: maintains production while reducing emissions; requires massive capital ($16+ billion Pathways Alliance proposal); not yet operating at oil-sands scale; energy penalty; cannot capture downstream combustion emissions abroad. Trade-offs: speed (cap is faster on emissions, slower on jobs transition; CCS preserves jobs but technology unproven at scale). Political feasibility: cap faces provincial opposition; CCS attracts industry support but needs public subsidy. Best policy mix: declining cap with CCS incentives, transition funding for workers, and complementary methane regulations.

13
[4]
Case study — Sea-level rise & Bangladesh: About 50 million Bangladeshis live within 5 m of sea level. If global sea level rises ~1 m by 2100 (mid-range IPCC scenario), describe the geographic risks, two adaptation strategies already in use, and one international policy response.
Answer Key

Risks: saltwater intrusion (drinking water, agriculture), cyclone storm surge, mass displacement, loss of mangrove buffers. Adaptation in use: raised embankments (polders), salt-tolerant rice varieties, cyclone shelters (Bangladesh has built ~14 000 since 1991), early-warning systems. International responses: Bangladesh-led Climate Vulnerable Forum, UNFCCC Loss and Damage Fund (operationalised at COP28, 2023), green-climate-fund grants for adaptation. The case illustrates climate-injustice: the people most at risk contributed least to emissions.

14
[4]
Issue-rich focus — Indigenous Land Back & conservation: Indigenous peoples manage or claim a disproportionate share of the world's remaining intact ecosystems. Outline two reasons Indigenous-led conservation (e.g., Canada's Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas, IPCAs) is increasingly recognised as effective.
Answer Key

(1) Empirical evidence: lands managed by Indigenous peoples have biodiversity and carbon outcomes comparable to or better than national parks, at lower cost (Garnett et al., Nature Sustainability 2018). (2) Cultural-ecological knowledge: traditional practices (fire management, rotational harvest, sacred-site protection) sustain ecosystems over generations. (3) Political legitimacy: Section 35 rights and UNDRIP support Indigenous-led governance. (4) Reconciliation alignment: TRC Calls to Action 92 and the 2021 UNDRIP Act make Indigenous-led conservation a policy priority. Canadian examples: Edéhzhíe Protected Area, the Pimachiowin Aki UNESCO site, the Thaidene Nëné National Park Reserve.
