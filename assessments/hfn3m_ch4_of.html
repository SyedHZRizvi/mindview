Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [14 marks]
1
[3]
Name the four NOVA processing groups and give one example of each.
Answer Key

Group 1 — unprocessed/minimally processed (apple, brown rice, chicken). Group 2 — culinary ingredients (oil, butter, salt, sugar). Group 3 — processed (canned beans, cheese, bread, smoked fish). Group 4 — ultra-processed (soft drinks, packaged snack cakes, instant noodles).

2
[2]
Which Canadian foodborne-illness outbreak in 2008 led to major reforms of federal food-safety law?
Answer Key

C — Maple Leaf 2008: 23 deaths from Listeria-contaminated cold cuts; drove the modern integrated regulatory regime, including the Safe Food for Canadians Act (2012) and Regulations (2019).

3
[3]
Define the Maillard reaction. State its temperature threshold and one food it creates.
Answer Key

Maillard reaction = non-enzymatic browning above ~140 °C between amino acids and reducing sugars, producing hundreds of flavour compounds. Creates the browned surface of toast, roast beef crust, fried onions, dark chocolate, and the seared crust of a steak. Distinct from caramelisation (~160 °C) which is sugar browning alone.

4
[3]
Name the four Cs of food safety and give one example of correct behaviour for each.
Answer Key

Clean — wash hands 20 seconds with soap before handling food; sanitise cutting boards with diluted bleach. Separate — use a different cutting board for raw meat; store raw meat on bottom shelf of fridge. Cook — use a thermometer to confirm internal temperature (74 °C for ground poultry/leftovers, 71 °C for ground beef). Chill — refrigerate within 2 hours (1 hour if > 32 °C); fridge ≤ 4 °C.

5
[3]
List three foodborne pathogens and the food most associated with each.
Answer Key

(Any three) Salmonella — eggs and raw poultry. E. coli O157:H7 — undercooked ground beef and contaminated leafy greens. Listeria — deli meats and soft cheeses. Campylobacter — raw poultry. Norovirus — contaminated water, ready-to-eat foods. Clostridium botulinum — improperly home-canned low-acid foods and honey for infants < 12 months.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[4]
A 200 g portion of broccoli originally contains 180 mg vitamin C. After cooking, vitamin C drops to 81 mg. Calculate the percent loss and explain the most likely cause.
Answer Key

Loss = (180 − 81) / 180 × 100 = 55%. The most likely cause is boiling, which leaches the water-soluble vitamin C into the discarded cooking water and oxidises some by the heat. Steaming would retain ~85% (~ 153 mg); microwaving comparable.

7
[4]
Design an experiment to compare vitamin C retention between boiling, steaming, and microwaving 200 g of broccoli. Identify variables, controls, and how you would measure vitamin C.
Answer Key

IV: cooking method (boiling 10 min, steaming 5 min, microwave 3 min). DV: vitamin C concentration (mg/100 g), measured by titration with iodine solution (DCPIP) or HPLC. Controls: same broccoli source and batch, same starting mass, same final core temperature, same cooking-vessel size, same kitchen, same observer. Replicate at least 3× per condition. Hypothesis: steaming and microwaving retain ≥80% vs ~45% for boiling. Statistical test: one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc comparisons.

8
[4]
Four hours after a backyard BBQ, two of twelve guests develop vomiting and diarrhoea; by 24 hours six are sick. The menu was hamburgers (well-done), potato salad with mayonnaise (sat outside 3 h at 30 °C), raw spinach salad, chocolate cake, lemonade. Identify the most likely pathogen, the food vehicle, and the prevention failure.
Answer Key

Most likely pathogen: Staphylococcus aureus (preformed heat-stable toxin → rapid 1–6 h onset; classic symptom: vomiting). Food vehicle: potato salad with mayonnaise — protein-rich, left in 4–60 °C danger zone for 3 hours. Prevention failure: the two-hour rule was violated (one hour at temperatures > 32 °C). Hamburgers were cooked through; the cake is low water-activity; lemonade is acidic — none of these match the time-course or symptom profile.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[4]
Explain dry-heat vs moist-heat cooking. Reference Maillard reaction, nutrient retention, and texture.
Answer Key

Dry-heat methods (baking, roasting, sautéing, grilling, frying) use air or fat. Above ~140 °C the Maillard reaction develops the characteristic browned crust and savoury flavours. Texture: crisp exterior, drier interior. Water-soluble vitamins are partially preserved because the food is not in water. Moist-heat methods (boiling, simmering, steaming, poaching, braising) use water or vapour. They do not brown food because temperature is capped near 100 °C. Texture is moist and soft. Water-soluble vitamins are preserved if cooking liquid is consumed (soup, stew) but leached out if liquid is discarded.

10
[4]
Construct a one-page kitchen poster (text only) titled "The Four Cs of Food Safety" that a 13-year-old could understand. Include action steps for Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill.
Answer Key

CLEAN — Wash hands 20 seconds with soap before handling food. Wipe counters with hot soapy water and sanitise with diluted bleach. Rinse fruit and vegetables. SEPARATE — Use a red cutting board for raw meat and a green one for vegetables. Store raw chicken on the bottom shelf, fruit on the top. Never reuse a marinade that touched raw meat. COOK — Use a thermometer. 71 °C for ground beef. 74 °C for chicken and turkey and leftovers. 63 °C + 3 min rest for whole cuts of beef and pork. CHILL — Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours (1 hour if > 32 °C). Fridge ≤ 4 °C. Freezer ≤ −18 °C. Thaw frozen meat in the fridge, in cold water (change every 30 min), or microwave — never on the counter.

11
[4]
Compare and contrast canning, freezing, drying, and fermentation in a 4-row table covering: mechanism, effect on nutrients, suitable foods, and a notable risk.
Answer Key

Canning — heat sterilises and hermetic seal excludes air. Nutrient loss similar to long cooking (vitamin C reduced). Suitable for many foods. Risk: low-acid foods home-canned without pressure cooker → botulism. Freezing — halts microbial growth at ≤ −18 °C. Excellent nutrient retention especially when blast-frozen at harvest. Suitable for most vegetables, meat, fish. Risk: bacteria not killed; thawed food can spoil quickly. Drying — reduces water activity to a<sub>w</sub> < 0.6. Vitamin C decreased; minerals preserved. Suitable for fruit, herbs, meat (jerky). Risk: insect/rodent damage if not stored sealed. Fermentation — beneficial microbes outcompete spoilage. Often improves digestibility and may add probiotics. Suitable for yogurt, cheese, sauerkraut, kimchi, beer, wine. Risk: contamination if pH not low enough; risk of botulism if anaerobic fermentation incomplete (rare).

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[4]
Case Study — Holiday turkey audit. A family thaws a 6 kg turkey on the counter for 24 hours, rinses it in the kitchen sink, and judges doneness by colour (not thermometer). Identify three food-safety failures and corrective actions.
Answer Key

(1) Thawing on counter — surface enters danger zone (4–60 °C) long before centre thaws. Correct: thaw in fridge over 3 days, or in cold water changed every 30 min, or in microwave. (2) Rinsing in the sink — Health Canada explicitly warns against this; aerosolised Campylobacter splatters around sink and onto surfaces. Correct: do not rinse; pat dry with paper towel. (3) Judging doneness by colour — pink does not mean undercooked, brown does not mean done. Correct: use a meat thermometer in the thickest part of the thigh; pull at 74 °C, rest covered 15 min.

13
[4]
Case Study — Frozen vs fresh broccoli in February. Compare nutrient retention, carbon footprint, and cost of frozen broccoli vs broccoli "fresh" imported from Mexico for a Toronto household in February. Recommend one and justify.
Answer Key

Frozen: blast-frozen at harvest; vitamin C retention ≈ 80%; lower carbon footprint than air-freighted; cost typically $2/kg. Fresh imported: respiration losses during 5+ days of transport reduce vitamin C by 30–50%; higher carbon footprint from refrigerated transport; cost $4–5/kg. Recommend frozen: better nutrient retention, lower cost, lower carbon — and just as safe. The intuition that "fresh is always best" fails for produce out of season.

14
[4]
STSE — Food irradiation in Canada. Health Canada permits irradiation of ground beef. Explain (i) the rationale, (ii) how irradiation works, and (iii) one concern that critics raise and Health Canada's response.
Answer Key

(i) Rationale: ground beef carries a higher E. coli O157:H7 risk than whole cuts because grinding mixes surface bacteria throughout the meat. Irradiation reduces pathogen load to safe levels and can save lives, particularly in vulnerable people. (ii) Mechanism: ionising radiation (gamma rays from Co-60, X-rays, or electron beams) damages microbial DNA. The food does not become radioactive — the rays pass through and the food's chemistry is essentially intact aside from radiolysis products comparable to those from cooking. (iii) Concern: critics argue irradiation could be used as a substitute for hygiene in processing plants, masking poor sanitation. Health Canada response: irradiation is permitted in addition to good manufacturing practice, not as a substitute; irradiated foods must carry the Radura symbol and the words "Treated with radiation".
