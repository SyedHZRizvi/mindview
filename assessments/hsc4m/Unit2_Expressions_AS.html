Topic 2.1 — Language as Cultural Carrier
Question 1
The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis is best summarized as the claim that:
Solution: The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis (developed by Sapir & Whorf in the 1930s) proposes linguistic relativity: language structure influences thought. The strong "determinism" version (language DETERMINES thought) is rejected; the weak "influence" version (language shapes attention, categories, ease of expression) is supported by experimental work (e.g., Boroditsky on Kuuk Thaayorre and spatial orientation).
Question 2
When a language dies, anthropologists argue something more than vocabulary is lost. What specifically?
Solution: Languages encode unique categorisation systems, accumulated ecological knowledge (Sami snow types; Inuit ice categories), kinship distinctions, narrative traditions, and worldview. The TRC's framing of residential schools as cultural genocide rests partly on the deliberate suppression of Indigenous languages.
Topic 2.2 — Religion & Cosmology
Question 3
Émile Durkheim's The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912) argued that religion is fundamentally:
Solution: Durkheim's classic claim: religion's primary function is social — binding the group, marking the sacred from the profane, producing collective effervescence in ritual. He defined religion as "a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things ... which unite into one single moral community ... all those who adhere to them." Whatever its theological truth, the social function is undeniable.
Question 4
"In the Anishinaabe creation story, the world is renewed after a great flood when Wenabozho asks Muskrat to dive for soil from which a new earth grows on Turtle's back."
From a social-science perspective, how should we treat this story alongside, say, the Genesis 1 creation story?
Solution: Social scientists treat both stories as coherent worldviews with internal logic, ethical content, and cosmological claims about how the world is and how humans should relate to it. The Anishinaabe story emphasizes relational responsibility, the kinship of animals, and renewal; Genesis emphasizes divine sovereignty, human dominion, linear history. Each shapes the ethics and law of its culture. Treating Indigenous stories as "myth" while Christian stories as "fact" is the colonial framing the discipline has worked to displace.
Topic 2.3 — Ritual & Rites of Passage
Question 5
Arnold van Gennep's (1909) three-stage structure of rites of passage is:
Solution: Van Gennep's three stages — separation (removed from ordinary life), liminality (betwixt-and-between threshold), incorporation (re-entered with new status) — apply to ceremonies as varied as bar/bat mitzvahs, military boot camp, university orientation, and Indigenous coming-of-age. Victor Turner later developed the concept of liminality as transformative threshold and communitas as the bond among co-initiates.
Question 6
The Anishinaabe Jingle Dance is significant because:
Solution: The Jingle Dance originated in Anishinaabe (Ojibwe) tradition in the early 20th century from a vision-dream connected with the 1918 influenza pandemic; it carries a healing function. Powwows including jingle-dance were banned under the Indian Act from 1885 until amendments in 1951. The dance has been re-affirmed in contemporary cultural revitalization at Idle No More gatherings (2012–13), pandemic-healing ceremonies (2020–22), and ordinary powwows across Treaty 3, Treaty 9, and beyond.
Topic 2.4 — Art & Aesthetics
Question 7
The hierarchy between "fine art" and "craft" is mostly a product of which historical period and context?
Solution: The fine-art / craft hierarchy is a 19th-century European invention. It systematically elevated oil painting and sculpture (associated with men, with Europe, with university training) and demoted weaving, ceramics, beadwork, basketry (associated with women, with Indigenous and colonised peoples, with the home). The post-1970s push to recognise quilts, beadwork, Inuit printmaking, and Indigenous arts as serious traditions is part of dismantling this hierarchy.
Question 8
Kenojuak Ashevak's The Enchanted Owl (1960) was produced through which Indigenous arts initiative?
Solution: The Cape Dorset (Kinngait) printmaking co-operative was started in 1959 by James Houston and Inuit artists; it became one of Canada's most successful Indigenous arts initiatives. Kenojuak Ashevak's The Enchanted Owl appeared on a 1970 Canadian postage stamp and on the reverse of the 2004 Canadian $10 note — a striking case of Indigenous visual art entering the symbolic apparatus of the state.
Topic 2.5 — Music, Dance & Performance
Question 9
The West African griot tradition is significant for cultural studies because:
Solution: Griots (Manding, Mandé, Wolof, and other West African traditions) are specialised praise-singers and historians who, through years of apprenticeship, memorise the cultural archive in song. The kora (21-string harp-lute) is the griot's instrument. The tradition demonstrates that pre-literate societies were not "without records" — they used song as memory technology, an archive carried in human bodies and instruments.
Question 10
Hip-hop's global spread illustrates which dynamic of cultural change?
Solution: Hip-hop originated in the South Bronx in the 1970s as a Black-and-Latino American form (Tricia Rose, Black Noise, 1994). Within fifty years it has become globally dominant, with major scenes in Seoul, Lagos, Mumbai, Toronto (Drake, the Weeknd), and Berlin. The same beat does different cultural work in different contexts — politically radical in some hands, conservative or apolitical in others. This is hybridization plus localisation: form travels, meaning is locally re-made.
