Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[3]B · Foundations
Name the FIVE key characteristics of culture (Tylor & modern derivations).
Answer Key

Learned, shared, symbolic, integrated, dynamic. 0.5 marks each, plus 0.5 for accurate framing.

2
[2]B
Distinguish material culture from non-material culture. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

Material = physical objects (smartphones, buildings, food, tools). Non-material = beliefs, values, norms, language, ideologies. They interlock — every artefact carries non-material meaning.

3
[2]B
Ethnocentrism is best defined as:
Answer Key

B. Sumner (1906). Operates below conscious notice in everyone; methodological cultural relativism is the disciplined corrective.

4
[2]B
The signature method of cultural anthropology is:
Answer Key

B. Long-term participant observation producing "thick description" (Geertz); Malinowski's Trobriand fieldwork (1915–18) is the founding example.

5
[3]C · Expressions
Define the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis. Identify both the strong and weak versions and indicate which is supported by contemporary evidence.
Answer Key

Sapir–Whorf hypothesis = language structure shapes thought. Strong version: language DETERMINES thought (rejected). Weak version (linguistic relativity): language INFLUENCES thought; supported by experiments (Boroditsky on Kuuk Thaayorre spatial orientation; Russian blue-shade studies). 1 mark each.

6
[3]C
Van Gennep's three stages of rites of passage. Define and give one example of each.
Answer Key

Separation (removed from ordinary life — pre-graduation rehearsal), Liminality (betwixt-and-between — moment of cap-and-gown crossing), Incorporation (re-entered with new status — diploma reception). 1 mark each.

7
[2]D · Identity
Cooley's "looking-glass self" (1902) describes self as formed by:
Answer Key

A. The self is fundamentally social, formed in interaction. Mead extended this with the "generalized other."

8
[2]D
Kimberlé Crenshaw coined the term intersectionality in:
Answer Key

A. Crenshaw's "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" in the University of Chicago Legal Forum; framework attends to multiple, simultaneous axes of identity and power.

9
[3]D
Define each: cisgender, transgender, two-spirit.
Answer Key

Cisgender — alignment between assigned and lived gender. Transgender — non-alignment; umbrella for many gender experiences. Two-spirit — pan-Indigenous umbrella term (coined 1990, Winnipeg) for traditional Indigenous gender/sexual roles outside colonial categories. 1 mark each.

10
[3]E · Change
Define and contrast cultural homogenization, hybridization, and heterogenization. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

Homogenization (Ritzer's McDonaldization): increasing global uniformity. Hybridization (Kraidy, Pieterse): global forms locally reshaped — McAloo Tikki burger, K-pop. Heterogenization (Appadurai): increased variety via diasporic flows — Tamil-Canadian platforms; Indigenous social-media revitalization. 1 mark each.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
11
[8]B + D + E · Comparative Case
Case A: Kohanga Reo (Māori language nests) — founded 1982 in Wainuiomata; immersion preschool programs producing thousands of new Te Reo speakers; supported by the 1987 Māori Language Act, Māori-medium schooling, and the 2004 launch of Māori Television.
Case B: Tsi Tyónnheht Onkwawén:na (Mohawk-language adult immersion) — Tyendinaga Mohawk Territory, Ontario; founded mid-1980s with major adult-immersion revival in the 2000s. Operates alongside similar programs in Kahnawà:ke (QC), Akwesasne (Treaty 13/border), and Six Nations of the Grand River (ON).
Compare and contrast these two language revitalization movements. Address: (i) the historical disruption being addressed, (ii) the strategies being used, (iii) the structural conditions that have shaped outcomes, (iv) one shared challenge and one shared opportunity. Aim for 10–12 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong answers will address: (i) Disruption: Māori — British colonisation, English-medium schooling from 1867 Native Schools Act, by 1980s fewer than 20% of Māori spoke Te Reo fluently. Mohawk — French and English colonisation, residential schools, Indian Act, language transmission severed. (ii) Strategies: Māori — Kohanga Reo, then Kura Kaupapa Māori, then Wharekura, then university programs, plus Māori TV, government-translated documents, parliament with Māori use. Mohawk — adult immersion (Tsi Tyónnheht), some preschool programs (especially Kahnawà:ke), media (CKON in Akwesasne), online resources, university courses at Six Nations Polytechnic. (iii) Structural conditions: Māori has single political nation, single government to negotiate with, official language status; Mohawk is split across multiple communities, multiple jurisdictions (Ontario, Quebec, New York, two federal governments), more fragmented funding. (iv) Shared challenge: elder-generation language carriers declining in number. Shared opportunity: intergenerational community will, digital tools for distance learning. Marks: 2 each for the four parts.

12
[6]D · Source Analysis
"At a Toronto secondary school, the social-justice club's executive (three white students, two Asian students, one Black student) plans a Black History Month event. They ask the Black student to bring two friends and speak about 'the Black experience.' The white students choose readings and the Asian students design posters."
Critique this scenario using Unit 3 concepts. Identify at least THREE problems (with concept names) and propose how the event could be organised differently. Aim for 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Problems: (1) Tokenism — Black student asked to be sole representative of "the Black experience" (which itself flattens diversity within Black-Canadian communities). (2) Burden of representation — unpaid emotional labour for the event placed on the Black student. (3) Replication of dominant-culture dynamics — white students decide content, Black student does the labour of representation. (4) Failure to consult Black-Canadian community. Better approach: Black students lead from start; consult Toronto Black-Canadian organisations (Federation of Black Canadians, BlackNorth, Ontario Black History Society); choose readings from a range of Black-Canadian scholars (Hill, Edugyan, Maynard, Walcott); pay/recognise guest speakers; address structural racism, not only celebration. Marks: 2 per problem with concept name; 2 for proposal.

13
[5]E · Globalization Analysis
"McDonald's entered India in 1996 — adapted the menu to local norms (Maharaja Mac, McAloo Tikki, separate veg/non-veg kitchens). By 2025, McDonald's India operates 500+ outlets across 50+ Indian cities."
Analyse this case using all three globalization theories (homogenization, hybridization, heterogenization). For each theory, identify what the case supports.
Answer Key

(1) Homogenization: McDonald's brand, drive-through format, fast-food rhythm spreading into India, potentially displacing dhabas and street-food vendors. (2) Hybridization: McAloo Tikki is genuinely Indian fast food in McDonald's drag — a hybrid that did not pre-exist. (3) Heterogenization: the global brand has been substantially Indianised; the corporation has learned vegetarianism at scale; both local and global have changed. Marks: 1.5–2 each.

14
[6]A · Inquiry Design
Topic: "Korean-Canadian high school students in Toronto." Working through the inquiry process, develop a focused research question and a methods plan for a Grade 12 ISP. Address: research question, primary method, sample, two secondary sources, one ethical consideration.
Answer Key

Sample: Research question: "How do second-generation Korean-Canadian Grade 11–12 students in Scarborough's TDSB schools experience and respond to parental academic expectations, and how does this experience compare to their first-generation peers?" Primary method: semi-structured interviews (45–60 min, recorded with consent, transcribed). Sample: 8 participants — 4 first-generation (born in Korea), 4 second-generation (born in Canada). Secondary sources: Park, Korean-Canadian Adolescent Studies (2018); Hofstede dimensions for cross-cultural framing. Ethical consideration: participants are minors — parental and assent consent; anonymise; topics may touch family tensions, debrief and offer school-counsellor info. Marks: 1 for each element + 1 for plausibility.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
15
[10]All strands · Extended Response
Essay (extended response, ~400–500 words): "Canadian multiculturalism, as official policy since 1971, has often been described as a 'mosaic.' What does this metaphor accomplish, and what does it leave out? Draw on at least TWO of the five HSC4M strands and at least two specific cases."
Answer Key

Strong essay structure: (1) Introduction — Canadian multiculturalism becomes official 1971 (PET in House of Commons), enshrined Multiculturalism Act 1988, Section 27 of Charter 1982. Thesis: the mosaic metaphor accomplishes important recognition of ethnic-cultural diversity BUT leaves out (a) Indigenous nation-to-nation relationships that precede the Canadian polity, (b) structural inequities the metaphor risks obscuring with celebration-and-festival framing. (2) What the metaphor accomplishes (use Strand D Identity/Diversity material) — distinguishes Canada from American "melting pot"; protects and celebrates ethnic communities; supports community institutions, multicultural-education funding, official-language translation. Specific case: GTA's Tamil-Canadian, Italian-Canadian, Filipino-Canadian, Caribbean-Canadian communities each have substantial institutions and visible presence. (3) What it leaves out (use Strand E Change material and Indigenous-rights framing) — Indigenous peoples are nations, not "ethnic groups"; Section 35 (Constitution Act 1982) and UNDRIP (2007) frame their relationship to Canada as nation-to-nation, not as inclusion in the mosaic. Specific case: TRC Calls to Action 62/63 require Indigenous content integration across curriculum, not as one more cultural group. Critics including Glen Coulthard, Sunera Thobani, Himani Bannerji argue multiculturalism risks reducing cultural difference to celebration while leaving structural inequalities (employment, housing, education outcomes) untouched. (4) Conclusion — multiculturalism is real and partial; serious engagement requires holding both the policy and the critique together; reconciliation with Indigenous peoples needs to be additive, not folded into multiculturalism. Marks: 2 for thesis; 2 each for two strand-based arguments with specific cases; 2 for nuanced conclusion; 2 for communication and citation.

16
[5]A · Writing & Citation
Write one strong analytical paragraph (5–7 sentences) on any HSC4M topic, demonstrating: a topic sentence, embedded direct quotation (real or invented) with APA-7 in-text citation including page number, interpretation of the evidence, and a closing analytical move. Then write the corresponding APA-7 reference list entry.
Answer Key

Sample: "Lera Boroditsky's research on the Australian Aboriginal language Kuuk Thaayorre demonstrates a measurable cognitive consequence of linguistic structure: '[Speakers] don't use words like 'left' and 'right' — they use cardinal directions. ... [T]hey have to know where they are at all times' (Boroditsky, 2011, p. 65). This finding supports the weak version of linguistic relativity — language amplifies attention to category that would otherwise require effortful construction. The case complicates any easy claim that language is merely communicative; it suggests language is structurally cognitive."

Reference: Boroditsky, L. (2011). How language shapes thought. Scientific American, 304(2), 62–65.

Marks: 1 topic sentence; 1 embedded quotation; 1 APA in-text citation; 1 interpretation; 1 reference list entry.

17
[3]D
Convert this weak claim into a strong thesis suitable for HSC4M: "Globalization is changing things a lot."
Answer Key

Sample: "Globalization simultaneously homogenises cultural forms (McDonald's, Hollywood), hybridises them through localisation (McAloo Tikki, K-pop), and heterogenises through diasporic and revitalization counter-flows (Māori Kohanga Reo, Tamil-Canadian transnational platforms) — such that analysing any specific case requires distinguishing which flow dominates and at whose cost." Marks: 1 specificity; 1 debatability; 1 theoretical grounding.

18
[3]A
Convert this informal sentence into proper Grade 12 social-science academic register: "A lot of Indigenous languages are dying, but some are coming back, which is good."
Answer Key

Sample: "While UNESCO classifies thousands of Indigenous languages globally as endangered, the success of revitalization initiatives such as the Māori Kohanga Reo (since 1982) and Mohawk Tsi Tyónnheht Onkwawén:na (since 1985) demonstrates that endangered status need not be terminal — sustained community effort, dedicated funding, and political recognition can produce significant intergenerational language renewal." Marks: 1 register; 1 evidence specificity; 1 nuanced claim.

19
[4]A
Identify three common forms of plagiarism in student research, and for each, identify how to avoid it.
Answer Key

(1) Direct copying without quotation/citation — use quotation marks and in-text citation including page. (2) Paraphrase without citation — substantively rewrite AND cite the source. (3) Using AI-generated text without acknowledgement — most schools and universities now require acknowledgement of AI assistance; using AI to write for you is academic misconduct. Other forms: false citation, self-plagiarism. 1 mark each + 1 for the no-AI-misuse point.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
20
[8]All strands · Case Application
"In May 2021, 215 unmarked graves were discovered at the former Kamloops Indian Residential School site. Similar discoveries followed at sites including Marieval (Saskatchewan), Brandon (Manitoba), and others. In September 2021, the federal government declared September 30 the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, a federal statutory holiday."
Apply HSC4M concepts to analyse this passage. Address: (i) why the TRC's 2015 finding of cultural genocide is supported by the residential school system, (ii) what specifically was suppressed (use Unit 1 and Unit 2 concepts about culture and language), (iii) what the 2021 events did and did not change in Canadian public discourse, (iv) what TRC Calls to Action remain inadequately implemented (specify at least one). Aim for 10–12 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong answers: (i) Cultural genocide because the schools targeted the enculturation process — children removed from family/community during the formative years when language, kinship, ceremony, worldview are transmitted; explicit aim was "kill the Indian in the child" (Duncan Campbell Scott, 1920); punishment for speaking Indigenous languages and practising traditions. (ii) Specifically suppressed: Indigenous languages, kinship systems, oral traditions, ceremonies (potlatch and sundance bans, 1885–1951), traditional gender-and-sexual roles, ecological knowledge, land-based knowledge. The cumulative effect is cultural transmission interruption across generations. (iii) The 2021 discoveries made the TRC's findings undeniable in mainstream public discourse, accelerated the statutory holiday, prompted institutional responses (museums, universities, churches, governments). They did NOT in themselves resolve reconciliation: structural reforms in child welfare, justice, education, housing remain incomplete. (iv) Inadequate implementation: Call 11 (adequate funding for First Nations education on-reserve); Call 13 (cultural support for survivors and intergenerational); Call 80 implemented but full cultural support insufficient; Call 42 (Indigenous justice systems); Calls 18–24 (health); Calls 7–10 (employment-equity). Marks: 2 per part (i–iv).

21
[8]B + C + D · Comparative Cultural Practice
Compare and contrast TWO cultural practices from different cultures that share a function but differ in form (e.g., two coming-of-age ceremonies; two religious rituals; two language-revitalization efforts; two diaspora communities). For each: identify the function, describe the form, situate in cultural context, identify how a Grade 12 cultural-studies analysis would address both. Aim for 10–12 sentences.
Answer Key

Sample on coming-of-age ceremonies: Quinceañera (Mexican/Latin American 15th-birthday rite) and bat mitzvah (Jewish 12th-year girls' coming-of-age). Both use van Gennep's three stages: separation (preparation period), liminality (the ceremony itself), incorporation (recognised as young adult). Form differs: Quinceañera includes Catholic Mass, court of damas and chambelanes, "changing of the shoes," father–daughter waltz; bat mitzvah includes Torah reading (the young woman reads a portion in Hebrew, with full liturgical responsibility), often a celebration meal, gifts; the religious framing is Catholic vs Jewish, with different theological content. Cultural context: Quinceañera carries Mexican/Latin American Catholic and family-based identity, often combined with deeply gendered expectations; bat mitzvah carries Jewish religious-textual identity, with a more egalitarian contemporary framing (girls receive equal treatment with boys in most modern Jewish communities, though Orthodox communities have their own traditions). Function: both mark transition from child to young adult, transmit gendered cultural-religious identity, bind extended family. A Grade 12 analysis would name the shared function (rite of passage), the formal differences (religious / ceremonial), and the contextual specifics (which culture, which historical period, what gendered expectations) — refusing to flatten either into the other. Marks: 2 per culture's analysis; 2 for the comparison/contrast; 2 for the social-science framing.

22
[5]E · STSE Application
STSE / Real-World Application: A Toronto fashion brand markets a "Boho Festival Collection" featuring fake-feathered headpieces, dreamcatcher prints, and "tribal" patterned skirts. Marketing copy refers to "tribal spirit" without naming any specific Indigenous nation. Apply Unit 4 concepts to analyse this and propose a more respectful approach. (5–7 sentences.)
Answer Key

(1) The headdress is sacred in Plains-nation traditions — earned, not decorative. (2) "Tribal" without specifying flattens hundreds of distinct cultures into a generic exotic-Other aesthetic — Edward Said called this Orientalism; here applied to Indigenous peoples. (3) All four elements of appropriation are present: power asymmetry (mainstream brand / marginalised peoples), meaning ignored, no credit/compensation, distortion of sacred practice. (4) The brand also commodifies — turning ceremonial significance into market goods stripped of context. (5) Respectful approach: collaborate with specific Indigenous designers (e.g., Manitobah Mukluks, Sage's Daughter, Pam Baker designs); credit and compensate; restrict sacred elements; focus on items source community has authorised for commercial use; contribute back to Indigenous communities. Marks: 1 each for: sacred-symbol point, generic-tribal critique, appropriation framework, commodification, proposal.

Reflect on what you have learned from doing your Independent Study Project (ISP) in HSC4M. Address: (a) one concept from the course that became more usable through your research, (b) one challenge in the research process and how you addressed it, (c) one finding from your ISP, (d) one thing you would do differently next time. Aim for 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Marks: 1 each for four reflection-elements with specific detail. Strong reflection moves beyond "I learned a lot" toward concrete instances — names a specific concept (e.g., intersectionality, cultural relativism, surveillance capitalism) and shows how the ISP work tested or refined it; names a specific methodological challenge (e.g., recruiting participants, deciding on coding categories) and the resolution; presents a specific finding with appropriate hedging; identifies a specific change for future research (e.g., better proposal scoping, more time on literature review, earlier ethics review).
