Topic 4.1 — Globalization Theories
Question 1
George Ritzer's "McDonaldization" thesis (1993) is best understood as a version of:
Solution: Ritzer's "McDonaldization of Society" argues that the principles of fast-food production — efficiency, calculability, predictability, control — are spreading globally and increasing uniformity. This is the classic cultural homogenization thesis. Critics (Marwan Kraidy, Pieterse) emphasize hybridization; Appadurai emphasizes heterogenization. All three are happening at once in different sectors.
Question 2
Arjun Appadurai's "five -scapes" (ethnoscape, technoscape, financescape, mediascape, ideoscape) framework emphasizes:
Solution: Appadurai (Modernity at Large, 1996) emphasises that ethnoscape (people), technoscape (technology), financescape (capital), mediascape (media images), and ideoscape (ideologies) flow at different speeds — capital moves faster than people; media faster than capital. The resulting "disjunctures" produce the uneven, textured character of globalisation. A Mumbai call-centre worker watches Toronto Raptors games but cannot easily emigrate.
Topic 4.2 — Media & Digital Culture
Question 3
Marshall McLuhan's famous claim "the medium is the message" means:
Solution: McLuhan (Understanding Media, 1964) argued that the form of a medium structures thought independently of content. Print produces linear, sequenced thought; television produces synchronous mass-audience experience; smartphones produce fragmented, always-on subjectivity. The Canadian theorist's framework remains useful because it directs analysis to the structural properties of media, not just their content.
Question 4
Shoshana Zuboff's term "surveillance capitalism" (2019) describes:
Solution: Zuboff (The Age of Surveillance Capitalism, 2019) describes a new business logic — first developed at Google, now widespread — in which the platforms' product is not really what they appear to sell but the prediction of user behaviour, sold to advertisers. Users provide "behavioural surplus" (the data exhaust of online activity) which is mined to refine prediction and intervention. The framework is now influential across media studies and digital sociology.
Topic 4.3 — Cultural Appropriation
Question 5
The disciplined definition of cultural appropriation includes which key elements?
Solution: Susan Scafidi (Who Owns Culture?, 2005) and Adrienne Keene's Native Appropriations work define it precisely: (1) dominant culture borrowing from marginalised culture (power asymmetry), (2) ignoring meaning, (3) no credit/compensation, (4) often distorting sacred or significant practices. A two-way borrowing between cultures of roughly equal power is cultural exchange, not appropriation. The concept's bite comes from the power asymmetry — not from any cross-cultural borrowing at all.
Question 6
A non-Indigenous Halloween costume company sells a "Pocahontas Costume" featuring a feathered headdress, fringed dress, and tomahawk.
This case fits cultural appropriation because:
Solution: All four elements of appropriation are present: power asymmetry (dominant company / marginalised people), meaning ignored (the headdress is earned through specific deeds and ceremonies; it is not decorative), no credit/consent/compensation, and distortion (the cartoon-Pocahontas conflation erases the actual historical Powhatan history of Matoaka's kidnapping, forced marriage, and death at age 21).
Topic 4.4 — Migration & Diaspora
Question 7
The concept of diaspora originally referred to:
Solution: "Diaspora" originally described Jewish dispersal after the Babylonian exile (c. 586 BCE), then the long history of dispersed Jewish communities globally. The concept has been extended to describe other dispersed communities — Palestinian, Armenian, African (forced through the trans-Atlantic slave trade), Tamil (post-1983 civil war), Somali, Punjabi, and many others. Modern diaspora studies emphasises maintained ties (real or imagined) to a homeland across generations.
Question 8
Mary Waters's concept of "ethnic options" describes:
Solution: Mary Waters's Ethnic Options (1990, on European-Americans) showed that third-generation white ethnics often have substantial choice about whether and when to claim heritage — Polish-American identity can be a once-a-year perogy-festival affair (Herbert Gans's "symbolic ethnicity") or a daily commitment. The framework applies less to racialized communities, whose ethnicity is more often imposed than chosen.
Topic 4.5 — Cultural Revitalization
Question 9
The Māori language revitalization movement in Aotearoa New Zealand began significant systemic momentum with:
Solution: Kohanga Reo (language nests, started 1982) are immersion preschools where Māori elders teach pre-school-age children in Te Reo Māori. The model proved highly successful and was followed by Kura Kaupapa Māori (Māori-medium primary schools, 1985) and Wharekura (secondary, 1989). The 1987 Māori Language Act established Te Reo as official. The model has inspired similar movements globally — Hawaiian language schools, Sámi institutions, Mohawk Tsi Tyónnheht Onkwawén:na.
Question 10
The 2021 discovery of unmarked graves at former residential school sites (Kamloops, Marieval, Brandon, others) had what impact on Canadian public discourse?
Solution: The May 2021 announcement of 215 unmarked graves at the Kamloops Indian Residential School site, followed by similar announcements at other former sites, transformed Canadian public discourse on residential schools. The federal government had earlier introduced the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation; in 2021 it became a federal statutory holiday. Many provincial actions followed (Ontario teacher training, museum exhibitions). The discoveries did not resolve reconciliation — they made the work harder to ignore but did not complete it.
