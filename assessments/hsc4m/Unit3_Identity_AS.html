Topic 3.1 — Socialization & Identity
Question 1
Cooley's "looking-glass self" (1902) describes:
Solution: Cooley's "looking-glass self": we form self-image by imagining how others perceive us, interpret their response, and feel pride or shame accordingly. The self is fundamentally social — formed in interaction, not in isolation. Mead's "generalized other" extends this to internalised community expectations.
Question 2
James Marcia's identity-status model identifies four positions in identity formation. A person who has committed to an identity WITHOUT having explored alternatives is in the status of:
Solution: Marcia (1966) — four statuses: Diffusion (no exploration, no commitment), Foreclosure (commitment without exploration — accepting parents' identity wholesale), Moratorium (active exploration without commitment), Achievement (commitment after exploration). University-age students often move from moratorium toward achievement.
Topic 3.2 — Gender & Sexuality Across Cultures
Question 3
Anthropologist Margaret Mead's Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies (1935) was significant because it:
Solution: Mead's three New Guinea cultures (Arapesh, Mundugumor, Tchambuli) showed dramatically different gender-role patterns; her argument was that gender is culturally constructed, not biologically determined. Later anthropologists have questioned specifics but the larger principle has been confirmed cross-culturally many times.
Question 4
The term "two-spirit" was coined at a 1990 Indigenous LGBT gathering in Winnipeg. It is used to:
Solution: "Two-spirit" is an umbrella term for traditional Indigenous gender/sexual roles that pre-existed colonial gender impositions — Anishinaabe niizh manidoowag, Lakota winkte, Cree iskwêhkân, and many others. The term explicitly distinguishes these from LGBTQ+ categories of Western origin, positioning them within Indigenous traditions of gender diversity that residential schools and Indian Act provisions had suppressed.
Topic 3.3 — Race & Ethnicity
Question 5
The contemporary social-science consensus on race is that:
Solution: The disciplined dual position: biologically, race is not a natural kind — the genetic variation within any "race" exceeds variation between races (American Anthropological Association, 1998). Sociologically, race is a real, powerful social fact that produces real outcomes (housing, hiring, policing). Both must be held simultaneously. The categories were historically constructed by colonial taxonomy (Linnaeus 1758, Blumenbach 1775) to justify slavery and colonization.
Question 6
In Canada, the Indian Act (1876) created a legal-administrative category of "Indian status." Until 1985 amendments (Bill C-31), an Indigenous woman who married a non-Indigenous man:
Solution: Pre-1985 Indian Act rules used patrilineal descent for Indian status — Indigenous women who married non-Indigenous men lost their status, but Indigenous men who married non-Indigenous women kept theirs (and the non-Indigenous wives gained status). Bill C-31 (1985) addressed major inequities; further reforms in Bill C-3 (2010) and Bill S-3 (2017) addressed remaining issues. The example shows how race is administratively constructed by state power, layered with gender, not by nature.
Topic 3.4 — Intersectionality
Question 7
Kimberlé Crenshaw coined the term intersectionality in:
Solution: Crenshaw's 1989 essay "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" in the University of Chicago Legal Forum coined the term. Her observation: anti-discrimination law treated race and sex as separate single-axis categories, so Black women who experienced specifically Black-female discrimination kept losing cases — the law could not see their position. Her 1991 follow-up "Mapping the Margins" extended the framework. Roots in Black feminist scholarship — Combahee River Collective (1977), Patricia Hill Collins, bell hooks.
Question 8
Intersectionality as Crenshaw originally framed it asks us to analyse:
Solution: Intersectionality attends to specific configurations of multiple axes (race × gender × class × sexuality × ability × etc.) as they interact to shape lived experience. The framework applies to privilege as well as oppression. Crenshaw has objected to versions that reduce it to "everyone has multiple identities" — the original is about specific power configurations, not unlimited categorical proliferation.
Topic 3.5 — Multicultural Canada & Indigenous Identity
Question 9
Canada became the first country to adopt official multiculturalism as state policy in:
Solution: Pierre Elliott Trudeau announced multiculturalism as official policy in the House of Commons on October 8, 1971. The Canadian Multiculturalism Act followed in 1988; Section 27 of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) recognizes multicultural heritage. The framing positions Canada as a "mosaic" rather than American-style "melting pot" — though critics including Indigenous scholars and racialized-immigrant scholars argue it erases settler colonialism and reduces difference to celebration without addressing structural inequality.
Question 10
The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (2008–2015) issued how many Calls to Action, and what was its central legal finding regarding residential schools?
Solution: The TRC issued 94 Calls to Action in 2015 and found the residential-school system constituted cultural genocide. Calls 62, 63 mandate Indigenous education in K-12; Calls 80–83 establish the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (September 30, federal statutory holiday since 2021). The MMIWG Inquiry (2016–2019) issued 231 Calls for Justice — a related but distinct document.
