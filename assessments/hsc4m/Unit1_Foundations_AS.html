Topic 1.1–1.2 — Defining Culture & Its Characteristics
Question 1
In Tylor's classic 1871 definition, culture is described as a "complex whole" that includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society. The KEY emphasis of this definition is that culture is:
Solution: Tylor's foundational move was to define culture as learned ("acquired ... as a member of society"). The five working characteristics of culture all build on this: learned, shared, symbolic, integrated, dynamic. The phrase "complex whole" emphasises integration; "as a member of society" emphasises learning and sharing.
Question 2
Material culture refers to physical objects produced and used by a society. Non-material culture includes:
Solution: Non-material culture = the intangible meaning system: beliefs, values, norms, language, ideologies, knowledge. The two interlock — a smartphone (material) carries non-material elements like the etiquette of when to answer and the algorithms that shape what we see.
Question 3
A child born in Mumbai but raised in Toronto from infancy will, by adulthood, speak English without an Indian accent, find Canadian humour funny, and likely have Canadian values and habits. This observation supports which key feature of culture?
Solution: The child's cultural competence in Canadian culture, despite their Indian origin, demonstrates that culture is acquired through socialization (enculturation), not biologically inherited. There is no genetic basis for cultural difference — a profound point with anti-racist implications.
Topic 1.3 — Cultural Universals & Particulars
Question 4
George Peter Murdock and other anthropologists have identified features that appear in every known human society — language, kinship systems, religion, food taboos, music, art, and a story about death. These are called:
Solution: Cultural universals are categories every society uses (everyone has language; everyone has some marriage form). Cultural particulars are the specific local forms (English vs Mandarin; Nayar visiting husbands vs nuclear-family marriage). Universals do not mean uniform content.
Question 5
An anthropologist describes the Nayar of southwest India in the 1950s: women had multiple "visiting husbands" who lived with their own matrilineal families. The husband-wife unit did not co-reside.
From an ETIC (outsider/analyst's) perspective, anthropologists today treat this arrangement as:
Solution: Contemporary anthropology treats the Nayar visiting-husband system as a culturally particular form of the universal category "marriage." The category is expanded, not narrowed; the emic (insider) view of Nayar themselves was that this WAS marriage, with its own kinship logic. Ranking it inferior would be ethnocentric.
Topic 1.4 — Ethnocentrism & Cultural Relativism
Question 6
William Graham Sumner (1906) coined the term "ethnocentrism" to describe:
Solution: Ethnocentrism = treating one's own culture as the implicit ruler for evaluating others. It operates below conscious notice: calling another culture's food "gross" or another society's family arrangement "weird" usually says more about the speaker than about the practice.
Question 7
Franz Boas and his students developed the principle of cultural relativism. The principle requires:
Solution: Cultural relativism is a methodological discipline: understand a practice in its own context before judging it. Most contemporary anthropologists accept methodological cultural relativism but reject ethical/moral relativism (the claim that any practice is acceptable as long as someone's culture endorses it). The grown-up move combines context-sensitivity AND universal-rights commitment.
Topic 1.5 — Three Social-Science Lenses
Question 8
Which method is the SIGNATURE method of cultural anthropology?
Solution: Ethnographic fieldwork — long-term participant-observation producing what Clifford Geertz called "thick description" — is the anthropological signature. Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) is the founding example. Strength: depth and contextual richness. Limit: generalizability.
Question 9
Émile Durkheim, Max Weber, and Karl Marx are foundational to which discipline?
Solution: Durkheim (social facts, solidarity, anomie), Weber (bureaucracy, rationalization, charisma), and Marx (class, capitalism, ideology) are the founding sociological theorists, focused on the structure and functioning of modern industrial societies. Boas and Malinowski are foundational to anthropology; Freud, Mead (G.H., not Margaret), and James to psychology.
Question 10
The acronym "WEIRD" — applied to psychology samples that have historically biased the discipline — stands for:
Solution: WEIRD = Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010 in Behavioral and Brain Sciences). Most psychology experiments have recruited undergraduate students at Western universities, who may not be representative of "human nature" generally. The acronym critiques psychology's generalizability and pushes the field toward more cross-cultural sampling.
