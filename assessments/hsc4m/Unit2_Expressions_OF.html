Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define linguistic relativity and distinguish the strong ("determinism") version from the weak ("influence") version. Which version is supported by contemporary experimental evidence?
Answer Key

Linguistic relativity = the proposal that language structure shapes thought (Sapir & Whorf, 1930s). Strong version (determinism): language DETERMINES thought; mostly rejected. Weak version (influence): language INFLUENCES thought — attention, categorical ease, perception of small distinctions; supported by experiments (e.g., Boroditsky's Kuuk Thaayorre / spatial-orientation work, Russian blue-shade studies). 1 mark per part.

2
[2]
In Durkheim's framing, religion is fundamentally:
Answer Key

B. Durkheim's Elementary Forms (1912) argued religion's primary function is social — what people worship is the community in symbolic form; ritual produces "collective effervescence" that binds the group. The framing is sociological; it does not require taking a position on theological truth.

3
[3]
Name and define van Gennep's three stages of a rite of passage. Give one example of each stage from a familiar Canadian (or other) ceremony.
Answer Key

Separation — initiate removed from ordinary life (graduation candidate not at school the morning of; bar/bat mitzvah preparation period). Liminality — betwixt-and-between threshold phase (the moment of standing in cap-and-gown before crossing the stage; the moment under the chuppah before being declared married). Incorporation — re-entry with new social status (diploma in hand at the reception; signing the marriage register). 1 mark per stage with appropriate example.

4
[2]
Define the difference between an artwork's function and its form, using a specific Inuit print (e.g., Kenojuak Ashevak's The Enchanted Owl) as illustration.
Answer Key

Form = the formal qualities (line, colour, composition); in The Enchanted Owl: bold black outline, symmetrical composition, rhythmic patterning of feathers, stonecut technique. Function = the social purposes (spiritual symbol, economic sustainment of Cape Dorset co-operative, national symbol on stamps and currency). 1 mark each.

5
[3]
Define each: (a) griot, (b) powwow, (c) polyrhythm. Specify a culture and region for each.
Answer Key

(a) Griot — West African specialised praise-singer/historian who memorises genealogies and royal histories in song; Manding, Mandé, Wolof traditions in West Africa. (b) Powwow — Indigenous North American social and ceremonial gathering centering drum and dance; widespread across Turtle Island including Anishinaabe, Cree, Dakota, and many other nations. (c) Polyrhythm — simultaneous use of multiple distinct rhythms; foundational to West African and Afro-diasporic music (Yoruba batá, Cuban son, jazz). 1 mark each.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"Among the Sami of northern Scandinavia, the noun ceavvi denotes a particular crust on snow that has been hardened by alternating freeze-thaw cycles — strong enough for reindeer to walk on without breaking through. The Sami language has dozens of such terms for snow types, each with practical and cosmological significance."
Analyse this passage in 5–6 sentences using linguistic relativity, non-material culture, and environment-and-culture interconnection. What does the case suggest about the relationship between language, knowledge, and survival? What danger arises when such a language is lost?
Answer Key

Strong answers: (1) The Sami snow vocabulary encodes ecological knowledge essential to herding survival — language is the carrier of detailed environmental knowledge accumulated over generations. (2) Linguistic relativity (weak version): having ready-made labels for snow types directs Sami attention to distinctions an outsider might miss; the language amplifies attention to ecologically-significant differences. (3) The vocabulary is non-material culture — intangible but practically critical. (4) When a language with such ecological encoding is lost, the practical knowledge it carries can be lost too — UNESCO estimates of 3,000 endangered languages by 2100 carry this risk. (5) The case illustrates the integration of environment and culture: the Sami cosmology, kinship, and economy all rest in part on this vocabulary. Marks: 1 each for accurate use of three concepts; 1 for the survival claim; 1 for the loss-implication.

7
[5]
Compare and contrast Indigenous oral tradition (e.g., Anishinaabe oral storytelling) and Western written history as ways of preserving cultural memory. In 5–7 sentences, identify what each carries, what each loses, and why social scientists treat both as legitimate forms of knowledge.
Answer Key

Strong answers: (1) Indigenous oral tradition carries relational, performative, contextual knowledge — the telling is part of the meaning; ceremonial protocols govern who tells, when, and to whom; the same story may be told differently in different contexts depending on what the audience needs. (2) Western written history offers reproducibility, indexability, and survives the death of any single teller. (3) Oral tradition loses the unedited record (no transcript) and depends on community continuity. Written history loses embodied performance, tone, the immediate relational obligation between teller and listener. (4) Social scientists treat both as legitimate because each preserves different aspects of cultural memory; the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (1996) and the TRC (2015) explicitly affirmed oral history as legitimate evidence. (5) The Wampum belt tradition in Haudenosaunee culture pairs visual record (the belt) with oral performance (the song / recitation) — an integrated technology of memory. Marks: 1 for each function, 1 for the legitimacy claim, 1 for synthesis.

8
[3]
A non-Indigenous teacher proposes incorporating Anishinaabe smudging into the school's weekly assembly. As a thoughtful cultural-studies student, what considerations and questions would you raise? Identify at least three.
Answer Key

Considerations: (1) Consultation — has the school consulted with local Indigenous community / Elder / Indigenous Education Council? (2) Authenticity / proper protocol — who will lead? smudging requires proper teachings about sage/sweetgrass/cedar/tobacco and the ceremonial framing; improvised by non-Indigenous staff it can be appropriative. (3) Consent and accommodation — students from religious traditions that prohibit participation in another religion's ceremony need clear opt-out; some Christian, Jewish, Muslim, and Hindu families may object. (4) Risk of tokenism — does the smudging substitute for substantive Indigenous content in curriculum (TRC Calls 62, 63)? Marks: 1 each for three considered points.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[6]
Write a clear, well-structured paragraph (7–9 sentences) responding to: "Religious worldview is not just personal belief — it shapes the legal and ecological practice of a society." Include a thesis, two specific examples from different cultures, and a nuanced conclusion. Reference at least one specific Indigenous worldview and one other (Abrahamic, Buddhist, secular).
Answer Key

Look for: (1) clear THESIS supporting the prompt; (2) Indigenous-worldview example — e.g., Anishinaabe/Cree relational worldview of land as kin (Turtle Island), informing treaty understanding that land cannot be sold because it is not property; consequences for environmental law and Idle No More; (3) contrasting example — Christian "dominion over the fish of the sea" (Genesis 1:28) producing European property law and resource extraction; OR Buddhist non-attachment shaping Bhutan's Gross National Happiness rather than GDP-only economic policy; OR secular humanism shaping French laïcité and its specific approach to public religion. (4) nuanced conclusion: worldview shapes practice, but also internal contestation within every tradition. Marks: 1 thesis; 2 each for two examples with specific detail; 1 conclusion.

10
[3]
Convert this weak claim into a strong analytical thesis: "Music is important to culture."
Answer Key

Sample: "Music functions as a non-textual cultural archive — encoding genealogies in the West African griot tradition, embodying healing in the Anishinaabe Jingle Dance, and carrying diasporic identity in Tamil-Canadian Carnatic teaching — such that the loss of a musical tradition is also the loss of a memory technology." Mark for specificity (1), debatability (1), evidence-readiness (1).

11
[3]
In one or two sentences each, distinguish: animism, polytheism, monotheism. Give one cultural example of each.
Answer Key

Animism — attribution of spiritual essence to natural beings (Anishinaabe and many Indigenous worldviews; Shinto in Japan). Polytheism — recognition of many gods (Hinduism, ancient Greek and Roman religion, Yoruba Orisha tradition). Monotheism — recognition of one supreme God (Judaism, Christianity, Islam). 1 mark each.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[6]
A Canadian fashion brand markets a "Boho Festival Collection" that includes feathered headdresses, dreamcatcher prints, and "tribal" patterned skirts. Marketing copy refers to "tribal spirit" without naming any specific Indigenous nation.
Apply Unit 2 concepts (symbol, sacred element, function/form, cultural transmission, ethics) to analyse this campaign. In 5–7 sentences, identify what is happening culturally, what is at stake, and what a more respectful approach would look like.
Answer Key

Strong answers: (1) The headdress is a sacred element in Plains-nation traditions — earned through specific deeds and ceremonies; using it as a costume is the use of a sacred symbol stripped of meaning and consent. (2) "Tribal" without specific naming flattens hundreds of distinct cultures into a colonial-era aesthetic — generic "exotic Other"; this is what Edward Said called Orientalism applied to Indigenous peoples. (3) The function of the original (ritual authority, spiritual protection) is replaced with the function of the fashion item (commercial differentiation, festival aesthetic). (4) Form survives, function and meaning are erased — that is the appropriative move. (5) A respectful approach would: collaborate with specific Indigenous designers, credit and compensate, restrict sacred elements, focus on items the source community has authorised for commercial use, and contribute back to Indigenous communities. Examples: Section 35-respecting Indigenous-led companies like Manitobah Mukluks, Sage's Daughter. Marks: 1 sacred-symbol point; 1 generic-tribal critique; 1 form/function analysis; 1 power asymmetry; 1 respectful-approach proposal; 1 communication/conventions.

13
[3]
STSE / Real-world application: Indigenous language nests (e.g., Māori Kohanga Reo since 1982, Mohawk Tsi Tyónnheht Onkwawén:na since 1985) have revitalised endangered languages by immersing pre-school children in fluent-elder environments. In 3–4 sentences, explain why these programs target the earliest age group, and identify ONE structural condition that supports their success.
Answer Key

Strong answers: (1) Children acquire language most fluently in early childhood — the critical-period hypothesis from linguistics says native-like fluency requires early exposure (typically before age 7–10); immersion at pre-school age produces the closest-to-native speakers. (2) Children then carry the language into school and family life, creating intergenerational transmission. (3) Structural conditions for success: dedicated funding, fluent-elder teachers, family commitment, schools that continue immersion (Kura Kaupapa Māori in NZ, Kanien'kéha primary schools in Mohawk communities), official language status / political recognition. Marks: 1 for critical-period reasoning; 1 for intergenerational transmission; 1 for structural condition.

Identify a contemporary RITUAL in your own life or your community (graduation, sports team initiation, holiday celebration, religious ceremony, citizenship ceremony, online wedding). In 3–5 sentences, analyse it using van Gennep's three stages AND identify what it transmits or reinforces culturally.
