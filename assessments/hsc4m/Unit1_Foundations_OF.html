Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
Define culture as social scientists use the term. Name three of the five working characteristics of culture.
Answer Key

Culture = the learned, shared, symbolic, transmitted system of meanings of a group; or Tylor's "complex whole" of knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom acquired as a member of society. Three of five characteristics: learned, shared, symbolic, integrated, dynamic. (1 mark definition; 1 mark for three characteristics.)

2
[3]
Distinguish cultural universal, cultural particular, and cultural relativism. Give a one-sentence example of each.
Answer Key

Cultural universal — feature in every known culture (every society has language; every society has some form of marriage). Cultural particular — the specific local form a universal takes (the language is Tamil; the marriage form is Nayar visiting-husband). Cultural relativism — the methodological discipline of analysing a practice in its own context before judging it (the Nayar visiting-husband system makes sense within their matrilineal kinship). Mark: 1 each for accurate definition + example.

3
[2]
In Pike's emic/etic distinction, which describes the INSIDER perspective (using the culture's own categories)?
Answer Key

Emic = the insider's view, using local categories. Etic = the outsider/analyst's view, using comparative categories. Both are needed in social-science work.

4
[3]
Compare the typical method, scale, and signature theorist of cultural anthropology, sociology, and cultural psychology.
Answer Key

Anthropology: ethnographic fieldwork (long-term participant observation), single community / small group, thick description; Malinowski, Boas, Mead, Geertz. Sociology: surveys, comparative case studies, statistical analysis; whole societies and institutions; Durkheim, Weber, Marx. Cultural psychology: controlled experiments, surveys, cross-cultural comparison; individuals and groups; Markus & Kitayama, Hofstede. 1 mark per discipline.

5
[3]
Define and give one example each: (a) ethnocentrism, (b) cultural relativism (methodological), (c) ethnographic fieldwork.
Answer Key

(a) Ethnocentrism — judging another culture by the standards of one's own. Example: calling another society's food "weird" or another marriage system "uncivilised." (b) Methodological cultural relativism — research stance: understand a practice in its own context before judging. Example: studying Nayar visiting-husband marriage by asking how it fits the surrounding matrilineal kinship rather than measuring it against the nuclear family. (c) Ethnographic fieldwork — extended participant observation in a community. Example: Malinowski's two years in the Trobriand Islands (1915–18). 1 mark each.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
"The marketplace at Antakya in southern Türkiye operates on a six-day cycle: prices are negotiated, eye contact and tea offered to potential customers, deals concluded with handshakes rather than receipts. A Canadian backpacker, attempting to compare prices online and refusing to drink the offered tea, finds vendors strangely hostile."
Analyse this scenario in 4–6 sentences using at least three concepts from Unit 1 (e.g., emic/etic perspective, non-material culture, ethnocentrism, cultural relativism, cultural particular). Identify what the backpacker is missing.
Answer Key

Strong answers will use concepts like: (1) the marketplace customs (negotiation, tea offering, handshake) are non-material culture that the backpacker fails to read; (2) by treating the Antakya market by Canadian standards (online price comparison, transactional efficiency), the backpacker engages in ethnocentrism; (3) a cultural-relativist / emic approach would understand the tea-and-haggling ritual as a relationship-building practice essential to the marketplace's social fabric, not an obstacle to it; (4) the cultural particular (Antakyan haggling ritual) is the local form of the universal of marketplace exchange. Marks: 1 for accurate scenario reading; 3 for the three concept applications; 1 for synthesis.

7
[4]
A practice once considered "culturally relative" by some early-20th-century anthropologists, but now widely condemned, is female genital cutting (FGC). In 4–6 sentences, explain how a careful contemporary social scientist would approach this practice — combining methodological cultural relativism (understanding context) with commitment to universal human rights. Avoid both blanket judgment and moral abdication.
Answer Key

Strong answers: (1) Methodological relativism step — understand the social, religious, kinship, and economic context in which FGC has been practised (initiation, marriage requirement, perceived purity), without dismissing the practitioners as "barbaric"; recognise that women within these cultures often defend the practice. (2) Universal rights step — recognise that FGC violates rights to physical integrity, freedom from harm, and (for children) consent, which the UDHR (1948), CRC (1989), and WHO frameworks identify as universal. (3) Recognise that cultures contain internal disagreement: African feminist movements (Tostan, the Inter-African Committee) have led successful community-led campaigns. (4) The most effective change has come from within communities, not from external moral condemnation, which often backfires. Marks: 1 for relativism step, 1 for rights step, 1 for internal critique, 1 for nuance.

8
[4]
Pick a familiar cultural practice (a meal, a sport, a religious or family ritual). Identify two ways it would be analysed differently by an anthropologist than by a sociologist. Be specific about the questions and methods each would use.
Answer Key

Strong answer chooses a practice and contrasts: Anthropologist — would ethnographically study how participants experience and give meaning to the practice; e.g., what does the Friday-night family dinner mean to participants? What is the symbolic role of food/seating/order? Methods: participant observation, interview, learning local terms. Sociologist — would study the structural conditions of the practice; e.g., who has time and economic resources for Friday-night family dinners? How does class, gender division of labour, work-schedules shape who eats together? Methods: surveys, time-use data, statistical comparison across classes. 2 marks for each lens, with appropriate detail.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write a clear, well-structured paragraph (6–8 sentences) responding to this prompt: "Is the concept of cultural relativism still useful in a world where we recognise universal human rights?" Include a thesis statement, evidence, and a nuanced conclusion. Use at least one specific example.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear THESIS that takes a position — typically that methodological cultural relativism is still useful but moral relativism is not; (2) reasoned argument distinguishing the two; (3) a specific example (e.g., contrasting harmless cultural difference like food/dress that requires relativist tolerance versus practices that violate human rights); (4) acknowledgement that human-rights universalism can itself be ethnocentric if imposed without engagement; (5) clear, well-organised prose with proper grammar. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 argument and example, 1 nuance, 1 conventions.

10
[4]
Rewrite the following weak topic sentence into a strong analytical claim suitable for a Grade 12 cultural-studies paper: "Culture is interesting and varies a lot across the world."
Answer Key

Sample strong thesis: "Although the human capacity for culture is universal, its specific expressions — kinship structures, religious cosmologies, gender categories — vary so radically that cross-cultural analysis requires both the etic (analyst's) discipline of comparison and the emic (insider's) discipline of contextual understanding." Look for: (1) a debatable claim, (2) specific concepts (universal / particular, emic / etic), (3) precise language, (4) a take rather than a description. 1 mark each.

11
[3]
In one or two sentences, accurately distinguish: subculture, counterculture, and dominant culture. Provide one Canadian example of each.
Answer Key

Subculture — distinct cultural group within a larger society sharing enough but with its own practices (Canadian hip-hop community; Sikh-Canadian community; competitive figure-skating culture). Counterculture — subculture that consciously opposes dominant values (the 1960s hippie movement; environmental direct-action groups). Dominant culture — most powerful, mainstream cultural system (in Canada, broadly Anglo-settler urban professional culture, though increasingly multicultural). 1 mark each.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
A Canadian intern at an international NGO is sent to a rural community in Bangladesh to help with a women's microfinance program. After two weeks, she writes home: "It's frustrating — they keep wanting to ask their husbands' permission for things, and the women's meetings have to stop for prayer five times a day. The program would work better if they were more like us."
Apply Unit 1 concepts to critique this intern's framing. Identify at least three problems with her approach, name the relevant concepts, and suggest a better starting orientation for her work.
Answer Key

Issues: (1) Ethnocentrism — measuring local practice against Canadian assumptions ("if they were more like us"). (2) Failure of cultural relativism (methodological) — she has not tried to understand why women's decision-making is structured the way it is in this kinship/religious context. (3) Failure to recognise cultures contain internal diversity — many Bangladeshi women lead, work, run businesses; her framing flattens. (4) Top-down framing — she positions herself as having the answers; OCAP-like principles would centre community direction. (5) Mistakes cultural particulars (prayer five times, deference rituals) for "obstacles" rather than fabric of the community. Better orientation: spend time, learn language and customs, listen first, build trust, ask community what they need. Marks: 3 for three problems with concept names, 2 for the better-orientation suggestion.

13
[4]
STSE / Real-world application: The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) found that the Canadian Indian Residential School system attempted to "kill the Indian in the child." Using Unit 1 concepts (especially enculturation, cultural relativism, dominant culture, cultural genocide), explain in 4–6 sentences why the TRC framed this finding in terms of cultural — rather than only physical — genocide.
Answer Key

Strong answers: (1) Residential schools targeted the enculturation process — they removed children from family/community, the primary agents of cultural transmission, during the formative years. (2) Children were punished for speaking Indigenous languages and practising Indigenous cultural elements, severing language transmission across generations. (3) The schools represented the dominant settler culture's attempt to replace Indigenous cultures with Anglo-Christian-settler norms. (4) The legal concept of cultural genocide (developed in part by Raphael Lemkin who coined "genocide") includes targeting cultural transmission even without targeting physical existence. (5) The TRC's finding emphasizes that the loss of language, kinship, ceremony, and worldview is itself a profound harm, not "merely cultural." Marks: 1 for enculturation, 1 for transmission interruption, 1 for dominant-culture imposition, 1 for cultural genocide framing.

14
[3]
Apply ONE social-science lens (anthropology, sociology, OR psychology) to analyse why Toronto-area secondary school students often feel pressure to dress in a particular brand or style. Be explicit about the lens you are using.
Answer Key

Anthropology focus: ethnographic study of style as cultural-meaning system in a specific school; how clothing functions as a symbolic communication of group belonging, status, and identity; the role of subcultures within the school. Sociology focus: how peer-group structure, class-based capital, and consumer-capitalism shape adolescent conformity; gendered expectations of appearance. Psychology focus: developmental need for belonging (Maslow, Erikson), conformity (Asch), social-identity theory (Tajfel), self-image and group identification. 1 mark for lens clearly named, 2 for application with appropriate concepts/methods.
