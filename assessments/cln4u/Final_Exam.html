Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[3]B · Theory
Distinguish briefly: (a) natural law; (b) legal positivism; (c) legal realism. Give one named theorist for each.
Answer Key

(a) Natural law: law has internal moral content; an unjust law fails to bind. Aquinas, Finnis, Fuller. (b) Positivism: law's validity depends on its source, not its content. Austin, Hart, Bentham. (c) Realism: doctrine under-determines decisions; psychology and policy fill gaps. Holmes, Llewellyn, Frank.

2
[3]B · Sources
List five primary sources of Canadian law. For each, give one Canadian example.
Answer Key

(1) Constitution (Constitution Act, 1982). (2) Statute (Criminal Code; Cannabis Act). (3) Regulation (Cannabis Regulations; Highway Traffic Act Regulations). (4) Common law (Donoghue v Stevenson; tort principles). (5) Civil Code (Civil Code of Quebec). (6) Indigenous legal traditions (Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee; recognised by s. 35 and UNDRIP Act 2021).

3
[3]B · Constitution
Identify the key features added by (a) the Constitution Act, 1867; (b) the Constitution Act, 1982; (c) the Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
Answer Key

(a) 1867: federation, division of powers (ss. 91/92), constitutional monarchy, responsible government. (b) 1982: patriation, Charter (Part I), Aboriginal and treaty rights (s. 35), amending formula (Part V). (c) Charter: constitutionalised bill of rights, includes ss. 1 limits and 33 override.

4
[2]B · Federalism
The federal "peace, order and good government" (POGG) clause is found in:
Answer Key

A. The opening words of s. 91 grant Parliament authority "to make Laws for the Peace, Order, and good Government of Canada" in relation to all matters not exclusively assigned to provinces. POGG is the federal residual power.

5
[3]B · Charter
Identify the four sub-elements of the proportionality stage of the Oakes test, in order.
Answer Key

(1) Pressing and substantial objective; (2) rational connection; (3) minimal impairment; (4) proportionality of effects (salutary effects outweigh deleterious effects). Preceded by the threshold question of "prescribed by law."

6
[3]C · International Sources
List the primary and subsidiary sources of international law set out in Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute.
Answer Key

Primary: (a) international conventions (treaties); (b) international custom (state practice + opinio juris); (c) general principles of law recognised by civilised nations. Subsidiary: (d) judicial decisions and the writings of the most highly qualified publicists. To these modern lists add (e) acts of international organisations and (f) "soft law."

7
[2]C · VCLT
VCLT Article 31 requires treaty interpretation according to:
Answer Key

A. VCLT Article 31(1). Article 32 permits preparatory work only as a supplementary means, to confirm or resolve ambiguity.

8
[3]D · UDHR
Briefly describe the legal status and historical significance of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).
Answer Key

The UDHR was GA Resolution 217 A (III), adopted 10 December 1948. It was not, on adoption, a treaty and therefore not, in itself, legally binding. Drafted by a multicultural committee (Eleanor Roosevelt chairing; Canadian John Peters Humphrey produced the first draft), it set out civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights in 30 articles. Many provisions now reflect customary international law; some (prohibitions on torture, slavery) are jus cogens. The UDHR is the foundation document of the modern human-rights regime and the touchstone for the 1966 ICCPR and ICESCR.

9
[3]D · Treaties
Define each: (a) non-refoulement under the 1951 Refugee Convention; (b) non-refoulement under CAT; (c) the principle of distinction in IHL.
Answer Key

(a) Refugee non-refoulement (Article 33): No state shall return a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on Convention grounds; narrow exceptions in 33(2). (b) CAT non-refoulement (Article 3): Absolute; no return to torture, no exceptions. (c) Distinction (API Article 48): Parties must distinguish at all times between combatants and civilians and may only direct attacks at military objectives.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
10
[8]B · Constitutional Analysis
Parliament enacts a federal "Truth in Political Advertising Act" that requires any political advertisement broadcast in Canada to be accompanied by a written disclosure of the funder and the funder's bonus history. The Act applies during the 28 days before a federal election. Violations attract administrative fines.
Conduct a full Charter analysis. Address: (a) which Charter rights are limited; (b) the prima facie infringement; (c) the section 1 Oakes analysis (all four sub-elements); (d) whether section 33 could be properly invoked if the statute were challenged. Aim for 10–14 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) S. 2(b) freedom of expression — political advertising is core protected speech. Possibly s. 8 if disclosure of bonus history involves privacy concerns. (b) Prima facie infringement of s. 2(b) — content-based regulation. (c) S. 1 analysis: (i) prescribed by law — yes. (ii) Pressing and substantial — electoral integrity and informed voting (Harper v Canada (AG)). (iii) Rational connection — disclosure can support informed voters. (iv) Minimal impairment — depends on tailoring; disclosure is less impairing than prohibition; specific format (text scroll) might still be modest. (v) Proportionality — salutary effects (transparency) plausibly outweigh deleterious effects (some chill). Likely upheld with proper tailoring. (d) S. 33 could override s. 2; it cannot override s. 3 democratic rights (not engaged here). Override of s. 2 would attract significant political cost. Marks: 1 right, 1 infringement, 4 Oakes (1 each), 2 s. 33 analysis.

11
[6]C · International Reasoning
Two states are parties to a treaty containing a vague obligation to "take all appropriate measures" to prevent transboundary harm. One state argues this requires due-diligence regulation; the other argues it leaves discretion to each state.
Apply VCLT Article 31 systematically — ordinary meaning, context, object and purpose, supplementary means. Conclude on which interpretation prevails.
Answer Key

Ordinary meaning: "All appropriate measures" suggests obligation of conduct, not merely best efforts. Context: If neighbouring articles use "endeavour" or "best efforts," the drafters chose stronger language deliberately. Object and purpose: Preventing transboundary harm — weak interpretation would defeat the object. Supplementary (Article 32): Negotiating history may show what level of commitment was contemplated. Conclusion: The first interpretation (due-diligence regulation) is more consistent with Article 31; the obligation requires positive measures, not bare discretion.

12
[6]D · R2P Analysis
A government has been systematically targeting an ethnic minority. Hundreds of villages destroyed. 100,000 displaced. International community calls for action under R2P.
Apply the three pillars of R2P. Identify which atrocity crime(s) are most likely engaged. Assess whether Pillar 3 action is legally available and what constraints apply. 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Pillar 1: State has failed to protect; is perpetrator. Pillar 2: International assistance has been or should be offered (diplomatic, sanctions). Pillar 3: Requires Security Council authorisation for coercive measures. Atrocity crimes: ethnic cleansing (forced displacement); crimes against humanity (widespread attack on civilians); possibly genocide if dolus specialis (specific intent to destroy the group "as such") is shown. Constraints: SC unanimity required; veto possible; Article 51 not available (no armed attack on outside state); unilateral humanitarian intervention is legally contested. South Africa v Israel and Gambia v Myanmar show alternative ICJ pathways.

13
[5]E · Self-Defence
Country J launches a small cross-border raid into Country K, killing 12 soldiers and destroying a radar. K is a UN member. K responds with airstrikes on three of J's military bases.
Apply Article 51 and the Caroline test. Was the initial raid an "armed attack"? Are K's airstrikes lawful self-defence? Are they proportionate? Address each step.
Answer Key

Armed attack threshold: 12 dead soldiers and destroyed radar is on the borderline; Nicaragua distinguishes armed attacks from lesser uses of force. If the raid is judged grave enough, Article 51 applies. Necessity: Were diplomatic options exhausted? In an ongoing-attack scenario, necessity is straightforward. Proportionality: Three airstrikes for one ground raid may be excessive depending on scale. Reporting: K must report to the SC. Conclusion: Borderline; proportionality is the key constraint; the response must not transform into wider conflict.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
14
[8]B/C · Comparative
Write a structured, well-organised paragraph (8–12 sentences) comparing the role of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms in Canadian law with the role of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in international law. Address (a) legal status; (b) enforcement; (c) influence on subsequent instruments; (d) one example of how each has been interpreted by a court.
Answer Key

Look for: Charter is constitutionally entrenched and binding on federal/provincial governments (s. 32); UDHR is a GA resolution, not binding in itself but its provisions have crystallised into custom and jus cogens. Charter enforced by Canadian courts (s. 24 remedies, s. 52(1) supremacy); UDHR enforced primarily through treaty-body and regional-court interpretation, not directly. Charter influenced regional/national bills of rights; UDHR influenced the ICCPR and ICESCR and many national constitutions. Charter example: R v Oakes [1986]; UDHR example: cited in many ECtHR judgments. Marks: 2 each — legal status, enforcement, influence, examples.

15
[6]A · IRAC Brief
Joaquim, a Canadian university student, is on summer travel in State X. He attends a protest where he displays a sign critical of State X's government. He is detained, held for 96 hours without charge, and ordered deported. State X is not a party to the ICCPR. Canada's foreign minister calls for his release.
Apply IRAC to the question: "Does international law provide Joaquim's rights any protection?" Address sources (treaties Canada has ratified, customary law, jus cogens), the legal status of the conduct, and the realistic remedies.
Answer Key

Issue: Whether international law provides protection for Joaquim given that State X is not party to the ICCPR. Rule: Customary international law and jus cogens bind all states regardless of treaty ratification. The prohibitions on torture, prolonged arbitrary detention, and persecution for political expression have customary status; arbitrary detention is widely treated as a violation of customary law. CAT non-refoulement applies regardless of refugee status. Application: Even without ICCPR, State X is bound by customary prohibitions. Detention without charge for 96 hours raises arbitrary-detention concerns. Deportation may be subject to non-refoulement if Joaquim faces persecution. Canada has diplomatic protection through consular services (VCCR 1963). Conclusion: Joaquim has limited but real international-law protection; remedies are diplomatic, customary, and via Canadian consular channels. Marks: 1 Issue, 2 Rule (custom + jus cogens), 2 Application, 1 Conclusion.

16
[6]D · Position Paper
In a 6–8 sentence position paper, argue for or against the following claim: "Canada should adopt monism for treaties — automatic domestic effect upon ratification — rather than the current dualist approach." Use appropriate legal vocabulary. Reference at least one Canadian case.
Answer Key

Look for: clear thesis; understanding that dualism currently requires implementing legislation; reference to Baker for the presumption of conformity in administrative law; Hape for customary international law's automatic reception; the policy argument for monism (more consistent compliance, easier domestic enforcement of treaty obligations) vs. dualism (democratic accountability, legislative oversight). Marks: 2 thesis, 2 explanation, 1 case reference, 1 conclusion + conventions.

17
[5]E · IHL Explainer
Write a 5–7 sentence explanation of the four core principles of international humanitarian law (distinction, proportionality, necessity, humanity), with one example illustrating how each applies in a modern conflict.
Answer Key

Look for: clear definitions; modern examples (urban warfare, drone strikes, dual-use targets); reference to API Articles 48 (distinction), 51(5)(b) (proportionality), and the general principle. Marks: 1 each principle definition + 1 example.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
18
[10]B/D · Extended Application
In 2025, the Government of Canada is considering legislation that would prohibit the wearing of religious symbols by public-sector employees (teachers, police officers, public-service managers). The legislation closely mirrors Quebec's Bill 21 but is federal in scope and would apply to federal public-sector employees nationwide. The government considers invoking s. 33.
Apply what you have learned to write an extended analysis (12–16 sentences) addressing: (a) which Charter sections are engaged; (b) the section 1 Oakes analysis if s. 33 were not invoked; (c) the relationship of the legislation to s. 35 and to UNDRIP; (d) the relevance of Canada's international human-rights obligations under ICCPR Articles 18 (religious freedom) and 26 (equality); (e) your recommendation as a constitutional adviser.
Answer Key

(a) S. 2(a) religion, s. 15 equality (religion is enumerated ground), s. 7 if liberty is engaged. (b) S. 1: pressing and substantial — laicité/secularism — recognised but contested as urgent in Canadian context; rational connection plausible; minimal impairment fails for absolute bans of all symbols across diverse roles; proportionality questionable given that those affected are predominantly visible religious minorities. (c) S. 35 not directly engaged but Indigenous spiritual practices could be implicated. UNDRIP Articles 11 (cultural practices) and 12 (spiritual traditions) are relevant. (d) ICCPR Article 18 protects religious freedom; Article 26 equality; UN Human Rights Committee has reviewed similar laws unfavourably. Presumption of conformity applies. (e) Recommendation: do not pursue absolute ban; consider role-specific, harm-targeted measures; consultation with religious and Indigenous communities; engagement with UN treaty bodies. Marks: 2 each — Charter, s. 1 Oakes, s. 35/UNDRIP, ICCPR, recommendation.

19
[8]C/E · Moot-Style Application
An unnamed coastal state (State M) is suing an inland state (State L) at the ICJ. State L's industrial waste contaminates a transboundary river that flows into State M's coastal waters, harming fisheries. Both states are parties to the 1992 Convention on the Protection and Use of Transboundary Watercourses and to UNCLOS. State M asks the ICJ to order State L to (a) cease the contamination; (b) pay damages.
Apply your knowledge of international law to: (a) identify the relevant sources (treaty, customary, general principle); (b) assess ICJ jurisdiction; (c) assess what relief State M can realistically obtain; (d) identify enforcement risks. 10–12 sentences.
Answer Key

(a) Sources: 1992 Watercourses Convention (treaty); UNCLOS (treaty); customary no-harm rule (Trail Smelter; Article 3 of ILC Draft Articles on State Responsibility); general principles of state responsibility for environmental damage. (b) Jurisdiction: Watercourses Convention may have a compromissory clause; UNCLOS has dispute-settlement Part XV. Optional-clause declarations may also apply. (c) Relief: Cease and desist order (provisional measures under Article 41 of ICJ Statute available); damages possible under state-responsibility rules; standard of proof is preponderance of evidence. Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay (2010) is a leading precedent. (d) Enforcement risk: ICJ judgments are binding (Article 94 UN Charter) but enforcement depends on SC or compliance; coastal-fishery disputes have mixed enforcement records. Marks: 2 sources, 2 jurisdiction, 2 relief, 2 enforcement.

[7]A · STSE Position Paper
STSE / Real-World Application: Choose one of the following 2025 issues:
  1. The continued use of s. 33 in Canada by provincial governments.
  2. Canada's response to recent ICC arrest warrants involving allied states.
  3. Climate change as an emerging issue in international and domestic law (e.g., environment-justice claims).
  4. The expansion of universal jurisdiction prosecutions in Canada under the Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act.
  5. The treatment of Indigenous legal traditions as a source of Canadian law under the UNDRIP Act (2021).
Write a structured 10–14 sentence position paper. Identify the legal issue, the major arguments, the relevant constitutional and international-law concepts, and your reasoned conclusion. Cite at least two specific provisions, cases, or instruments studied in this course.
Answer Key

Strong responses identify the issue precisely, name the relevant constitutional and international-law instruments (Charter sections; UNDRIP Articles; ICCPR provisions; Rome Statute; relevant cases), present opposing arguments fairly, and offer a reasoned personal conclusion supported by legal reasoning rather than mere opinion. Marks: 2 issue identification, 4 legal concepts (1 each), 4 argument quality, 4 conclusion + conventions.
