Topic 3.1 — Sources of International Law
Question 1
Article 38 of the ICJ Statute lists the sources of international law. Which is NOT among the primary sources?
Solution: Article 38(1)(a)–(c) lists treaties, custom, and general principles as primary sources; (d) lists judicial decisions and writings as subsidiary means. GA resolutions are not in Article 38; they can evidence opinio juris but are not, in themselves, a primary source.
Question 2
Customary international law has two elements:
Solution: Customary law requires (i) state practice (widespread, representative, consistent) and (ii) opinio juris (the belief that the practice is legally required). Mere habit is not custom.
Question 3
Jus cogens norms are:
Solution: Jus cogens = peremptory norms. VCLT Article 53: a treaty conflicting with jus cogens is void. The recognised list is narrow: prohibitions on aggression, genocide, slavery, apartheid, torture, and basic norms of humanitarian law.
Topic 3.2 & 3.3 — Sovereignty & the VCLT
Question 4
The four criteria of statehood in the Montevideo Convention (1933) are:
Solution: Article 1 Montevideo Convention. The four criteria are widely treated as customary international law. The declaratory theory holds that satisfying these makes an entity a state; recognition is acknowledgement, not creation.
Question 5
Universal jurisdiction allows a state to prosecute:
Solution: Universal jurisdiction is narrow: it applies to a defined set of crimes of universal concern. Canada used universal jurisdiction in R v Munyaneza (2009) for crimes committed in Rwanda. The doctrine does not allow prosecution of any crime anywhere.
Question 6
Article 31 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT 1969) requires a treaty to be interpreted:
Solution: VCLT Article 31(1) — "ordinary meaning + context + object and purpose, in good faith." Article 32 permits preparatory work as a supplementary means.
Topic 3.4 & 3.5 — UN System and ICJ
Question 7
The five permanent members (P5) of the UN Security Council are:
Solution: The P5 — China, France, Russia, UK, US — were the leading WWII allies. Each has a veto on substantive resolutions (Article 27).
Question 8
A General Assembly resolution is generally:
Solution: GA resolutions are recommendatory. They can evidence opinio juris and contribute to customary law formation, but they do not, in themselves, bind member states.
Question 9
ICJ contentious jurisdiction over a state requires:
Solution: ICJ contentious jurisdiction is consensual. Consent may be expressed by special agreement (compromis), by a compromissory clause in a treaty, or by an optional-clause declaration under Article 36(2). Without consent, the ICJ cannot hear a state-to-state dispute.
Topic 3.6 — Reception in Canada
Question 10
Canada is generally:
Solution: Canada follows the British tradition: dualist for treaties (which require implementing legislation to bind domestically), monist for custom (which is received directly into the common law since R v Hape [2007] 2 SCR 292).
Question 11
The "presumption of conformity" in Canadian law holds that:
Solution: The presumption of conformity (Hape) is the interpretive presumption that the legislature does not intend to legislate in violation of international law. The presumption can be overcome by clear contrary language, but it gives international law significant interpretive force.
