Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Briefly describe the key features added by each of the following constitutional documents: (a) Constitution Act, 1867; (b) Constitution Act, 1982; (c) Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
Answer Key

(a) Constitution Act, 1867: created the federation; divided powers in ss. 91/92; established Parliament and provincial legislatures; entrenched constitutional monarchy and responsible government. (b) Constitution Act, 1982: patriated the Constitution; added the Charter (Part I); recognised Aboriginal and treaty rights in s. 35 (Part II); created an amending formula (Part V). (c) Charter: constitutionalised a bill of rights binding on federal and provincial governments; includes fundamental freedoms, democratic, mobility, legal, equality, and language rights, with s. 1 limitations and s. 33 notwithstanding clause.

2
[2]
Section 91 of the Constitution Act, 1867 assigns which of the following powers to Parliament?
Answer Key

A. Section 91 enumerates federal powers — criminal law (s. 91(27)), banking, trade and commerce, plus the POGG residual. The others belong to s. 92 (provincial).

3
[3]
List the four sub-elements of the proportionality stage of the Oakes test.
Answer Key

(1) Pressing and substantial objective; (2) rational connection between means and objective; (3) minimal impairment of the right; (4) proportionality of effects (salutary effects outweigh deleterious effects). Preceded by "prescribed by law."

4
[2]
Identify the case that established the Aboriginal-rights analytical framework in 1990:
Answer Key

B. R v Sparrow [1990] 1 SCR 1075 set out the three-part s. 35 framework: existing right, prima facie infringement, justification.

5
[3]
Name three Charter sections that cannot be overridden by section 33, and explain why this limitation matters.
Answer Key

Sections that cannot be overridden include: democratic rights (ss. 3–5), mobility (s. 6), language rights (ss. 16–23), and s. 28 (sex equality). The limitation matters because these are treated as core elements of Canadian democracy and equality that legislatures cannot suspend. The fact that s. 33 cannot override the right to vote (s. 3) means that even a government using the notwithstanding clause cannot shut down democratic accountability.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Suppose Ontario enacts a statute providing: "No person may demonstrate within 100 metres of a hospital, with a fine of up to $5,000 for violation. This Act shall operate notwithstanding sections 2 and 7 of the Charter."
Analyse this hypothetical. Address: (a) which Charter rights are limited; (b) whether section 33 is properly invoked; (c) whether section 1 analysis remains relevant; (d) whether any Charter challenge can still proceed despite the override.
Answer Key

(a) Charter rights limited: s. 2(b) freedom of expression (demonstration is protected expression); s. 2(c) freedom of peaceful assembly; arguably s. 7 if the fine is high enough to engage liberty. (b) S. 33 invocation: The statute identifies the Act and specific Charter sections (2 and 7); explicit invocation satisfied. Five-year sunset operates automatically. (c) S. 1 analysis: Largely irrelevant for the overridden sections. (d) Charter challenges still open: Challenges based on sections not within s. 33's scope (e.g., s. 3 if voting were affected — not here; s. 6 mobility — not engaged; ss. 16–23 language — not engaged). The override does not apply to provincial constitutional review for ultra vires. Marks: 1 each part for the technical analysis, 1 for overall coherence.

7
[4]
Quebec's Bill 21 (2019), invoked s. 33 to prohibit certain public-sector workers from wearing religious symbols. The federal government did not invoke s. 33 against any Charter section in the same period.
Analyse the constitutional and political reasons why Quebec might invoke s. 33 while the federal government has not. Does the asymmetry reveal anything about the design of s. 33?
Answer Key

Quebec's use reflects long-standing concerns about secularism (laïcité) and identity, and historic grievance about patriation without Quebec's consent. The federal government has not invoked s. 33 because of higher political cost (national-level Charter override is a high-stakes move) and because federal legislation rarely touches the most-overridden Charter sections directly. The asymmetry reveals s. 33 as a federalism-balance device: it lets sub-national majorities preserve choices that conflict with the (federal) Charter. Critics argue it is increasingly used as routine politics rather than as the rare emergency device some drafters foresaw.

8
[4]
A federal statute prohibits any commercial advertisement of cannabis products on television. A cannabis manufacturer challenges under section 2(b) of the Charter.
Apply the Oakes test in 6–8 sentences to predict the likely outcome. Use RJR-MacDonald v Canada [1995] (the tobacco-advertising case) as your guide.
Answer Key

Section 2(b) protects commercial expression (Irwin Toy). Prima facie infringement is clear: the statute restricts protected expression. Section 1 analysis: (i) prescribed by law — yes; (ii) pressing and substantial — public health is a recognised objective (RJR-MacDonald); (iii) rational connection — limiting advertising can rationally reduce uptake; (iv) minimal impairment — the critical question. Does a total advertising ban impair minimally? A targeted ban (e.g., not in children's programming, with restrictions on content) might be a less-impairing alternative. RJR-MacDonald struck down the original tobacco-advertising ban for being too broad; a later, more tailored ban was upheld in Canada (AG) v JTI-Macdonald Corp [2007]. The likely outcome here depends on the breadth and tailoring of the cannabis ban.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write a structured paragraph (5–7 sentences) explaining the difference between section 1 and section 33 of the Charter, using technical legal vocabulary correctly. Include at least one example of when each applies.
Answer Key

Section 1 is the limitations clause, applied by courts under the Oakes test to determine whether a prima facie infringement of a right is a reasonable limit "demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society." Section 33 is the notwithstanding clause, used by Parliament or a provincial legislature to override sections 2 and 7–15 of the Charter by explicit declaration in legislation, with a five-year sunset. Section 1 is court-administered; section 33 is legislature-administered. Example of s. 1: the Supreme Court applied it in RJR-MacDonald [1995] (tobacco advertising) and Carter [2015] (assisted dying). Example of s. 33: Quebec's Bill 21 (2019, religious symbols). Marks: 1 each for s. 1 definition, s. 33 definition, contrast, two examples, conventions.

10
[4]
Rewrite the following weak statement into a strong, debatable thesis: "The notwithstanding clause is sometimes used by governments."
Answer Key

Sample strong thesis: "Quebec's invocation of section 33 in Bill 21 represented a shift in Canadian constitutionalism from the notwithstanding clause as an emergency mechanism to a routine policy instrument, raising fundamental questions about the equilibrium the framers intended between parliamentary sovereignty and judicial review." Look for: specific reference (Quebec/Bill 21), interpretive verb ("represented a shift"), debatable claim, named constitutional concept.

11
[3]
Compare and contrast Aboriginal title (recognised in Tsilhqot'in Nation, 2014) and ordinary fee-simple ownership. Three key differences, in 3–4 sentences.
Answer Key

(1) Aboriginal title is communal (held by the Nation, not individuals); fee simple is held by individuals or corporations. (2) Aboriginal title is inalienable except to the Crown; fee simple is freely transferable. (3) Aboriginal title carries the right to determine how the land is used and includes economic benefit (Tsilhqot'in); fee simple has analogous rights but no constitutional protection under s. 35. Also: Aboriginal title is sui generis and constitutionally entrenched.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
A First Nation in northern Ontario, holding s. 35 Aboriginal rights to fish on a particular river for food, social, and ceremonial purposes, faces a new federal regulation under the Fisheries Act that closes the river to all fishing for one year to allow stock recovery. The Nation has not been consulted.
Apply the Sparrow framework to analyse whether the regulation can be justified. Address each step of the test.
Answer Key

Step 1 — Existing right: Food, social, and ceremonial fishing is an established Aboriginal right (Sparrow, Delgamuukw). Existing since not extinguished prior to 1982. Step 2 — Infringement: Total closure is a meaningful diminution. Prima facie infringement established. Step 3 — Justification: (i) Valid objective — conservation is recognised in Sparrow. (ii) Fiduciary duty: (a) priority — after conservation, Aboriginal food fishing must have priority over other users; (b) minimal impairment — could a less-restrictive measure (gear restrictions, quotas, designated days) achieve conservation? (c) consultation — the failure to consult is itself a violation of Haida Nation; (d) compensation/accommodation where appropriate. Conclusion: The regulation is likely unjustified given absence of consultation and possibly less-restrictive alternatives. Marks: 1 each step + 1 for consultation analysis + 1 for conclusion.

13
[4]
A municipality enacts a by-law prohibiting overnight street camping in a city park where unhoused people sleep. A constitutional challenge under section 7 of the Charter is brought.
In 5–7 sentences, analyse whether section 7 is engaged, what the principles of fundamental justice require, and what the likely outcome would be. (Hint: Adams v Victoria 2008 BCCA, Abbotsford v Shantz 2015 BCSC.)
Answer Key

Section 7 protects life, liberty, and security of the person, deprivable only in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice. The lower courts (Adams, Shantz) have found that prohibiting overnight street sleep when shelter capacity is inadequate engages s. 7 by denying people a place to sleep, exposing them to harm — engaging both liberty and security. The principles of fundamental justice include arbitrariness, overbreadth, and gross disproportionality (Bedford [2013]). A by-law that effectively prohibits sleeping anywhere when shelters are full may be arbitrary or grossly disproportionate. Likely outcome: the by-law is unconstitutional unless either (i) sufficient shelter is provided or (ii) some alternative is allowed at night. Marks: 1 s. 7 engagement, 2 principles of fundamental justice, 1 outcome with reasons, 1 conventions.

Marks: 1 context, 2 transfer of concept, 1 specific example.
