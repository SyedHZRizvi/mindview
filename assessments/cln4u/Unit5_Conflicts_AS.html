Topic 5.1 — Article 2(4)
Question 1
Article 2(4) of the UN Charter prohibits:
Solution: Article 2(4) is a broad prohibition on military force, including threats. Economic sanctions are generally not covered (they are governed by other rules). The two textual exceptions are Article 51 self-defence and Chapter VII Security Council authorisation.
Question 2
In Nicaragua v United States [1986], the ICJ held that Article 2(4) reflects:
Solution: The ICJ in Nicaragua confirmed that Article 2(4) reflects customary international law. The Court distinguished armed attacks (triggering self-defence) from lesser uses of force (prohibited but not, alone, triggering self-defence).
Topic 5.2 & 5.3 — Self-Defence & Chapter VII
Question 3
The customary requirements of self-defence (the Caroline test) are:
Solution: The Caroline formula of 1837 requires that the need for self-defence be "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation" (necessity) and that the response be proportionate to the attack. Both apply even after Article 51.
Question 4
The Security Council's binding authority to take coercive measures is in:
Solution: Chapter VI is about pacific settlement (recommendatory). Chapter VII (Articles 39–51) governs coercive measures: Article 39 determination, Article 41 non-forcible measures, Article 42 forcible measures. Article 51 is the self-defence provision within Chapter VII.
Question 5
The Security Council's standard authorisation-of-force language is:
Solution: "All necessary measures" (or "all necessary means") is the SC's standard authorisation-of-force language, used in Resolution 678 (Iraq 1990), Resolution 1973 (Libya 2011), and elsewhere. This language signals the most muscular form of authorisation.
Topic 5.4 — IHL
Question 6
The four Geneva Conventions of 1949 protect:
Solution: The four 1949 Conventions, together with the 1977 Additional Protocols, are the core of IHL. They distinguish four categories of protected persons and apply universally (196 states parties).
Question 7
The principle of distinction in IHL requires:
Solution: Distinction (Additional Protocol I, Article 48) is the cornerstone IHL principle. Combatants and military objectives may be attacked; civilians and civilian objects may not. Failure to distinguish (indiscriminate attacks) is prohibited and may constitute a war crime.
Topic 5.5 — ICC
Question 8
The four core crimes within the jurisdiction of the International Criminal Court (Rome Statute Articles 5–8 bis) are:
Solution: Rome Statute Article 5 lists the four core crimes. Aggression was added effectively in 2018 via the Kampala amendments. Each has detailed elements in subsequent articles (Article 6 genocide, Article 7 crimes against humanity, Article 8 war crimes, Article 8 bis aggression).
Question 9
The principle of complementarity in the Rome Statute (Articles 17–20) means:
Solution: Complementarity respects state sovereignty. Domestic prosecution is the first option; the ICC steps in only when the state is unwilling or unable to investigate or prosecute genuinely (Article 17). Inability or unwillingness must be shown — e.g., a sham trial or institutional collapse.
Topic 5.6 — Peaceful Settlement
Question 10
UN peacekeeping was largely invented during which crisis?
Solution: UNEF (United Nations Emergency Force) was created during the 1956 Suez Crisis, largely on Canadian initiative led by Lester B. Pearson, who received the 1957 Nobel Peace Prize for his role. Peacekeeping is not in the Charter; it occupies the conceptual space between Chapter VI and Chapter VII.
Question 11
Article 33 of the UN Charter lists methods of:
Solution: Article 33 requires states first to seek peaceful resolution; it lists the major methods. The list is non-exhaustive ("or other peaceful means of their own choice"). Each method differs in degree of binding effect and party control.
