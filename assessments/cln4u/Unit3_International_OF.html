Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
List and briefly describe the primary and subsidiary sources of international law in Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute.
Answer Key

Primary: (a) International conventions (treaties) — written agreements binding the parties. (b) International custom — state practice + opinio juris. (c) General principles of law — doctrines common to major legal systems (estoppel, good faith). Subsidiary: (d) Judicial decisions and writings — interpretive aids, not independent sources.

2
[2]
A peremptory norm (jus cogens) is:
Answer Key

A. Jus cogens norms admit of no derogation; they bind all states; a treaty conflicting with them is void (VCLT Article 53). The accepted list is narrow: prohibitions on aggression, genocide, slavery, apartheid, torture, and basic IHL norms.

3
[3]
Define and distinguish (a) territorial jurisdiction; (b) nationality jurisdiction; (c) universal jurisdiction. Give one example of how Canada uses each.
Answer Key

(a) Territorial — over conduct within Canada's territory (most criminal offences). (b) Nationality — over conduct by Canadian nationals abroad (s. 7(4.1) Criminal Code on child sex tourism). (c) Universal — over a small set of grave crimes regardless of location (CAHWCA 2000; R v Munyaneza 2009 for Rwandan genocide).

4
[2]
Article 31(1) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties requires interpretation according to:
Answer Key

A. VCLT Article 31(1). Article 32 permits preparatory work only as a supplementary means.

5
[3]
Distinguish the General Assembly and the Security Council in terms of (a) membership; (b) voting; (c) legal effect of resolutions.
Answer Key

(a) Membership: GA — all UN members; SC — 15 members (5 permanent + 10 elected). (b) Voting: GA — one vote each; majorities or two-thirds for important questions; SC — 9 affirmative needed, no P5 negative vote (veto). (c) Effect: GA resolutions are recommendatory (except internal matters); SC Chapter VII resolutions are binding on all members (Article 25).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
State A and State B are parties to a treaty that requires each to "take all necessary measures to prevent transboundary pollution of shared rivers." State A's industrial waste pollutes a river shared with State B; State A says it has not "taken" any active polluting measure but cannot afford the regulatory enforcement to stop private polluters.
Apply VCLT Article 31 to interpret "take all necessary measures." Does State A's argument succeed? Analyse in 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Ordinary meaning: "Take" is active language; "all necessary measures" suggests obligation of conduct (not merely result), but a demanding one. Context: Other treaty provisions presumably elaborate or qualify. Object and purpose: Preventing transboundary pollution — interpreting "necessary measures" weakly would defeat object. Application: State A's argument that it cannot afford enforcement does not satisfy a positive obligation. The treaty requires due diligence, including regulation of private actors. State A is in breach. Supplementary means: If ambiguity persists, look to preparatory work. Conclusion: State A's argument likely fails; the treaty obligation requires regulating private polluters as a "necessary measure." Marks: 1 each Article 31 element + 1 application + 1 conclusion.

7
[4]
Critics of the UN Security Council argue that the P5 veto undermines the legitimacy of the Council's decisions. Analyse this critique in 5–7 sentences. Consider both the political reasons for the veto and the practical consequences for cases like Syria (2011–) or Ukraine (2022).
Answer Key

The veto was a 1945 compromise: P5 cooperation was the price of their participation in the UN. Without the veto, the UN would not have been created, and many advocates argued for it as the realistic alternative to non-cooperation. Practical consequences: paralysis on Syria, Ukraine, Myanmar, where SC action has been blocked by Russian or Chinese vetoes. Yet on cases where P5 align (Korea 1950, Iraq 1990, Libya 2011) the Council can be decisive. Reform proposals (additional permanent members, restraint on veto for atrocity crimes) have not advanced. The veto is a structural feature reflecting the political reality the system was designed for, even as that reality has changed. Marks: 2 political reasons, 2 consequences, 1 critical assessment.

8
[4]
Canada has signed but not ratified Treaty X, an international convention on environmental protection.
What is the legal status of Treaty X in Canadian domestic law? How does the presumption of conformity affect cases interpreting Canadian statutes that touch on environmental protection? In 5–7 sentences, address Canada's dualist approach and the role of administrative law as illustrated in Baker v Canada [1999].
Answer Key

Canada is dualist for treaties: unsigned or unratified treaties have no direct domestic legal effect. Even ratified treaties without implementing legislation do not bind domestic courts directly. Baker v Canada [1999] 2 SCR 817 held, however, that the Convention on the Rights of the Child, though not implemented, informed the reasonableness review of an administrative decision affecting a Canadian-born child. The presumption of conformity (Hape) means Canadian statutes are interpreted, where possible, consistently with Canada's international obligations. Treaty X, even unratified, can have some interpretive weight as part of the legal landscape but cannot directly create rights. Marks: 1 status of unratified treaty, 1 dualism, 2 presumption of conformity / Baker, 1 conclusion.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write a structured paragraph (6–8 sentences) explaining the difference between an "armed attack" and a lesser "use of force" under the UN Charter, with reference to the ICJ's decision in Nicaragua v United States (1986).
Answer Key

Look for: definition of "armed attack" (most serious uses of force); definition of "use of force" (broader, includes lesser uses); the distinction's significance for Article 51 self-defence (only armed attacks trigger); reference to Nicaragua's articulation of the distinction; example (invasion = armed attack; support for armed group can be use of force but not necessarily armed attack); the consequence for legal responses (proportionate countermeasures vs. self-defence). Marks: 1 each distinction, 1 Nicaragua reference, 1 examples, 1 consequence, 1 conventions.

10
[4]
Rewrite the following weak statement into a strong thesis: "The UN is sometimes useful for resolving disputes."
Answer Key

Sample: "The UN Security Council's authorisation of force in Libya in 2011 demonstrates that Chapter VII collective security can function when the P5 align, even as its paralysis on Syria reveals that the structural veto remains the system's most consequential limit." Look for: specific reference, debatable claim, two contrasting examples, named legal mechanism.

11
[3]
In 3–4 sentences, compare the ICJ's contentious and advisory jurisdictions. Identify a major example of each.
Answer Key

Contentious: Disputes between states; requires consent; judgments are binding (e.g., Nicaragua v United States [1986]; The Gambia v Myanmar [2020]; South Africa v Israel [2024]). Advisory: Legal opinions on questions submitted by UN organs; not binding but authoritative (e.g., Legality of Nuclear Weapons [1996]; Wall in Occupied Palestinian Territory [2004]; Legal Consequences of Israel's Policies in Occupied Territories [2024]).

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Canada is a party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and to the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC). A federal statute permits, in extraordinary circumstances, deportation of a foreign national to a country where they may face a substantial risk of mistreatment. The deportee has Canadian-born children.
How would Canadian courts approach the constitutional and international dimensions of this case? Reference Suresh v Canada [2002] and Baker v Canada [1999], the presumption of conformity, and Charter section 7 in your 5–7 sentence analysis.
Answer Key

Suresh v Canada held that deportation to face torture violates Charter s. 7 except in extraordinary circumstances, applying CAT-derived non-refoulement through the principles of fundamental justice. Baker v Canada incorporated the Convention on the Rights of the Child's "best interests of the child" into administrative-law reasonableness review even though the CRC was not implementing-legislation in force. The presumption of conformity (Hape) means statutes are interpreted consistently with Canada's international obligations. Section 7 protects life, liberty, and security; deportation to a substantial risk of mistreatment engages security. The principles of fundamental justice include considerations from the Convention against Torture, ICCPR, and CRC. The deportee can challenge under both Charter s. 7 and administrative law informed by international human-rights treaties. Marks: 1 Suresh, 1 Baker, 1 conformity presumption, 1 Charter s. 7, 1 integration.

13
[4]
A small archipelago state asserts that the maritime boundary with its neighbour, drawn in 1955 in a colonial-era treaty, no longer reflects equitable allocation of resources. The neighbour invokes pacta sunt servanda (Article 26 VCLT) — treaties must be performed in good faith.
Analyse, in 5–7 sentences, what international-law doctrines and forums might be available to the archipelago state to challenge or modify the boundary.
Answer Key

Doctrines: (1) Rebus sic stantibus (VCLT Article 62) — fundamental change of circumstances; narrowly construed but possibly applicable if conditions on which the treaty rested have changed materially. (2) Equity in maritime delimitation — modern delimitation jurisprudence (North Sea Continental Shelf, Romania v Ukraine) emphasises equitable principles and proportionality. (3) UNCLOS provisions on maritime boundaries. Forums: ICJ (if jurisdiction), ITLOS, or arbitration under UNCLOS Annex VII. The archipelago state could also propose renegotiation under Article 33 of the UN Charter (peaceful settlement) before formal dispute settlement. Marks: 2 doctrines, 2 forums, 1 process.
