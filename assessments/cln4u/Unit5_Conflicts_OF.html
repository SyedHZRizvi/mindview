Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
State the two textual exceptions to Article 2(4)'s prohibition on the use of force. Cite the relevant Charter articles.
Answer Key

(1) Article 51 — self-defence (individual or collective) in response to an armed attack, until the Security Council takes measures necessary to maintain international peace and security. (2) Chapter VII (Articles 39–51) — Security Council authorisation of coercive measures, including force under Article 42. No other textual exception exists; humanitarian intervention without SC authorisation is legally contested.

2
[2]
The Caroline test for self-defence requires:
Answer Key

A. The 1837 Caroline formula is now customary international law. The need for self-defence must be "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation"; response must be proportionate to the attack.

3
[3]
List the four core principles of international humanitarian law (IHL).
Answer Key

Distinction — distinguish combatants from civilians; only attack military objectives. Proportionality — incidental civilian harm not excessive in relation to anticipated military advantage. Necessity — only force needed to achieve legitimate military objective. Humanity — avoid unnecessary suffering; prohibit certain weapons.

4
[2]
The four core crimes within ICC jurisdiction under the Rome Statute are:
Answer Key

A. Rome Statute Article 5. Each has detailed elements in subsequent articles. Aggression's ICC jurisdiction became effective in 2018 via the Kampala amendments.

5
[3]
List five methods of peaceful settlement of disputes under Article 33 of the UN Charter, with a one-line description of each.
Answer Key

Negotiation — direct between parties. Enquiry — impartial fact-finding. Mediation — third party suggests solutions. Conciliation — formal commission proposes settlement. Arbitration — binding tribunal (e.g., PCA). Judicial settlement — permanent court (ICJ, ITLOS). Also accepted: good offices, regional arrangements.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
State C launches a cross-border armoured assault into State D, capturing 200 sq km of D's territory. State D, with significant casualties, asks State E (a friendly NATO ally) for assistance.
Apply Article 51 of the UN Charter step-by-step. Is State D's resort to force lawful? May State E join the response? What customary requirements apply? Analyse necessity, proportionality, and the duty to report.
Answer Key

(1) Armed attack? Yes — invasion is the paradigmatic armed attack (Nicaragua). (2) State D individual self-defence: Article 51 permits military response. (3) State E collective self-defence: Permitted at State D's request (Article 51 covers "individual or collective"); State E must act at D's request, not independently. (4) Necessity: Ongoing assault makes necessity straightforward. (5) Proportionality: Defensive force must be commensurate with repelling the attack and restoring D's territory; not unlimited regime change. (6) Article 51 reporting: Measures taken must be reported to the SC. (7) Termination: Self-defence ends when SC has taken necessary measures or attack is repelled. Marks: 1 each — armed attack, individual SD, collective SD, necessity, proportionality + 0.5 reporting + 0.5 termination.

7
[4]
A military commander considers an airstrike on a residential building. Intelligence indicates an enemy command-and-control node on the third floor. The building has 150 civilian residents. The strike is planned with precision-guided munitions.
Apply the principles of distinction and proportionality. Identify all the analytical factors a commander must consider before authorising the strike. Reference Additional Protocol I.
Answer Key

Distinction (API Article 48): The C2 node is a lawful military objective (API Article 52(2)). The civilians are protected and may not be the object of attack. Distinction requires planning that targets the military objective and minimises civilian impact. Proportionality (API Article 51(5)(b)): Is the incidental civilian harm "excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated"? Killing 50+ civilians for one C2 node is likely excessive; 5 incidental injuries might not be. The calculation must be ex ante. Precautions (API Article 57): Consider alternative targets, weapons, timing, warnings. Marks: 1 distinction, 2 proportionality, 1 precautions.

8
[4]
A senior military commander, General X, is alleged to have committed crimes against humanity during a recent armed conflict. State Y, the state with primary jurisdiction, is a party to the Rome Statute but has formally announced it will not investigate or prosecute General X "for reasons of national reconciliation." Analyse whether the ICC can exercise jurisdiction. Address complementarity, the trigger, and Article 27.
Answer Key

Trigger: The Prosecutor can investigate proprio motu (with Pre-Trial Chamber authorisation), or via state referral or SC referral. Complementarity (Article 17): State Y's announced unwillingness to prosecute satisfies the inability/unwillingness requirement — admissibility likely. Article 27 (no immunity): Even high officials lack immunity for the four core crimes; if General X holds office, immunity does not protect him from ICC process. Practical: Arrest depends on cooperation; warrants may go unenforced if Y or another non-cooperating state shelters him. Marks: 1 each — trigger, complementarity, Article 27, practical limit.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write a structured 6–8 sentence paragraph distinguishing jus ad bellum (the law on the resort to force) from jus in bello (the law applicable during armed conflict). Explain why they apply independently and give one example of how a state might be acting lawfully under one but unlawfully under the other.
Answer Key

Look for: definitions of each regime; their independence (a state acting in lawful self-defence is still bound by IHL on the battlefield; a state that attacked first is still bound by IHL); example (a state defending itself lawfully under Article 51 could nonetheless commit war crimes during operations); UN Charter (jus ad bellum) and Geneva Conventions (jus in bello) as core sources. Marks: 1 each — definitions, independence, example, sources, conventions.

10
[4]
Rewrite the following weak statement into a strong thesis: "The UN Security Council is important."
Answer Key

Sample: "The Security Council's authorisation of force in Libya in 2011 (Resolution 1973) demonstrates Chapter VII's coercive potential when the P5 align, but its failure to act on Syria after 2011 reveals that the veto remains the system's binding constraint and the principal driver of selective enforcement." Look for: debatable claim, specific dates and resolutions, named legal mechanism.

11
[3]
In 3–4 sentences, distinguish among traditional peacekeeping, peace-enforcement, and Chapter VII collective security. Reference one example of each.
Answer Key

Traditional peacekeeping: Consent, impartiality, non-use of force except self-defence (UNEF 1956, UNFICYP 1964). Peace-enforcement: More robust mandates, sometimes Chapter VII, often without full consent (UNMISS, MONUSCO). Chapter VII collective security: SC-authorised coercive operations (Resolution 678 Iraq 1990; Resolution 1973 Libya 2011).

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
State F's air force conducts a strike on what it identifies as a "command bunker" in State G. The bunker is in fact a building also used as a school during the day; the strike kills 28 students and 4 enemy commanders.
Analyse the legal status of the strike. Is the building a military objective? Was distinction respected? Was proportionality? Could this constitute a war crime? Reference Rome Statute Article 8.
Answer Key

Military objective? Article 52(2) API: an object whose nature, location, purpose, or use makes an effective contribution to military action. If used as a command bunker, the building is at the relevant time a military objective; dual-use status (also a school) makes the analysis fact-specific. Distinction: Required attempt to distinguish; if commander knew children would be present, distinction analysis becomes harder. Proportionality (API Article 51(5)(b)): 28 dead children for 4 enemy commanders may be disproportionate; the question is whether the military advantage justified the foreseeable civilian harm. War crime (Rome Statute Article 8(2)(b)(iv)): "Intentionally launching an attack in the knowledge that such attack will cause incidental loss of life or injury to civilians … which would be clearly excessive in relation to the concrete and direct overall military advantage anticipated." If the threshold is met, individual criminal responsibility attaches. Marks: 1 military objective, 1 distinction, 2 proportionality, 1 war crime.

13
[4]
State H argues that its targeted drone strikes on alleged terrorist leaders in State I are lawful self-defence against non-state actors. State I has not consented and has not itself attacked State H.
Analyse the international-law arguments for and against. Address (a) whether self-defence applies against non-state actors after 9/11 (SCR 1368, 1373); (b) the "unable or unwilling" doctrine; (c) the limits imposed by sovereignty and necessity/proportionality.
Answer Key

(a) Self-defence against non-state actors: Post-9/11, SCR 1368 and 1373 recognised self-defence in response to terrorist attacks. (b) "Unable or unwilling": Some states argue that strikes in third-state territory are lawful when the host state is unable or unwilling to address the threat. The doctrine is contested; not all states accept it. (c) Limits: Necessity (less-restrictive alternatives — diplomatic, cooperation with host state, capture); proportionality (drone strikes must be commensurate with threat); sovereignty (host state's consent or genuine inability/unwillingness must be shown). The legal status remains debated; lawful conduct depends on careful analysis of all three. Marks: 1 each — post-9/11, unable/unwilling, necessity, proportionality.

14
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: Choose one current international conflict (Ukraine 2022–, Gaza 2023–, Sudan, Yemen, or another). In 4–5 sentences, analyse one legal issue it raises using concepts from Chapter 5 (jus ad bellum, jus in bello, ICC, peacekeeping, peaceful settlement).
Answer Key

Strong responses identify how the chapter transfers: jus ad bellum analysis of invasion; jus in bello analysis of conduct of hostilities; ICC warrants against named officials; SC paralysis and General Assembly responses (Uniting for Peace); ICJ proceedings (Gambia/Myanmar, South Africa/Israel). Marks: 1 conflict, 2 concept transfers, 1 specific legal issue.
