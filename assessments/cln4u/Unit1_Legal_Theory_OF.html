Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define each and give one example: (a) public law, (b) private law, (c) the rule of law.
Answer Key

(a) Public law — governs the relationship between the state and individuals (constitutional, criminal, administrative). Example: Criminal Code charge of theft. (b) Private law — governs relationships between individuals (contract, tort, property, family). Example: a landlord–tenant dispute. (c) Rule of law — government and citizens alike are bound by law applied predictably and equally. The Supreme Court in the Secession Reference (1998) identified it as an unwritten constitutional principle.

2
[2]
Which school of jurisprudence is associated with the claim that an unjust law is not properly a law?
Answer Key

B. Natural law. The maxim lex iniusta non est lex reflects the natural-law claim that moral validity is internal to the concept of law. Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae articulated the classical version.

3
[3]
List the five primary sources of Canadian law, with a one-line description of each.
Answer Key

Constitution — supreme law (Constitution Acts 1867 and 1982). Statute — Act of Parliament or a legislature. Regulation — delegated legislation under statutory authority. Common law — judge-made law from cases. Civil Code of Quebec (for private matters in Quebec). Increasingly recognised as a sixth: Indigenous legal traditions (s. 35, Sparrow, UNDRIP Act 2021).

4
[2]
What does stare decisis mean, and why does it matter in a common-law system?
Answer Key

Stare decisis ("to stand by what is decided") means courts are bound to follow earlier decisions of higher courts (and, less strictly, their own past decisions). It produces predictability and equal treatment. The Supreme Court of Canada can depart from its own precedents but does so cautiously (e.g., R v Henry [2005], Carter v Canada [2015]).

5
[3]
Distinguish between common-law and civil-law systems. Identify which tradition predominates in (a) Ontario, (b) Quebec (for private-law matters), (c) France.
Answer Key

Common law: judge-led, binding precedent, adversarial procedure. Civil law: code-led, persuasive precedent, inquisitorial procedure. (a) Ontario — common law (with federal statute on top). (b) Quebec — civil law for private matters (Civil Code of Quebec); common-law method for public matters. (c) France — civil law (Napoleonic descent).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
Parliament enacts a statute criminalising any public criticism of the Prime Minister, with a penalty of two years' imprisonment.
Apply both natural-law and positivist reasoning to this hypothetical. Which school would treat the statute as "law"? Would either school provide grounds for refusing to obey? In your view, which analysis is more persuasive and why?
Answer Key

Positivist: If properly enacted, the statute is law. Validity does not depend on content. Constitutional challenge under Charter s. 2(b) is a separate question. Natural-law: The statute attempts to criminalise the exercise of a fundamental human capacity (political speech). Aquinas/Finnis would deny it the moral authority of law. Fuller would point to the statute's incompatibility with the "inner morality of law" (clear advance notice, generality, etc.). Marks: 1 each school definition, 1 each application, 1 critical comparison.

7
[4]
In R v Lavallee [1990] 1 SCR 852, the Supreme Court allowed expert evidence on battered-woman syndrome and reshaped the doctrine of self-defence to incorporate women's experience of recurring intimate-partner violence.
How would each of the following theorists explain the decision? (a) A legal realist (Holmes); (b) a feminist jurisprudence scholar (MacKinnon).
Answer Key

Realist: The pre-Lavallee doctrine produced unjust results in a recurring fact-pattern. The Court's reasoning was responsive to social knowledge of battered-woman experience; policy considerations drove the doctrinal shift. Feminist: The "reasonable person" of self-defence law was historically a "reasonable man." Battered women's experience of recurring violence and power asymmetry was invisible. Lavallee incorporated women's experience into the substantive law, correcting a gendered exclusion. Marks: 2 each explanation, with reference to the decision.

8
[4]
Analyse the relationship between law and justice. Are they the same? Different? How? Use a 4–6 sentence response. Reference at least one example from Canadian law.
Answer Key

Law is a set of binding rules issued by recognised institutions; justice is a moral evaluation of whether those rules and their application are fair. They overlap but are not identical. A law can be unjust (residential-school statutes; the Indian Act for much of its history); justice can require law reform. Examples: the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action argue that Canadian law has not yet achieved justice for Indigenous peoples. Marks: 1 each definition, 2 relationship, 1 example.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Aisha, a delivery driver, is charged with theft under s. 322 of the Criminal Code after she accepted a customer's $50 gift card "for being so friendly" and used it later that day. Her employer's policy forbids accepting any gifts from customers; the customer was not coerced and freely offered the card.
Apply the IRAC method in a formal short brief: state the issue, the rule (s. 322), the application to the facts, and the conclusion. 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Issue: Did Aisha commit theft under s. 322 by accepting and using the gift card? Rule: s. 322 — "Everyone commits theft who fraudulently and without colour of right takes … anything with intent to deprive … the owner of it…" Theft requires actus reus (taking/conversion without colour of right) and mens rea (fraudulent intent, intent to deprive). Application: The customer voluntarily gave the gift card; there was no taking without consent. Aisha had colour of right (the customer's voluntary transfer). Mens rea of fraudulent intent is absent — she did not deceive the customer. The employer's policy creates an internal-discipline issue, not a theft. Conclusion: Aisha did not commit theft under s. 322. Marks: 1 each Issue, Rule statement, 2 Application, 1 Conclusion.

Rewrite the following weak topic sentence into a strong analytical thesis statement appropriate for a CLN4U essay: "The rule of law is important."
Answer Key

Sample: "The Supreme Court of Canada's recognition of the rule of law as an unwritten constitutional principle in the Secession Reference (1998) demonstrates that Canadian constitutionalism rests on substantive moral commitments — not merely procedural correctness — and forms a check on legislative power that complements the Charter." Marks: debatable claim (1), specific reference to text/decision (1), interpretive verb (1), conventions (1).

11
[3]
Compare and contrast a case brief and an IRAC paragraph in 3–4 sentences. Define each and explain when each is appropriate.
Answer Key

A case brief is a structured summary of a single judicial decision (facts, issue, holding, reasoning, disposition); used for studying precedents. An IRAC paragraph applies a rule to a set of facts (issue, rule, application, conclusion); used in essays, factums, and exam answers. Briefs are about a case; IRAC paragraphs use rules from cases to analyse new facts.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
A First Nations community sets a community-protocol rule prohibiting commercial fishing on a traditional ground during the spawning season. A non-Indigenous commercial fisher ignores the rule. The federal Crown charges him under the Fisheries Act; the community simultaneously brings a court application asserting an Aboriginal right.
Identify which sources of Canadian law are engaged by this scenario, and explain how they interact. Reference at least three of: Constitution, statute, common law, Indigenous legal traditions, UNDRIP.
Answer Key

(1) Constitution Act, 1982, s. 35 — Aboriginal rights are constitutionally affirmed; the Sparrow framework applies. (2) Federal statute — the Fisheries Act and its regulations purport to govern, but their application is subject to s. 35. (3) Common law — Sparrow, Marshall, and Mikisew Cree structure the analysis. (4) Indigenous legal tradition — the community-protocol rule is a source of law in its own right, increasingly given weight by courts. (5) UNDRIP Act (2021) — federal statutory commitment to align law with UNDRIP, including resource and self-government rights. Marks: 1 each source correctly identified, 2 for explaining interactions.

13
[4]
A government proposes a new statute creating an offence of "social-media harassment of public officials." Applying what you have learned, write a 4–6 sentence response identifying (a) which schools of jurisprudence would find the proposal acceptable, (b) which would not, and (c) what your assessment is.
Answer Key

Look for: identification of free-speech concerns (s. 2(b) Charter), the natural-law concern that broad criminalisation of speech approaches the rule against rule by law, the positivist position that validly enacted statutes are law (with Charter review as a separate question), the realist/feminist concern about how the statute would actually be enforced (and who would be targeted), the CLS concern about whose voices the statute would silence, and a reasoned personal assessment.
