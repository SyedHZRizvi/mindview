Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Identify and briefly describe the three documents that make up the International Bill of Human Rights. When was each adopted?
Answer Key

(a) UDHR (1948) — Universal Declaration of Human Rights; GA resolution; aspirational. (b) ICCPR (1966) — International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights; binding treaty; in force 1976. (c) ICESCR (1966) — International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights; binding; in force 1976. Plus optional protocols. Canada is party to all three.

2
[2]
The Genocide Convention (1948) defines genocide to require intent to destroy a protected group "as such." Which groups are protected?
Answer Key

A. Article II of the Genocide Convention lists national, ethnical, racial, and religious groups. Political and gender groups were debated in 1948 but excluded; they remain outside the legal definition (though crimes against humanity can cover such groups).

3
[3]
List the five enumerated grounds in the Refugee Convention's definition of "refugee" (Article 1A(2)), and explain why "economic migrant" generally does not qualify.
Answer Key

The five grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, political opinion. Economic migrants flee hardship not tied to one of these Convention grounds; they may be in deserving circumstances but do not meet the legal definition. The Convention's purpose was to protect those persecuted on specifically political/identity grounds.

4
[2]
The principle of non-refoulement under CAT (Convention against Torture) Article 3 differs from refugee non-refoulement under the 1951 Convention because:
Answer Key

A. CAT Article 3 is absolute. Refugee Convention Article 33(2) has narrow exceptions for serious-crime convictions or national-security threats. Suresh v Canada [2002] held that Canadian deportation to face torture violates Charter s. 7 except in extraordinary circumstances — bringing the constitutional standard close to (though not identical with) CAT.

5
[3]
Describe the three pillars of the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine as articulated in the 2005 World Summit Outcome Document.
Answer Key

Pillar 1: Each state has the responsibility to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity. Pillar 2: The international community has the responsibility to assist states in meeting Pillar 1. Pillar 3: When a state manifestly fails, the international community has the responsibility to take collective action through the Security Council, including force in last resort.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[5]
A government in State Q has carried out a campaign that destroyed 60% of an ethnic minority's villages, closed minority-language schools, killed hundreds of community leaders, and forced 200,000 members into refugee camps in neighbouring states.
Analyse whether this constitutes (a) genocide under the 1948 Convention; (b) crimes against humanity under the Rome Statute; (c) ethnic cleansing. Address mens rea, actus reus, and the legal threshold for each.
Answer Key

(a) Genocide: Actus reus appears to include killing, causing serious bodily/mental harm, and inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about destruction (Article II(a)–(c)). The hard question is mens rea — specific intent to destroy the group "as such." Evidence from speeches, planning documents, and patterns is needed. Without dolus specialis, no genocide. (b) Crimes against humanity: Widespread or systematic attack against a civilian population — the actus reus is satisfied (mass attack). Mens rea is knowledge of the attack. Likely satisfied. (c) Ethnic cleansing: Not a separate legal category — describes the conduct (forced displacement) but is prosecuted as genocide or crimes against humanity. Marks: 2 each crime + 1 distinction.

7
[4]
Claimant Aman, a 28-year-old Afghan journalist who reported on Taliban abuses before the 2021 takeover, files a refugee claim in Canada. He has documented threats from named Taliban commanders.
Apply the Refugee Convention Article 1A(2) test step-by-step to Aman's case. Conclude whether he meets the refugee definition.
Answer Key

(1) Well-founded fear: Subjective fear plus objective evidence (country-condition documentation). (2) Persecution: Detention, torture, killing for journalism — serious harm satisfies the standard. (3) Convention ground: Political opinion (his journalism); possibly particular social group (journalists). (4) Outside country of nationality: Aman is in Canada — satisfied. (5) Unable or unwilling to avail of state protection: The Taliban government is the perpetrator; state protection is unavailable. Conclusion: Aman meets the definition. Non-refoulement under Article 33(1) and CAT Article 3 protects against return.

8
[4]
Critics of R2P argue that it has been applied selectively and has been used by powerful states to justify intervention that would not otherwise be permitted. Defenders argue that the doctrine is a needed framework for preventing atrocity crimes. Analyse this debate in 5–7 sentences, with reference to Libya (2011) and Syria (2011–).
Answer Key

R2P was operationalised in SC Resolution 1973 (Libya, 2011), which authorised "all necessary measures" to protect civilians; NATO-led action followed. Critics argued the intervention exceeded the civilian-protection mandate and amounted to regime change; this charge has made subsequent Security Council action harder. On Syria (2011–), the SC has been blocked by Russian and Chinese vetoes, leading to no R2P-grounded action despite catastrophic civilian harm. Selectivity is the structural feature, not an accident: SC authorisation depends on P5 consensus. Defenders argue R2P provides the political and legal framework that, when consensus exists, enables decisive action; the failures are veto failures, not doctrinal failures. Marks: 2 cases, 2 analysis, 1 conclusion.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write a structured 6–8 sentence paragraph explaining the difference between civil and political rights (ICCPR) and economic, social, and cultural rights (ICESCR). Address the distinction between "immediate effect" and "progressive realisation" and give one example of each kind of right.
Answer Key

Look for: ICCPR contains civil/political rights with immediate-effect obligations (free expression, due process, vote); ICESCR contains ESC rights with progressive realisation to maximum of available resources (work, health, education); the CESCR has clarified that ICESCR contains immediate-effect components (non-discrimination, minimum core); the historical reason for the split (Cold War); examples from each (ICCPR: freedom of assembly; ICESCR: right to housing). Marks: 1 each — definitions, distinctions, examples, immediate-effect/progressive, conventions.

10
[4]
Rewrite the following weak statement into a strong thesis: "UNDRIP is important for Indigenous rights."
Answer Key

Sample: "Canada's enactment of the UNDRIP Act in 2021 transformed UNDRIP from an aspirational declaration into a binding standard against which federal law must increasingly be measured, particularly through the requirement of free, prior, and informed consent for measures affecting Indigenous peoples." Look for: debatable claim, specific dates and instruments, named legal concept (FPIC).

11
[3]
In 3–4 sentences, compare the European, Inter-American, and African regional human-rights systems. Name one distinctive feature of each.
Answer Key

European: ECtHR in Strasbourg; oldest, most developed; binding individual-complaint mechanism; margin-of-appreciation doctrine. Inter-American: Court in San José + Commission in Washington; significant enforced-disappearance and Indigenous jurisprudence. African: Court in Arusha; African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights uniquely protects both individual and peoples' rights and includes duties.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
In Baker v Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) [1999] 2 SCR 817, an undocumented mother with Canadian-born children faced deportation. The Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), though ratified, was not implemented in Canadian legislation. The Supreme Court held that the CRC's "best interests of the child" standard nonetheless informed the reasonableness review of the deportation decision.
Explain how Baker v Canada illustrates the relationship between unimplemented treaties and Canadian domestic law. Reference Canada's dualist approach, the presumption of conformity, and the role of administrative law. 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Canada is dualist for treaties: unratified or unimplemented treaties have no direct domestic effect. Baker nonetheless held that the CRC informed the standard of review of the discretionary deportation decision. The presumption of conformity (R v Hape [2007]) is that Canadian statutes will be interpreted, where possible, consistently with international obligations. Baker applied this presumption in administrative law: the "best interests of the child" became part of what the decision-maker should have considered. Unimplemented treaties thus have interpretive force; they do not create directly enforceable rights but can shape statutory and administrative-law reasoning. Marks: 1 dualism, 2 presumption + Baker, 1 admin-law, 1 conclusion.

13
[4]
Apply the three pillars of R2P to the situation in Myanmar (Rohingya, 2017–). Has Pillar 1 been met? Pillar 2? Pillar 3? Why has Chapter VII action not followed despite the gravity of the violations?
Answer Key

Pillar 1: Myanmar's government failed to protect (and is the perpetrator of) the Rohingya. Pillar 2: International assistance has been offered (UN missions, ASEAN intervention) but rejected; sanctions imposed. Pillar 3: SC action under Chapter VII has been blocked by China's veto. The Gambia v Myanmar at the ICJ has provided an alternative legal forum (genocide proceedings). Provisional measures were granted in 2020. The case illustrates R2P's structural dependence on SC unanimity. Marks: 1 each pillar + 1 explanation of veto/alternative.

STSE / Real-World Application: The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 50 calls for the establishment of Indigenous law institutes. Choose one current Canadian legal issue involving Indigenous peoples (treaty rights, child welfare, resource development, FPIC). In 4–5 sentences, explain how concepts from Chapter 4 (UNDRIP, human-rights treaties) help analyse it.
Answer Key

Strong responses identify how the chapter transfers: UNDRIP Articles 18–19 (consultation, FPIC), CRC for child-welfare issues, CERD for racial discrimination, ICCPR Article 27 (minority rights), and the UNDRIP Act 2021 for domestic application. Marks: 1 issue, 2 specific concepts, 1 application.
