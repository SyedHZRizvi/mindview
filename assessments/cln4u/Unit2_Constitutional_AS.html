Topic 2.1 — Constitution Act, 1867 & Federalism
Question 1
Sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867 do which of the following?
Solution: Section 91 enumerates federal powers (criminal law, banking, trade and commerce, POGG, etc.); section 92 enumerates provincial powers (property and civil rights, administration of justice, local matters). This is the architectural heart of Canadian federalism.
Question 2
The doctrine of "pith and substance" asks:
Solution: The pith-and-substance doctrine characterises a law by its dominant purpose and effect, then matches it to a head of power under ss. 91 or 92. The test is purposive and effects-based, not formal.
Question 3
The doctrine of paramountcy in federalism means:
Solution: Paramountcy resolves operational conflict between valid federal and valid provincial laws: federal law prevails, and the provincial law is inoperative to the extent of the conflict. The provincial law remains valid; it is just inoperative on that conflict.
Topic 2.2 — 1982 Patriation
Question 4
The Constitution Act, 1982 was proclaimed on:
Solution: The Constitution Act, 1982, including the Charter, was proclaimed by Queen Elizabeth II on 17 April 1982 on Parliament Hill in Ottawa. From that date Canada gained full authority to amend its own Constitution.
Question 5
The general amending formula (section 38) is often called the "7/50" formula. It requires:
Solution: Section 38 requires resolutions of the Senate, the House of Commons, and legislative assemblies of at least two-thirds of the provinces (7) containing at least 50% of the population. Section 41 (unanimity) applies to a smaller set of items.
Topic 2.3 & 2.4 — Charter and Oakes Test
Question 6
Which section of the Charter protects "life, liberty and security of the person" and the principles of fundamental justice?
Solution: Section 7 protects life, liberty, and security of the person, deprivable only in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice. It is the most-litigated Charter provision in criminal law (Bedford, Carter, BC Motor Vehicle Reference).
Question 7
The Oakes test was established in R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103. It analyses:
Solution: The Oakes test structures the section 1 analysis: (1) prescribed by law; (2) pressing and substantial objective; (3) rational connection; (4) minimal impairment; (5) proportionality of effects. The government bears the burden.
Question 8
The four branches of the proportionality stage of the Oakes test are:
Solution: After "prescribed by law," the Oakes test has four sub-elements: pressing and substantial objective, rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionality of effects. Minimal impairment is most often decisive.
Topic 2.5 & 2.6 — Section 33 & Section 35
Question 9
Section 33 of the Charter, the notwithstanding clause, can be used to override:
Solution: Section 33 can override only sections 2 and 7–15 (fundamental freedoms; legal rights; equality). Democratic rights (3–6), mobility (6), language rights (16–23), section 28 (sex equality), and section 35 (Indigenous rights, which is outside the Charter) cannot be overridden.
Question 10
The sunset period for a section 33 declaration is:
Solution: Section 33(3) provides a five-year automatic sunset. A legislature can re-enact the override after five years, but it must explicitly do so. This periodicity is the structural check on the use of s. 33.
Question 11
In R v Sparrow [1990], the Supreme Court set out a three-part s. 35 framework. The third step is:
Solution: The Sparrow framework is: (1) existing Aboriginal right; (2) prima facie infringement; (3) justification — valid objective AND conduct consistent with fiduciary duty (priority, minimal impairment, consultation, compensation).
Question 12
The Supreme Court first recognised Aboriginal title to a specific tract of land in:
Solution: Delgamuukw [1997] set out the doctrine; Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia [2014] 2 SCR 257 was the first declaration of Aboriginal title to a specific area of land on the ground (over 1,750 km² of Tsilhqot'in territory in central BC).
