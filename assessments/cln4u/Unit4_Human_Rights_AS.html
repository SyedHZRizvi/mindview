Topic 4.1 — UDHR
Question 1
The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted on:
Solution: The UDHR was adopted by GA Resolution 217 A (III) on 10 December 1948. The date is annually observed as Human Rights Day. The Canadian John Peters Humphrey wrote the first draft; Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting committee.
Question 2
The UDHR is, in formal terms:
Solution: The UDHR was a GA resolution and therefore not, on adoption, legally binding. Over time, many provisions (prohibitions on torture, slavery, racial discrimination) have crystallised into customary law and, in some cases, jus cogens.
Topic 4.2 — International Bill of Rights
Question 3
The two binding 1966 covenants that complete the International Bill of Human Rights are:
Solution: The International Bill of Human Rights = UDHR (1948) + ICCPR (1966, civil and political rights) + ICESCR (1966, economic, social, and cultural rights) + optional protocols. Both 1966 covenants entered into force in 1976 and were ratified by Canada in 1976.
Question 4
Under the ICESCR, the obligation to realise economic, social, and cultural rights is generally described as:
Solution: Article 2(1) ICESCR requires states "to take steps … to the maximum of available resources, with a view to achieving progressively the full realization of the rights." Note: progressive does NOT mean unenforceable — states have immediate-effect obligations including non-discrimination and minimum core obligations.
Topic 4.3 — Specialised Treaties
Question 5
The legal definition of genocide in Article II of the 1948 Convention requires:
Solution: The Genocide Convention's mens rea requires dolus specialis — specific intent to destroy the protected group as such. The protected groups are limited to national, ethnical, racial, and religious; political groups were excluded in 1948.
Question 6
The Convention against Torture (CAT, 1984) prohibits return of a person to a state where they would face:
Solution: CAT Article 3 prohibits return ("refoulement") to a state where the person would be in danger of being subjected to torture. The protection is absolute — even refugees with serious criminal convictions are protected. Different from refugee non-refoulement (Article 33(2)) which has narrow exceptions.
Topic 4.4 — Refugee Law
Question 7
The 1951 Refugee Convention defines a refugee as someone with a "well-founded fear of being persecuted" for reasons of:
Solution: Article 1A(2) of the 1951 Convention lists five enumerated grounds. The persecution must be linked to one of them; economic hardship alone or undifferentiated war-driven displacement does not generally satisfy the definition (though civil war combined with persecution on a Convention ground can).
Question 8
The principle of non-refoulement (Article 33 Refugee Convention) prohibits states from:
Solution: Non-refoulement (Article 33(1)) is the cornerstone obligation. It applies at the border, in territorial waters, and arguably extraterritorially. Article 33(2) has narrow exceptions for serious-crime convictions or national-security threats; the CAT non-refoulement obligation has no such exceptions.
Topic 4.5 & 4.6 — Regional Systems, UNDRIP, R2P
Question 9
The European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) is seated in:
Solution: The ECtHR is in Strasbourg, France. It administers the European Convention on Human Rights for all 46 members of the Council of Europe (Russia was expelled in 2022).
Question 10
UNDRIP, the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, was adopted by the General Assembly in:
Solution: UNDRIP was adopted on 13 September 2007 as GA Resolution 61/295. Canada initially opposed, then endorsed in 2010 with caveats, then enacted the UNDRIP Act (2021) committing federal law to consistency with UNDRIP. The federal UNDRIP Act came into force in 2021, not the Declaration itself.
Question 11
The Responsibility to Protect (R2P), as articulated in the 2005 World Summit Outcome Document, covers responses to:
Solution: R2P (paragraphs 138–139 of GA Resolution 60/1, 2005) is limited to the four atrocity crimes. Each state has the primary responsibility; the international community has the responsibility to assist and, in cases of manifest failure, the responsibility to take collective action through the Security Council.
