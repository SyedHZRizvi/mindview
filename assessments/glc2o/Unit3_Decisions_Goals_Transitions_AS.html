Topic 3.1 — Decision-Making
Question 1
A "type-1" decision (Bezos's framing) is best described as:
Solution: Type-1 = roughly irreversible (major surgery, leaving a long career, signing a 5-year program with loans). These deserve slow careful evaluation. Type-2 = easily reversible (joining a club, summer job, elective). Treating every choice as type-1 produces paralysis.
Question 2
A student insists: "I've already spent two years studying biology, so I have to keep going even though I don't enjoy it."
This reasoning is an example of:
Solution: Sunk-cost reasoning means past costs that cannot be recovered shouldn't dictate future choices. The two years are spent either way; the question is what serves the student now. Career Studies asks students to name these biases out loud while making decisions.
Topic 3.2 — SMART Goals
Question 3
In SMART goals, the letter A typically stands for:
Solution: SMART = Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. A too-easy goal is wasted; a too-hard goal is demoralising. Achievable means real-but-stretching.
Question 4
Which is the strongest SMART goal?
Solution: Option C is Specific (MPM2D, percentage), Measurable (67→75), Achievable (a realistic improvement), Relevant (likely supporting a postsecondary goal), and Time-bound (end of second term). The action plan is built in.
Topic 3.3 — Résumés & Cover Letters
Question 5
The standard Canadian résumé format is:
Solution: Reverse-chronological is the Canadian standard. Functional/skills-based résumés are used in some career-change cases, but recruiters expect reverse-chronological by default. Photos are not used in Canada (legal risk for employer).
Question 6
A student writes: "Worked at McDonald's."
The strongest rewrite of this résumé bullet is:
Solution: Option B includes job title, location, dates, scale (80+ customers), specific tasks (cash, drive-thru), responsibility growth (trained new hires), and external recognition. Every résumé bullet should answer: what did I do, how much/often, and what was the result?
Topic 3.4 — Interview Skills
Question 7
The STAR framework for answering behavioural interview questions stands for:
Solution: STAR — Situation (context), Task (what you needed to do), Action (what you did), Result (what happened). It is the structure of a strong behavioural answer to "tell me about a time you…"
Topic 3.5 — Financial Literacy
Question 8
The 50/30/20 budgeting rule recommends:
Solution: 50/30/20 is a guideline for after-tax adult income: 50% to essentials, 30% to discretionary, 20% to savings/debt. The numbers shift by life stage but the principle of "allocate before spending" is what makes budgets work.
Question 9
If you carry a $2,000 balance on a credit card at 22% annual interest and pay only the minimum each month, paying it off:
Solution: At 22% APR with minimum payments (typically ~2% of balance), interest compounds faster than payments reduce principal. The total paid can easily exceed double the original balance. The single most useful credit habit is: pay the statement balance in full each month so interest never accrues.
Topic 3.6–3.7 — Transitions & IPP
Question 10
In William Bridges's three-phase transition model, the messy middle period is called the:
Solution: Bridges's phases: Ending → Neutral Zone → New Beginning. Most distress in transitions sits in the neutral zone because nothing feels stable; naming the phase keeps it tolerable while the new arrangement forms.
Question 11
The Ontario Individual Pathways Plan (IPP) is structured around four questions. The first one is:
Solution: The four IPP questions (Creating Pathways to Success): Who am I? What are my opportunities? Who do I want to become? What is my plan for achieving my goals? Self-knowledge is the foundation; the others build on it.
Question 12
The Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is best described as:
Solution: A TFSA shelters investment gains from tax — contributions are made with after-tax money, but growth and withdrawals are tax-free. Once you are 18 you can contribute up to an annual limit ($7,000 in recent years). It is especially powerful for young adults because compound growth has decades to work.
