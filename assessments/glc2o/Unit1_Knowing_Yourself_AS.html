Topic 1.1–1.2 — Self-Assessment & RIASEC
Question 1
A "personal profile" in Career Studies is best described as:
Solution: The Ministry frames the personal profile as a synthesis of multiple self-assessments — interests, skills, values, learning style, personality — that you use as a working hypothesis. It is a starting point for planning, not a fixed diagnosis or label.
Question 2
In Holland's RIASEC framework, the letter I stands for:
Solution: RIASEC = Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional. Investigative types prefer analytical and research-oriented work — biology, software development, statistics, medical research.
Question 3
Camila's RIASEC results return a top three code of SAI (Social, Artistic, Investigative). She is curious about which careers her code might point toward.
A reasonable career hypothesis cluster for a Holland code of SAI is:
Solution: SAI combines people-helping (S), creative self-expression (A), and analytical curiosity (I). Drama teacher, school counsellor with creative arts, or health-education researcher fit. Option (a) is RRR; (b) is EEE; (d) is CCC.
Topic 1.3 — Skills & Multiple Intelligences
Question 4
Which is the strongest example of a "skill statement with evidence"?
Solution: Option C names the skill (using OSP language), gives a specific example with audience and duration, and corroborates with external feedback. The others are unsupported claims that employers discount.
Question 5
The Ontario Skills Passport (OSP) categorises employability into:
Solution: The Ontario Skills Passport uses 9 essential skills and 3 work habits (working safely, working independently, using initiative). It is the Ministry's common language for talking about employability across school and work.
Topic 1.4 — Work Values
Question 6
The three families of work values commonly used in career counselling are:
Solution: Intrinsic values are about the work itself (challenge, meaning, helping); extrinsic values are about the rewards (salary, status, security); lifestyle values are about fit with the rest of life (hours, location, flexibility). All three matter; the mix varies by person.
Question 7
Tomás values "balance" (time with family), "helping others," and "integrity" in his top three. He is offered a job at a high-paying investment bank that requires 70-hour weeks and approval of practices he finds questionable.
A career counsellor would describe this situation as:
Solution: Value conflict is the technical term for a job whose daily demands violate a person's deeper values. Research finds that value conflict reliably predicts burnout and turnover, regardless of pay. Tomás's three values are all violated; he should weigh the offer very carefully.
Topic 1.5–1.6 — Personality & Growth Mindset
Question 8
In the Big Five personality model (OCEAN), the trait most strongly linked to job performance across nearly every field is:
Solution: Decades of meta-analyses find Conscientiousness predicts work performance across roles better than any other Big Five dimension. Students who score high on it tend to be reliable, well-organised, and persistent — exactly the qualities employers value.
Question 9
Carol Dweck's "growth mindset" is best described as:
Solution: Growth mindset = ability is developed (not fixed). It contrasts with fixed mindset (ability is innate). Growth mindset is not toxic positivity; it is a method — when you fail, you collect specific information about what you don't yet know and try a new strategy. The word "yet" is the key shift.
Question 10
Sara fails her first Grade 10 math test. She thinks: "I haven't mastered factoring yet. Let me figure out which questions I missed, and ask my teacher what study strategy would work better."
Sara's response illustrates:
Solution: Sara uses the word "yet," analyses what she missed (diagnostic), and seeks a new strategy. That is textbook growth-mindset action — turning a setback into a plan, rather than a verdict on identity.
