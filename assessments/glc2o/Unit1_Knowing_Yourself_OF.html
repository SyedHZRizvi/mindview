Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define the six Holland RIASEC interest types and give one occupation example for each.
Answer Key

Realistic — hands-on, practical, outdoor (electrician, paramedic, farmer). Investigative — analytical, problem-solving, research (biologist, software developer, statistician). Artistic — creative self-expression, design, performance (graphic designer, musician, architect). Social — helping, teaching, counselling (nurse, teacher, social worker). Enterprising — leading, persuading, selling (sales manager, entrepreneur, lawyer). Conventional — structured, data, organisation (accountant, administrator, logistics analyst). 0.5 marks per definition + 0.5 per valid example × 6.

2
[2]
In the Ontario Skills Passport, name three of the nine "essential skills" and one of the three "work habits."
Answer Key

Any three essential skills: reading, writing, numeracy, oral communication, digital, problem solving, working with others, finding and using information, continuous learning. Any one work habit: working safely, working independently, using initiative. 0.5 marks each.

3
[2]
The Big Five (OCEAN) trait most strongly linked to job performance across nearly every field is:
Answer Key

Conscientiousness. Decades of meta-analyses show this is the single Big Five trait most reliably tied to job performance — including in school, in trades, and in knowledge work.

4
[3]
Distinguish between interests, skills, and values. Give one sentence for each.
Answer Key

Interests = what you enjoy doing or are curious about (e.g., reading mystery novels, fixing engines). Skills = what you can demonstrably do, built through practice (e.g., welding, public speaking). Values = the qualities you treat as important — what matters most about how you live and work (e.g., helping others, autonomy, security). All three are necessary inputs to a good career plan; none is sufficient alone. 1 mark each.

5
[3]
Define growth mindset (Dweck) and grit (Duckworth). How do the two concepts relate?
Answer Key

Growth mindset = the belief that ability is developed through effort, strategy, and feedback (contrasted with a fixed mindset, where ability is innate). Grit = sustained passion and perseverance for long-term goals. Relationship: growth mindset is a precondition for grit — you only stay engaged with a long-term goal if you believe effort will pay off. Grit extends growth mindset across years. Both are learnable. 1 mark each definition + 1 for the relationship.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[4]
A friend says: "I took one online quiz that said I should be a graphic designer. So now I know what I'm going to do for the rest of my life."
Critique your friend's reasoning. Identify two flaws in how they are using self-assessment, and recommend a better approach.
Answer Key

Flaws: (1) Single source — one quiz is not enough; the Ministry expects triangulation across multiple assessments, feedback from others, and reflection. (2) Treating the result as a verdict rather than a hypothesis — Holland code or quiz results should be tested in real settings (job-shadow, volunteer, club), not treated as fixed identity. (3) Skipping the interaction with values, skills, opportunities, and labour-market evidence. Better approach: complete several assessments, get external feedback, do informational interviews, and test the hypothesis in low-stakes ways before committing. 1 mark per flaw, 2 marks for a substantive recommendation.

7
[5]
Priya's RIASEC code is SAI (Social, Artistic, Investigative). Her top three values are helping others, learning, and balance (family time). She is strong in oral communication and weaker in numeracy under time pressure.
Identify two careers that fit Priya's profile and one career that would not fit. For each, briefly justify by referring to her code, values, and skills.
Answer Key

Two good fits (examples): Speech-language pathologist — S (helping people communicate), A (designing creative therapy activities), I (assessing patients analytically), aligns with helping/learning, predictable hours fit balance. School counsellor / teacher — S, A, helping, learning, balance (school calendar). Health-education researcher — S, I, A, helping/learning. Poor fit (example): Day trader / investment banker — high E and C, frequent time-pressure numeracy, long hours that violate balance value. Marks: 1 per career fit (× 2) + 1 for justification each + 1 for poor-fit reasoning.

8
[4]
A student says: "I'm just not a math person." Analyse this statement through the lens of fixed vs. growth mindset. What is the underlying belief, what are the likely consequences, and how would you redirect the thought?
Answer Key

The statement reflects a fixed mindset — math ability framed as an innate identity ("a math person") rather than a skill that develops. Likely consequences: lower effort, withdrawal at difficulty, mark slides further, the verdict feels confirmed. Redirect using the word "yet" and a strategy question: "I haven't mastered this yet. What specifically did I get wrong on the last test? Was it the concept or the conditions? What study strategy will I try differently?" Marks: 1 identify mindset, 1 underlying belief, 1 consequences, 1 redirect.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]
Write a one-paragraph (5–7 sentences) "personal profile" about yourself, suitable for an Individual Pathways Plan. Include at least one interest, one skill (with brief evidence), one value, and one learning preference. Use clear, specific language — no slogans.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) at least one interest named specifically; (2) at least one skill with brief evidence (not just a claim); (3) at least one value named; (4) a learning preference; (5) cohesive paragraph form; (6) absence of vague slogans ("I'm a people person"). Marks: 1 per element + 1 for clear conventions/no slogans.

10
[3]
Rewrite the following weak skills claim into a strong, evidence-backed statement suitable for a résumé or IPP: "I'm a great team player."
Answer Key

Sample strong rewrite: "Working with others: In a Grade 10 history group of four, I coordinated weekly meetings, redistributed tasks when one member fell behind due to a family commitment, and helped the group deliver a presentation that scored 88% — the teacher's feedback highlighted our slide design as strongest in the class." Marks: 1 named skill (OSP-style), 1 specific evidence (numbers/dates/setting), 1 corroboration or result.

11
[4]
In 3–4 sentences, explain to a Grade 9 student why values matter for career planning. Use one concrete example.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a clear definition of values in plain language; (2) the idea of value conflict — that a job clashing with deep values produces dissatisfaction over time even if pay or interest seem fine; (3) a concrete example (e.g., someone who values balance ending up in a 70-hour job, or someone who values helping ending up in a strictly profit-driven role); (4) clear communication for a Grade 9 audience. Marks: 1 each element.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Apply the self-assessment process to your own situation. Identify your top three career hypotheses (occupations you would explore further). For each, name one element of your personal profile that supports the hypothesis (an interest, a value, a skill, or a Holland code). You don't need to commit — these are working hypotheses to test in Unit 2.
Answer Key

Look for: 3 named occupations + 1 grounded justification each (interest, value, skill, or Holland-code link). Honesty is more important than ambition — a student who lists a job that genuinely matches their profile scores higher than one who lists a "prestigious" job with no connection to themselves. Marks: 1 per hypothesis (× 3) + 2 marks for thoughtful justifications.

13
[4]
Apply growth mindset to a real Grade-10 setback. Describe one academic or social setback you experienced (or might experience), and write the inner monologue you would use to respond with a growth mindset rather than a fixed one.
Answer Key

Look for: (1) a specific, plausible setback (low test mark, conflict with a friend, missed selection for a team); (2) inner monologue that names what went wrong specifically; (3) use of the word "yet" or equivalent; (4) a concrete next step (talking to the teacher, changing study strategy, asking for feedback). Marks: 1 setback, 2 monologue elements, 1 next step.

14
[3]
STSE / Real-World Application: Why might an employer care more about your work habits (responsibility, organisation, initiative) than about which subjects you got the highest marks in? Give 2–3 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong response: most jobs can train technical skills, but work habits are harder to teach. An employee who arrives on time, follows through, and takes initiative on small problems creates value reliably; an employee who got high marks but is unreliable creates costs. Ontario's Learning Skills report exists exactly because employers and postsecondary educators consistently rate work habits as predictive of success. 1 mark per insight, max 3.

📝 Communication Category Rubric (instructor-graded)

Per Ontario's Growing Success (2010) Achievement Chart, Communication is one of the four equally-weighted categories (25% of the course mark). This rubric is applied to every student-written response (short-answer, extended-response, essay, lab report, deputation, etc.) on this assessment.

Expression and organisation of ideas Ideas expressed with limited clarity; little organisation. Ideas expressed with some clarity; some organisation. Ideas expressed clearly and organised effectively. Ideas expressed with clarity, precision, and a high degree of organisation.
Communication for different audiences and purposes Limited awareness of audience/purpose; tone often inappropriate. Some awareness of audience/purpose; tone occasionally inappropriate. Consistently appropriate tone, register, and address to audience. Tone, register, voice, and address are precisely matched to audience and purpose.

Virtually error-free; precise and varied use of discipline-specific vocabulary.

Level 3 represents the Provincial Standard. Use the most-recent and most-consistent evidence across multiple Communication tasks (this assessment, prior FOR work, written responses on AS quizzes, oral presentations) when assigning a course-level Communication mark. Growing Success, 2010, p. 17.
