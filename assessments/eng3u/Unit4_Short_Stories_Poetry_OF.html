Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Define and distinguish first-person, third-person limited, and third-person omniscient narration.
Answer Key

First-person ("I") — narrator is a character inside the story. Third-person limited — narrator is outside the action grammatically (uses he/she/they) but is psychologically tethered to one character's interior. Third-person omniscient — narrator freely enters every character's interior. Also acceptable: second-person ("you"), third-person objective (no interiority at all).

2
[2]
Identify the rhyme scheme of a Shakespearean sonnet.
Answer Key

A. Three quatrains in alternating rhyme + closing couplet. The couplet often delivers a turn or resolution (the volta in a Shakespearean sonnet usually arrives at line 13).

3
[3]
Define each: (a) enjambment, (b) caesura, (c) volta. Give an example sentence or position for any one.
Answer Key

(a) Enjambment — running a sentence past a line break without punctuation. (b) Caesura — a strong pause within a line, often marked by punctuation. (c) Volta — the turn in argument in a sonnet; typically at line 9 in a Petrarchan sonnet, line 13 in a Shakespearean sonnet.

4
[3]
Define theme and distinguish it from topic. Give one topic and convert it into a theme statement.
Answer Key

A topic is what the text is about (love, war, family) — a noun. A theme is a claim about a topic the text is making — a sentence. Example: topic = "memory"; theme = "In this story, memory operates as a form of revision rather than recall: the older self can change what the younger self saw simply by retelling it."

5
[2]
Identify the metrical foot demonstrated by "of MICE / and MEN":
Answer Key

B. Iamb = unstressed / stressed (da-DUM). "Of MICE" and "and MEN" are both iambs. Trochee is DUM-da; dactyl is DUM-da-da; anapest is da-da-DUM.

6
[2]
What is the difference between an unreliable narrator and an omniscient narrator?
Answer Key

An unreliable narrator is one whose reports the reader cannot fully trust — the narrator's perceptions, memories, or judgements may be distorted. An omniscient narrator knows every character's interior and reports with authority. The two are not opposites — omniscient narrators can be reliable; first-person narrators can be either; the categories are distinct.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
7
[5]
"My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun; / Coral is far more red than her lips' red; / If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun; / If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head." — Shakespeare, Sonnet 130
Analyse how Shakespeare uses negation and conventional Petrarchan imagery in this opening quatrain to subvert the love-poem tradition.
Answer Key

Shakespeare lists conventional Petrarchan tropes (eyes like the sun, coral lips, snow-white skin, golden hair) and negates each one ("nothing like," "far more red than," "dun," "black wires"). The negation strategy mocks the formulaic blazon while paradoxically still describing the mistress in detail. The subverted tradition prepares the reader for the volta in the closing couplet, where the speaker insists his love is "as rare / As any she belied with false compare" — realer for being un-idealised. Marks: 1 identifying Petrarchan tropes, 2 the negation mechanism, 1 specific quotations, 1 connecting to overall thematic effect.

8
[5]
"The fog comes / on little cat feet. // It sits looking / over harbor and city / on silent haunches / and then moves on." — Carl Sandburg, "Fog"
Identify three literary devices in this short poem and explain how each contributes to the poem's overall effect (stillness, transience, observation).
Answer Key

Possible devices: (1) extended metaphor (fog as cat, sustained for the whole poem) — produces a sentient, prowling presence; (2) personification ("sits looking," "moves on") — gives the fog contemplative attention that mirrors the speaker's; (3) free verse / short lines / white space — the lack of regular meter enacts the silent transience of fog. Also acceptable: alliteration ("silent" / "sits"), imagery. Marks: 3 device identifications, 2 connecting each to the poem's effect.

9
[5]
"It is so pleasant to have such a great room all to myself, and so airy and full of sunshine. … And yet I cannot be with him, it makes me so nervous." — Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wallpaper"
In one paragraph (4–6 sentences), analyse how the contradiction between the narrator's two statements signals an unreliable narrator. Use a PEEL structure.
Answer Key

Sample: "Gilman's narrator betrays her unreliability in a single sentence pair. (P) The narrator's attempt to convince herself the room is 'pleasant' is undercut by her admission of nervousness, signalling that she cannot be trusted to interpret her own situation. (E) She begins '[i]t is so pleasant to have such a great room all to myself' — three superlatives in twelve words — then almost immediately confesses 'I cannot be with him, it makes me so nervous'. (Explain) The contradiction reveals an unreliable narrator attempting to manage her own perception of her confinement; the surface optimism is the very evidence of her unease. (Link) The rest of the story develops this gap between what the narrator says and what her syntax reveals." Marks: 1 P, 2 E, 1 Explain, 1 Link + conventions.

Part C: Communication [15 marks]
10
[5]
Write one analytical PEEL paragraph (5–7 sentences) on the following claim: "In Frost's 'The Road Not Taken,' the speaker's tone is more wistful than triumphant." Embed at least one direct quotation with proper line citation.
Answer Key

Look for: (P) clear topic sentence asserting wistfulness over triumph; (E) one embedded quotation with signal phrase and line citation; (Explain) interpretation of how diction ("sorry," "sigh") and future-tense framing ("I shall be telling this with a sigh") signal regret rather than victory; (Link) closing sentence linking back to thesis. Marks: 1 P, 1 E with citation, 2 Explain, 1 Link + conventions.

11
[5]
Rewrite this weak topic sentence as a strong analytical claim about any poem you know: "The poem talks about death."
Answer Key

Sample: "Emily Dickinson's 'Because I could not stop for Death' transforms the conventional image of death-as-villain into death-as-civil-gentleman, using the politeness of the personification to expose how thoroughly nineteenth-century manners had domesticated even mortality." Look for: a debatable claim (1), specific reference to text/technique (1), an interpretive verb ("transforms…to expose") (1), specific evidence implied (1), conventions (1).

12
[5]
Compare a metaphor and a symbol in one short paragraph (4 sentences). Define each, explain how they relate, and use one literary example for each.
Answer Key

Both connect a concrete image to an abstract idea. A metaphor is local — a single comparison (e.g., "Juliet is the sun"). A symbol is sustained — the same concrete image recurs and accumulates meaning across a whole work (the dagger in Macbeth, the road in Frost). All sustained metaphors can become symbols; not every metaphor does. Marks: 1 each definition, 1 the relationship, 1 examples and conventions.

Part D: Application [15 marks]
13
[8]
Passage A: "Two roads diverged in a yellow wood, / And sorry I could not travel both / And be one traveller, long I stood / And looked down one as far as I could / To where it bent in the undergrowth." — Robert Frost, "The Road Not Taken"
Passage B: "The fog comes / on little cat feet. // It sits looking / over harbor and city / on silent haunches / and then moves on." — Carl Sandburg, "Fog"
Apply comparative-paragraph technique. Compare and contrast these two short poems in one paragraph (8–10 sentences). Address: (i) the dominant figure of speech in each, (ii) the speaker's stance toward the natural world, (iii) the function of form (rhyme/meter or free verse). Conclude with a synthesis sentence that names something neither poem could say alone.
Answer Key

Sample synthesis sentence: "Frost's metered, rhymed quatrain stages choice as deliberation across time, while Sandburg's free-verse miniature stages the natural world as something that pauses, watches, and disappears without choosing — what the comparison reveals is that form decides what a poem can even ask about, and the two poets have made different decisions about whether poetry has time to deliberate." Marks: 2 figure of speech (extended metaphor in B vs. extended metaphor in A's roads), 2 speaker's stance, 2 form function, 2 synthesis (a sentence neither poem could produce alone).

14
[7]
Real-World Application: Choose one short song lyric you know well (one or two lines). Analyse it as you would a poem: identify one literary device, explain its effect, and argue what theme the lyric (or the song as a whole) develops. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong responses treat the lyric as a poem: identify a specific device (metaphor, simile, repetition, irony, hyperbole), quote the lyric exactly (with the artist named), explain why the device produces its effect, and end with a defensible thematic claim. Marks: 1 lyric quoted accurately, 2 device identified and explained, 3 theme argued from evidence, 1 communication.
