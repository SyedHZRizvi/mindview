Topic 2.1 — Writing Process & Thesis
Question 1
Which of the following is a thesis, not a topic?
Solution: A thesis makes a debatable, specific claim about the text. Choice (c) names a mechanism (ambition as contagious), specifies what evidence will appear (the alternation between the Macbeths), and gives the intellectual stakes. (a) is a topic, (b) is an obvious truth, (d) is biography.

Question 2
The writing process is best described as:
Solution: The writing process is recursive: a draft sentence exposes a flaw in the outline, a revision step reveals the thesis needs sharpening, an editing pass uncovers a logical gap. The linear diagram is a useful map, not the territory.
Question 3
When should you finalise your thesis?
Solution: Strong writers often draft the thesis last, after a body section or two has revealed their actual argument. Locking in a vague thesis early and refusing to revise it is one of the most common reasons Grade 11 essays underperform.
Topic 2.2 — Paragraph Structure (PEEL)
Question 4
In PEEL, what is the function of the second "E" (Explain)?
Solution: The Explain step is where analytical work happens. Without it, the paragraph is summary or paraphrase. Strong Explain sentences name a specific technique and show why it produces the effect the topic sentence claimed.
Question 5
A reliable rule of thumb for the ratio of evidence to commentary in an analytical body paragraph is:
Solution: Each quotation requires at least two sentences of commentary. Stacking quotations without proportional commentary produces a paragraph in which the writer's own analytical voice is overwhelmed by source material — the opposite of analysis.
Question 6
A strong Link sentence at the end of a body paragraph should:
Solution: The Link advances the argument. A weak Link restates the Point; a strong Link names what this paragraph's analysis contributes to the thesis or signals the move to the next paragraph.
Topic 2.3 — Essay Forms
Question 7
An expository essay primarily aims to:
Solution: Expository essays inform and explain. The burden of proof is accuracy and clarity. The writer's voice is steady; emotional appeals are rare. Persuasive essays add the argumentative move; personal essays add the reflective one.
Question 8
Aristotle's three rhetorical appeals are:
Solution: Aristotle named three classical appeals: logos (logic and evidence), ethos (credibility of the speaker), pathos (emotion of the audience). Strong persuasive writing combines at least two.
Question 9
A personal essay is distinguished from a diary entry by:
Solution: A personal essay shapes lived experience into something a stranger could find valuable. It uses specific detail and earned reflection; it is not raw self-expression. Strong personal essays often pivot from a small concrete moment to a larger insight.
Topic 2.4 — Revising and Editing
Question 10
What is a comma splice?
Solution: A comma splice joins two independent clauses with only a comma. Repair: replace the comma with a period or semicolon, add a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so), or subordinate one clause.
Question 11
Revision and editing differ because:
Solution: Revision and editing are different operations. Revise first (cut paragraphs, sharpen thesis, find stronger evidence), then edit (commas, fragments, agreement). Editing a sentence you will later cut is wasted effort.
Question 12
The single most reliable editing technique for the final pass is:
Solution: Reading aloud reliably catches awkward phrasing, run-on sentences, missing words, and tense shifts that silent reading skips. The technique is free, takes ten minutes per 1,000 words, and catches errors no other method does.
