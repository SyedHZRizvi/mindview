Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Name the four parts of the PEEL paragraph structure and the function of each.
Answer Key

Point — the topic sentence stating a debatable claim. Evidence — a short embedded quotation with citation. Explain — interpretation of how the evidence supports the point, naming the device or technique and explaining its effect. Link — closing sentence that connects back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph.

2
[3]
Define and distinguish expository, persuasive, and personal essays.
Answer Key

Expository — informs and explains; thesis is informative; voice is steady; burden of proof is accuracy. Persuasive — argues an opinion; uses logos/ethos/pathos; engages counterargument; burden is to move a sceptical reader. Personal — reflects on lived experience; arrives at an insight a stranger can share; burden is honesty plus craft.

3
[3]
Define Aristotle's three rhetorical appeals and give one short example of each.
Answer Key

Logos — appeal to logic and evidence (e.g., a statistic or causal argument). Ethos — appeal through credibility (e.g., "As a Canadian doctor with 30 years of experience…"). Pathos — appeal to emotion (e.g., the puppy in an insurance ad, the slow piano under a political message).

4
[3]
Identify and correct the error in each sentence: (a) "Macbeth hesitates, Lady Macbeth pushes him forward." (b) "Because the witches plant the idea." (c) "Each character must guard their tongue."
Answer Key

(a) Comma splice — repair: "Macbeth hesitates, but Lady Macbeth pushes him forward." or use a period/semicolon. (b) Sentence fragment — repair: "Because the witches plant the idea, Macbeth begins to consider the murder." (c) Pronoun-antecedent disagreement (singular antecedent, plural pronoun) — repair: "Characters must guard their tongues" (plural) or "Each character must guard his or her tongue."

5
[3]
What is the difference between revision and editing? Give one example of each.
Answer Key

Revision — large-scale changes to argument, structure, and evidence (e.g., cutting an entire paragraph, sharpening the thesis, replacing weak evidence). Editing — sentence-level corrections (e.g., fixing a comma splice, repairing subject-verb agreement, removing redundant words). Revise first, edit second.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[5]
Rewrite the following weak thesis as a strong analytical thesis on the same text and topic: "Macbeth shows that ambition is bad."
Answer Key

Sample strong thesis: "Shakespeare presents ambition in Macbeth not as a character flaw but as a contagious idea — one that moves between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth across the play, depending on which of them is doing the imagining, so that the play tracks not one ambitious person but the way ambition migrates between people." Marks: 1 specificity, 1 debatability, 1 reference to evidence/mechanism, 1 a clear analytical claim, 1 conventions.

7
[5]
Read the following weak paragraph and identify three specific weaknesses. For each weakness, suggest how to fix it.
"Macbeth feels really guilty after killing Duncan. He sees a dagger. The dagger is not really there but he thinks it is. This shows he is feeling bad. Lady Macbeth doesn't feel guilty at first. She says 'a little water clears us of this deed.' Later she goes crazy. So the play is about guilt."
Answer Key

Weaknesses include: (1) topic sentence is a feeling statement, not a debatable claim — replace with a claim about how guilt is staged (e.g., bodily contamination, hand-washing motif). (2) Quotation has no citation and no signal phrase — repair: "Lady Macbeth dismisses the murder with the casual claim that 'a little water clears us of this deed' (2.2.86)." (3) Final sentence is paraphrase, not analysis — replace with a claim about what the play does with guilt (e.g., "Lady Macbeth's mid-scene confidence and her later sleepwalking are the same character without the request — Shakespeare lets guilt migrate between the Macbeths"). Marks: 1.5 per identified weakness + suggested fix; 0.5 conventions.

8
[5]
A student wants to write a persuasive essay arguing that high schools should drop standardised testing. Draft (a) one ethos appeal, (b) one logos appeal, and (c) one pathos appeal she could use, each in 1–2 sentences.
Answer Key

Sample: (a) Ethos: "The Ontario College of Teachers, an organisation representing more than 200,000 educators, has repeatedly questioned the validity of standardised testing in 21st-century classrooms." (b) Logos: "Three independent meta-analyses of standardised-test effects show no consistent correlation between test scores and later academic success once family income is controlled for." (c) Pathos: "When a thirteen-year-old has to be told her future depends on a multiple-choice exam she will take in three weeks, we are not measuring her — we are teaching her that her education is a guess." Marks: 1.5 each appeal + 0.5 conventions.

Part C: Communication [15 marks]
9
[8]
Write one complete PEEL body paragraph (6–8 sentences) on the following claim: "Lady Macbeth's 'unsex me here' speech (1.5.40–55) is not a declaration of cruelty but a confession of need." Embed at least one direct quotation with proper act.scene.line citation.
Answer Key

Look for: (P) topic sentence asserts that the speech is a confession of need; (E) embedded quotation with signal phrase and citation, e.g., "She prays for spirits to 'fill me from the crown to the toe top-full / Of direst cruelty' (1.5.42–3)"; (Explain) interpretation of how the verb "fill" admits emptiness and the metaphor of a vessel reveals current absence rather than current presence of cruelty; (Link) closing sentence connecting to a larger argument (e.g., the collapse in 5.1 is the playing-out of a borrowed strength). Marks: 1 Point, 2 Evidence (quotation + citation), 3 Explanation depth, 2 Link + conventions.

10
[7]
Write the opening of a personal essay (5–7 sentences) on the topic "Something small that changed how I pay attention." Begin with a specific concrete moment; end with a sentence that gestures toward the reflection the rest of the essay would develop.
Answer Key

Look for: a specific concrete starting moment (not vague generalisation); sensory or precise detail; a clear narrative voice; a pivot from the concrete to the reflective in the final 1–2 sentences. Marks: 2 specificity of opening, 2 voice and craft, 2 the pivot toward reflection, 1 conventions.

Part D: Application [15 marks]
11
[7]
Apply what you have learned about counterargument. Choose a contested claim from current Ontario or Canadian public life (cellphone bans, AI in classrooms, voting age, healthcare, climate policy, anything). Write a one-paragraph (5–7 sentences) counterargument-and-refutation section: state the strongest opposing view, concede its strongest part, then refute it on specific grounds.
Answer Key

Strong responses follow the protocol: name the strongest opposing view honestly (1), concede the strongest part of it (1), refute on specific grounds with named evidence (2), end with a sentence that re-establishes the writer's claim (1). Avoid strawmanning (1) and avoid one-sentence dismissals (1). Total 7.

12
[8]
Real-World Application: A student is preparing to apply to university. Explain in 5–7 sentences why each of the three essay forms (expository, persuasive, personal) might be useful in different parts of a university application. Be specific about which form serves which purpose.
Answer Key

Strong responses might note: expository for explaining a program choice (the program-specific supplement that asks "why this program?"); persuasive for advocating for a particular admission decision when a reference letter or interview asks "make the case for your fit"; personal for the supplemental essay that asks about lived experience and growth ("describe a challenge you've faced"). Marks: 2 for each form correctly matched to a purpose with a specific example (6), 2 communication.
