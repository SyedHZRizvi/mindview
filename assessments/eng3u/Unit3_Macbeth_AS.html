Topic 3.1 — Jacobean Context
Question 1
Macbeth was probably written around:
Solution: Macbeth is a Jacobean play, written ~1606. Knowing this matters because the play responds to specific contemporary anxieties — regicide, the Gunpowder Plot, equivocation, witchcraft — that shape what the play imagines.
Question 2
"Equivocation" — words that say two contradictory things at once — was a doctrine of interest to Shakespeare's first audience because:
Solution: The Porter's drunken speech in 2.3 about "an equivocator" refers directly to Garnet's trial. The witches' "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" (1.1.10) is a verbal equivocation, and the line lands as charged contemporary reference for a 1606 audience.
Question 3
Banquo is descended (in the play's genealogy) from a figure whose line stretches to "th' crack of doom" (4.1.117). This compliments:
Solution: James I claimed descent from Banquo. Showing Banquo's line stretching forward in 4.1 was a deliberate compliment to the patron sitting (potentially) in the front row of a court performance.
Topic 3.2 — Tragic Form
Question 4
Macbeth himself names his tragic flaw in a soliloquy. He calls it:
Solution: Macbeth names his hamartia in Act 1 Scene 7: "I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on th' other" (1.7.25–28). The metaphor (a rider whose vault clears the horse) already contains the catastrophe.
Question 5
In Aristotle's Poetics, hamartia means:
Solution: Hamartia is a tragic flaw or error of judgement. Hubris is one species of hamartia (excessive pride). Anagnorisis is recognition. Peripeteia is reversal. Knowing these four terms cold is a Grade 11 expectation.
Question 6
Banquo, Macduff, and Malcolm function in the play as Macbeth's:
Solution: Foils sharpen the tragic question by staging alternative responses. Banquo hears the same prophecy and does not act; Macduff loses his family to political principle; Malcolm tests his readiness for rule. Reading Macbeth through the foils is what graduates a competent essay to a strong one.
Topic 3.3 — Soliloquy
Question 7
A soliloquy is:
Solution: Soliloquy is the convention by which a character alone on stage shares thought with the audience. A long speech with others present is a monologue. The two are often confused; the test is whether other characters are present.
Question 8
"If it were done when 'tis done, then 'twere well / It were done quickly." (1.7.1–2)
What is the analytical significance of the repetition of "done" four times in two lines?
Solution: Macbeth is trying to compress the murder into a single closed action ("done"). The repetition exposes the effort and the impossibility — the play spends three acts proving that the murder will never be "done" in the sense Macbeth needs it to be.
Question 9
When Lady Macbeth speaks in prose in Act 5 Scene 1 (the sleepwalking scene), the verse-to-prose shift signals:
Solution: In Shakespeare, the verse-to-prose shift is a grammatical marker of social status, madness, or psychological breakdown. Lady Macbeth's collapse from formal blank verse (1.5) to fragmented prose (5.1) is the play's syntactic announcement of her unmaking.
Topic 3.4 — Motifs
Question 10
The motif of blood in Macbeth:
Solution: The blood motif moves from external violence to internal guilt without ever leaving the same word. Tracking it across acts produces a theme — that moral guilt is staged through bodily contamination, and the body cannot be cleaned of what the mind has authorised.
Question 11
A "theme" in Macbeth is best stated as:
Solution: Themes are sentences, not nouns. A topic is "ambition"; a theme is a claim about ambition the play makes. The diagnostic question: could a reader disagree with this sentence? If yes, you have a theme.
Question 12
"A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!" — Lady Macbeth (2.2.86–7)
"Here's the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand." — Lady Macbeth (5.1.55–7)
Reading these two Lady Macbeth lines together, the strongest analytical observation is:
Solution: Shakespeare uses the same image (water on hands) across three acts to track a moral exchange between two characters. Tracking the motif across the play is how a strong essay produces a defensible theme rather than a recycled assertion that "the play is about guilt."
