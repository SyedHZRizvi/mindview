Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Define and give one short example of each: ethos, pathos, logos.
Answer Key

Ethos — appeal through speaker credibility (e.g., a 30-year doctor recommending a brand; a 150-year-old company invoking its history). Pathos — appeal to audience emotion (the puppy in an insurance ad; slow piano under a political message). Logos — appeal to logic and evidence (statistics, demonstrations, comparison charts).

2
[3]
Define and give one short example of each propaganda technique: bandwagon, testimonial, glittering generality.
Answer Key

Bandwagon — appeal to popularity ("Everyone is switching to…"). Testimonial — endorsement by a celebrity or authority figure. Glittering generality — vague positive language with no falsifiable content ("Vote for change," "Open happiness").

3
[3]
"SIFT" for source evaluation stands for:
Answer Key

A. Mike Caulfield's SIFT method. Stop before reading; Investigate the source by opening a new tab; Find better coverage; Trace claims to their original context.

4
[3]
Define affordance in media studies and give one specific affordance of any one platform (TikTok, Instagram, X/Twitter, podcast, print).
Answer Key

Affordance = what a medium makes easy or hard to do. Example: TikTok's affordances include vertical short-form video, algorithmic For You distribution, and built-in remix tools — they reward density of attention and punish complexity. Podcast's affordances include length (episodes are routinely 60+ minutes), audio intimacy (the voice is in the listener's ear), and time-shifting (asynchronous listening).

5
[3]
Distinguish among news reporting, opinion / editorial, and sponsored / native content. Why does the distinction matter to a reader?
Answer Key

News reporting — fact-led, verified sources, attributed; standard journalism. Opinion / editorial — argued position from a writer or editorial board; voiced and labelled. Sponsored / native content — paid material designed to resemble news. The distinction matters because reputable outlets label these clearly; less reputable ones blur them. The first move on any digital story is to find the label.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[5]
Two headlines on the same story: (A) "Federal government commits $4 billion to transit; opposition warns of debt impact." (B) "Liberals push through $4 billion transit spending despite Conservative debt warnings." Analyse the framing of each. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

(A) leads with the action ("commits"), uses neutral verbs, frames opposition as a contingent voice ("warns"). (B) leads with the actor's party label ("Liberals"), uses an aggressive verb ("push through"), elevates the opposition to an equal noun ("Conservative debt warnings"). The facts are identical; the framing has done political work before the reader has begun the article. Lateral reading exposes which framings are local to which outlets. Marks: 2 specific framing differences, 2 interpretation of the effect, 1 communication.

7
[5]
A 30-second cola advertisement features a diverse group of friends laughing at a beach at sunset, with one bottle of the brand visible in the centre of the frame. The voice-over says: "Open happiness." Analyse three rhetorical techniques the ad uses and explain how each produces its effect. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Likely techniques: (1) identification (Burke) — the viewer is invited to recognise herself as the kind of person whose afternoons could look like this; (2) ethos — a 130-year-old brand name carries automatic credibility; (3) glittering generality — "Open happiness" is vague enough to deny falsification but specific enough to imply a causal claim; (4) minimal logos — no claims about flavour or nutrition. Strong responses notice that the product is small in the frame, because the real product is identity. Marks: 3 techniques + 2 effect explanations.

8
[5]
A friend forwards a viral post claiming a Canadian celebrity has died. The post links to a website you have never heard of. In 5–7 sentences, walk through the steps you would take to evaluate the claim before sharing.
Answer Key

Strong responses apply SIFT and lateral reading: (1) Stop — notice your reaction and resist sharing yet; (2) Investigate the source — open a new tab; search the site's name + "credibility" / "wiki"; check whether it has named authors and editorial standards; (3) Find better coverage — search for the celebrity's name on Reuters, CBC, BBC, the Globe and Mail; if the story is real, multiple reputable outlets will report it; if no reputable outlet reports it, the claim is unverified at best; (4) Trace claims — check whether the original source is named in the post; if the post is a screenshot of a tweet, find the tweet and check the account. Marks: 4 procedural moves named and explained, 1 communication.

Part C: Communication [15 marks]
9
[7]
Write a 250-word media-analysis paragraph on any real-world media text (an advertisement, a news headline, a viral social media post). Identify the form and platform, name two rhetorical techniques, and end with a defensible claim about what the text is doing.
Answer Key

Look for: clear identification of form and platform (1), two rhetorical techniques named and explained (3), a defensible thematic claim about the text's argument or effect (2), conventions and voice (1).

10
[8]
Draft a 60-second PSA brief on a topic you care about (mental health, voter registration, road safety, social-media use, any). The brief must name: (a) purpose, (b) audience, (c) platform, (d) intended effect, (e) two specific creative choices (music, framing, length, tone, captions, voiceover). Aim for 5–7 sentences total.
Answer Key

Look for: each of the five required elements (a–e) named and specific (5 marks for 1 each); the creative choices are answerable back to the brief — i.e., they serve the audience and intended effect (2); communication (1). A vague brief like "make a video about voting" loses the specificity marks.

Part D: Application / Original Media Text [15 marks]
11
[10]
Apply what you have learned. Submit your original media text (short video, podcast episode, advertising campaign, social-media awareness series, print zine, or interactive piece) according to the brief from question 10 — or a new brief approved by your teacher. The submission box below is for the project link / file reference and a brief description; the actual media file is submitted separately.
Rubric — Original Media Text (10 marks)

Conformity to brief (2): does the artifact actually do what the brief said it would? Form / conventions (2): does it use the form's conventions skillfully? Accessibility (2): captions present and accurate; high-contrast typography; alt text where applicable. Originality and craft (2): does the artifact rise above a template? Ethical use (2): copyright respected; sources credited; no unattributed AI-generated content.

12
[5]
Reflection (Production Journal): Write a 200-word reflection on the media text you just submitted. Address: what trade-off you made and why; one production limit you ran into; what you learned for next time.
Answer Key

Strong reflections name a specific trade-off (e.g., "the rough cut was 75 seconds and I had to lose the strongest interview line"), defend the decision (e.g., "I kept the silence over the line, because the silence works as both a pause and a rhetorical caesura"), name a production limit, and state a concrete lesson. Marks: 1 trade-off, 1 defended, 1 limit, 1 lesson, 1 communication.
