Topic 1.1 — Close Reading
Question 1
The single most reliable practice that distinguishes a strong Grade 11 reader from a weak one is:
Solution: Close reading depends on re-reading and annotation. The first read absorbs plot; the second read sees structure and pattern. Annotations leave a trail of attention you can return to when drafting.
Question 2
"It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." — George Orwell, opening of 1984
A strong close reader's first analytical move on this sentence is to notice:
Solution: The opening juxtaposes a realistic setting with one impossible detail (thirteenth chime), training the reader from the first sentence to look for what is wrong in this world. That move is what close reading produces.
Question 3
Tone and mood differ because:
Solution: Tone is produced by the writer (bitter, reverent, sardonic). Mood is felt by the audience (eerie, joyful). Keeping them separate prevents one of the most common analytical confusions in Grade 11 essays.
Topic 1.2 — Literary Devices
Question 4
"O my Luve is like a red, red rose / That's newly sprung in June." — Robert Burns
The dominant device in these two lines is:
Solution: A simile uses "like" or "as" for the comparison; a metaphor identifies one thing as another without those markers. Distinguishing the two is one of the most-tested literary-device questions in Grade 11.
Question 5
A boy in a story plants a seed; later, his mother dies; later, the seed has grown into a tree. The recurring image of the tree is best described as:
Solution: Symbols are concrete images that gain abstract meaning through repetition and the surrounding context (here, life persisting through loss). A symbol must accumulate weight; a one-off image is not yet a symbol.
Question 6
In Macbeth, the audience knows the witches are dangerous from the first scene, but Macbeth does not yet. This gap between audience knowledge and character knowledge is:
Solution: Dramatic irony is the structured gap between what a character knows and what the audience knows. Verbal irony is saying the opposite of what is meant; situational irony is the gap between expectation and outcome.
Topic 1.3 — Inference and Evidence
Question 7
The difference between an inference and a guess is:
Solution: An inference is a reasoned claim about the text supported by stated and implied details. A guess is unsupported. Strong analysis names the inference, the evidence, and the reasoning that connects them.
Question 8
The strongest form of textual evidence for a Grade 11 essay is:
Solution: A short, embedded quotation is precise, integrates with the writer's analytical sentence, and shows craft. Block quotations are reserved for moments when the full passage's structure is itself the evidence.
Question 9
A "signal phrase" is:
Solution: A signal phrase is the words that introduce a quotation and tell the reader where it came from — "Atwood writes…", "Macbeth observes…". Without a signal phrase, the quotation reads as orphaned.
Topic 1.4 — Paraphrase vs Analysis
Question 10
Which of the following is analysis rather than paraphrase?
Solution: Choice (c) names a specific technique (the unanswered question, the syntax), explains the effect (doubt enacted by form), and connects to a larger claim. (a), (b), and (d) are summary or paraphrase.
Question 11
In the PEEL paragraph structure, the second "E" stands for:
Solution: PEEL = Point, Evidence, Explain, Link. The "Explain" step is where analytical work happens — interpreting how the quoted evidence supports the topic-sentence claim. Skipping it produces summary, not analysis.
Question 12
A strong "Link" sentence at the end of a body paragraph should:
Solution: The Link advances the argument. A weak Link restates the Point; a strong Link names what this paragraph's analysis contributes to the larger thesis or signals the move to the next paragraph.
