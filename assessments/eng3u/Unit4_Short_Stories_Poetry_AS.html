Topic 4.1 — The Short Story
Question 1
A first-person retrospective narrator is one who:
Solution: First-person retrospective is a common literary point of view. The older voice can see what the younger one could not — the gap between the two selves is often where the story's analytical meaning lives.
Question 2
An unreliable narrator is one who:
Solution: Unreliable narrators are central to many strong short stories (Charlotte Perkins Gilman, Edgar Allan Poe, Vladimir Nabokov). Identifying the narrator's reliability is the first analytical move in any short-fiction reading.
Question 3
In medias res means:
Solution: In medias res (Latin: "into the middle of things") drops the reader into action and trusts the reader to catch up. It is the opposite of a conventional exposition-first opening.
Topic 4.2 — Reading Poetry
Question 4
"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" — Shakespeare, Sonnet 18
The metre of this line is:
Solution: Iambic pentameter — five iambic feet, each unstressed-stressed. Scan: "shall I / com-PARE / thee TO / a SUM- / mer's DAY." This is Shakespeare's default meter for nobles and for his sonnets.
Question 5
A Shakespearean (English) sonnet is structured as:
Solution: The Shakespearean sonnet has 14 lines: 3 quatrains in alternating rhyme (ABAB CDCD EFEF) followed by a closing couplet (GG) that often delivers a turn or resolution. Choice (a) describes the Petrarchan / Italian sonnet.
Question 6
Enjambment is:
Solution: Enjambment is one of the most powerful devices in poetry — and is almost invisible until heard. Reading aloud is the fastest way to feel it. The micro-pause at the line break, even when grammar continues, creates tension that prose cannot reproduce.
Topic 4.3 — Theme & Figurative Language
Question 7
Which of the following is a theme (as opposed to a topic)?
Solution: Themes are sentences, not nouns. A topic is what the text is about; a theme is what the text claims about its topic. The diagnostic question: could a reader disagree with this sentence? If yes, it is a theme.
Question 8
"And miles to go before I sleep, / And miles to go before I sleep." — Robert Frost, "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening"
The strongest analytical reading of this repeated final line is:
Solution: The repetition is the technique. First iteration is literal; second invites metaphor. Repetition is one of the most common ways poems produce theme — layering meaning by saying the same words a second time in a new context.
Question 9
A "counter-reading" of a text is:
Solution: Strong texts sustain more than one defensible reading. A strong essay engages a serious counter-reading and either concedes-and-refutes or extends the original claim in light of it. Ignoring counter-readings makes an essay weaker, not stronger.
Topic 4.4 — Comparative Analysis
Question 10
In a comparative essay, "point-by-point" structure means:
Solution: Point-by-point: each paragraph touches both texts under a single analytical category. Block (text-by-text) is the alternative — easier to draft but tends to produce parallel summaries. Point-by-point produces sharper analytical paragraphs and is recommended for Grade 11.
Question 11
The strongest test of whether a comparative paragraph is doing comparative work is:
Solution: The closing sentence test: if you removed one of the two texts from the paragraph, would the closing sentence still be defensible? If yes, the paragraph is parallel summary. If no, the comparison has produced an analytical claim neither text alone could produce. That is synthesis.
Question 12
The most useful way to think about comparative analysis is:
Solution: The disagreement between texts is the analytical point. If two stories handle a parent-child relationship differently, the gap between them tells you what choices each writer made that another writer could have made differently — that gap is what comparative analysis is for.
