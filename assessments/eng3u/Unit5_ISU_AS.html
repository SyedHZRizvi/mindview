Topic 5.1 — Research Question
Question 1
Which of the following is a strong ISU research question?
Solution: Strong research questions are specific, have multiple defensible answers, and predict the evidence the essay will need. (c) meets all three criteria. (a) and (d) are answerable in one sentence; (b) is a topic, not a question.
Question 2
When should you finalise your ISU research question?
Solution: Strong ISUs treat the research question as a moving target. Reading reshapes the question, and that reshaping is part of the work. Locking in a question on day one and refusing to revise it almost always produces an essay that tries to force evidence into a shape that no longer fits.
Question 3
The most common reason ISU projects fail is:
Solution: ISU projects fail more often from fatigue than from incompetence. If you cannot imagine yourself wanting to read about your chosen topic in week seven (when the essay is due in week eight and you are tired), the topic is wrong. Sustained interest is the sleeper requirement.
Topic 5.2 — Source Evaluation
Question 4
The SIFT method stands for:
Solution: Mike Caulfield's SIFT method: Stop before reading; Investigate the source by opening a new tab; Find better coverage from reputable outlets; Trace claims to their original context. It is faster and more reliable than a checklist of "is this site trustworthy?"
Question 5
"Lateral reading" means:
Solution: Lateral reading is the single most useful evaluation technique available to students. It costs thirty seconds and produces a substantial upgrade in judgment — shifting from a design question (which is cheap to fake) to an evidence question (which is much harder to fake).
Question 6
Which of the following is NOT a citable academic source for an ISU?
Solution: SparkNotes, CliffsNotes, LitCharts, and AI chatbots are study aids, not citable sources. They have no peer review, no named author, and are designed to substitute for reading. Citing them in an ISU undermines credibility and removes you from the actual scholarly conversation.
Topic 5.3 — MLA Citation
Question 7
In MLA 9th edition, the in-text citation for drama (a Shakespeare play) is formatted as:
Solution: Drama uses act.scene.line. Poetry uses line numbers. Prose uses page numbers. Using page numbers for plays is a common Grade 11 error that reads as careless.
Question 8
When embedding a verse quotation that spans more than one line in your essay's sentence, the line break is marked by:
Solution: Short verse quotations embedded in your sentence use a forward slash to mark each line break. Example: "Some say the world will end in fire, / Some say in ice" (Frost 1–2).
Question 9
Paraphrased ideas (where you restate a source's idea in your own words):
Solution: Paraphrased ideas still require citation. The rule is about ideas, not just words. Changing the words while keeping the idea — without attribution — is still plagiarism. When in doubt, cite.
Topic 5.4 — Oral Presentation
Question 10
A strong ISU oral presentation should be structured around:
Solution: A talk that tries to convey six claims usually conveys none. A talk that lands a single sharp claim with three supporting reasons is memorable. Signposting helps the listener follow ("My second reason is…"), because they cannot rewind.
Question 11
For slides, the most reliable design rule is:
Solution: Slides should support, not replace, the talk. Six words is a reasonable upper bound; an image, a quotation, or a single data point reads better than a bulleted paragraph. The audience should leave remembering your argument, not your animations.
Question 12
When answering a hostile or challenging question in the Q&A, the strongest move is:
Solution: Paraphrase shows you understood; concession earns credibility; refutation advances the argument; redirection ties the answer back to your thesis. This sequence is the Q&A protocol and works across topics.
