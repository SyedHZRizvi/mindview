Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Define each device and give one example: (a) verbal irony, (b) dramatic irony, (c) situational irony.
Answer Key

(a) Verbal irony — saying the opposite of what is meant; e.g., "Lovely weather!" during a storm. (b) Dramatic irony — the audience knows something a character doesn't; e.g., the audience knows the witches' prophecy is dangerous; Macbeth does not yet. (c) Situational irony — outcome is the opposite of what is expected; e.g., a fire station burns down.

2
[2]
Define a simile and distinguish it from a metaphor. Give one original example of each.
Answer Key

A simile is an explicit comparison using "like" or "as" ("My love is like a red rose"). A metaphor is an implicit identification, without those markers ("Juliet is the sun"). The presence or absence of "like"/"as" is the diagnostic feature.

3
[2]
Define tone and distinguish it from mood.
Answer Key

Tone = the author's or speaker's attitude toward the subject (bitter, reverent, sardonic). Mood = the emotional atmosphere created in the reader (eerie, joyful). Tone is produced by the writer; mood is felt by the audience.

4
[3]
Name and define three points of view used in narrative fiction.
Answer Key

First-person ("I"): narrator is a character inside the story. Third-person limited: narrator is outside but tied to one character's mind. Third-person omniscient: narrator knows every character's interior. Also acceptable: second-person ("you"), third-person objective (no inner thoughts).

5
[2]
In a PEEL paragraph, what does each letter stand for?
Answer Key

Point — a topic sentence stating a debatable claim about the text. Evidence — a short embedded quotation with citation. Explain — analysis of how the evidence supports the point, naming the device or technique and saying why it produces this effect. Link — a closing sentence connecting back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph.

6
[3]
Define connotation, denotation, and diction. Use the word "home" to illustrate the first two.
Answer Key

Denotation = the dictionary meaning. "Home" = a place where one lives. Connotation = the emotional/cultural associations. "Home" connotes warmth, safety, belonging — connotations a synonym like "residence" lacks. Diction = the author's or speaker's deliberate word choices, ranging from formal to colloquial, abstract to concrete.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
7
[5]
"It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." — George Orwell, opening of 1984
Analyse what this opening sentence accomplishes. Refer to specific words and the juxtaposition of details. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Strong responses notice: (1) the paired adjectives "bright cold," which should not feel pleasant together, signal discomfort from the first words; (2) "April" — the month of renewal — is used in a way that feels wrong; (3) "the clocks were striking thirteen" slips one impossible detail into otherwise realistic prose. The sentence trains the reader to look for what is wrong in this world. Marks: 1 for identifying juxtaposition, 1 for the diction observation, 1 for the impossible detail, 1 for explaining the cumulative effect (uneasy realism), 1 for conventions and integration.

8
[5]
"My father was a fox farmer. That is, he raised silver foxes, in pens; and in the fall and early winter, when their fur was prime, he killed them and skinned them and sold their pelts." — Alice Munro, opening of "Boys and Girls"
Make two inferences about the narrator (a young farm girl looking back) based on this opening, and identify the textual detail that supports each.
Answer Key

Strong responses might infer: (1) the narrator already identifies herself by her father — supported by the sentence "My father was a fox farmer," in which the narrator's first self-introduction is via her father; (2) the narrator is reporting from between two worlds, since the parenthetical "That is" assumes a reader unfamiliar with farm life. (3) Acceptable also: the narrator has been desensitised to violence as ordinary labour, supported by the matter-of-fact verb sequence "killed them and skinned them and sold." Marks: 2 inferences (1 each) + 2 supporting details (1 each) + 1 communication.

9
[5]
"The fog comes / on little cat feet. // It sits looking / over harbor and city / on silent haunches / and then moves on." — Carl Sandburg, "Fog"
Identify three literary devices in this short poem and explain how each contributes to the poem's overall effect (stillness, transience, observation).
Answer Key

Devices likely include: (1) extended metaphor — fog as cat, sustained for the whole poem, makes the fog seem like a sentient observer; (2) personification ("sits looking," "moves on") — gives the fog a contemplative attention that mirrors the speaker's; (3) free verse / short lines — the lack of regular meter and white space enacts the weightless transience of fog. Also acceptable: alliteration ("silent" / "sits"), imagery. Marks: 3 device identifications, 2 effect explanations.

Part C: Communication [15 marks]
10
[5]
Write one analytical PEEL paragraph on the following claim: "In Frost's 'The Road Not Taken,' the speaker's tone is more wistful than triumphant." Embed at least one direct quotation with proper line citation. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Look for: (P) clear topic sentence asserting wistfulness over triumph; (E) one embedded quotation with signal phrase and line citation, e.g., "And sorry I could not travel both" (line 2); (Explain) interpretation of how diction ("sorry," "sigh") and future-tense framing ("I shall be telling this with a sigh") signal regret rather than victory; (Link) closing sentence linking back to thesis. Marks: 1 Point, 1 Evidence with citation, 2 Explanation, 1 Link + conventions.

11
[5]
Rewrite the following weak topic sentence into a strong analytical claim: "The story is about a girl who lives on a farm."
Answer Key

Sample strong topic sentence: "Munro's 'Boys and Girls' uses the narrator's slow naming as 'only a girl' — and her quiet acceptance of the label — to record how social verdicts colonise interior life faster than overt force could." Look for: a debatable claim (1), specific reference to text/technique (1), an interpretive verb ("uses…to record") (1), specific evidence implied (1), conventions (1).

12
[5]
Compare and contrast a metaphor and a symbol in a short paragraph (4 sentences). Define each, explain how they relate, and use one literary example for each.
Answer Key

Both connect a concrete image to an abstract idea. A metaphor is local — a single comparison (e.g., "Juliet is the sun"). A symbol is sustained — the same concrete image recurs and accumulates meaning across a whole work (the dagger in Macbeth, the road in Frost). All sustained metaphors can become symbols; not every metaphor does. Marks: 1 each definition, 1 the relationship, 1 examples and conventions.

Part D: Application [15 marks]
13
[5]
"After supper my father says, 'Want to go down and see if the Lake's still there?'" — Alice Munro, opening of "Walker Brothers Cowboy"
Apply what you have learned about close reading: in 5–7 sentences, analyse what the father's question reveals about the family's relationship to place. Refer to specific words.
Answer Key

Strong responses notice: (1) the matter-of-fact question registers a child's sense that the world might be unstable enough for a lake to vanish overnight; (2) the timing ("after supper") makes the descent to the lake a ritual; (3) the father's casual delivery ("Want to…") makes the trip feel both intimate and small; (4) the contraction "Lake's" — capitalised — treats the lake as a proper name, suggesting a singular landmark that anchors the family. Marks: 2 specific details from the sentence, 2 interpretations, 1 communication.

14
[5]
"It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." — Jane Austen, opening of Pride and Prejudice
Apply the concept of verbal irony to this sentence. Whose voice are we hearing? Whose belief is being mocked? What does the irony achieve as an opening?
Answer Key

The sentence states the opposite of Austen's actual view: wealthy single men are not, factually, "in want of a wife"; rather, the matchmaking mothers of the neighbourhood are in want of wealthy sons-in-law. The "universally acknowledged truth" is a parody of how village gossip elevates self-interest into universal law. The irony immediately positions the narrator's wit above the surrounding social world, training the reader to read everything else with measured distance. Marks: 1 identifying verbal irony, 1 naming whose belief is mocked, 2 the social-satire reading, 1 the structural function.

15
[5]
Real-World Application: Why does close reading matter outside English class? Choose one real-world context (a news headline, a social-media post, an advertisement, a politician's tweet) and explain in 5–7 sentences how the reading skills from this unit (tone, diction, irony, connotation, inference) help you evaluate it critically. Provide a specific (real or invented) example.
Answer Key

Strong responses identify how close-reading transfers: tone-detection helps spot sarcasm in a tweet; diction analysis flags loaded language ("crackdown" vs "policy"); irony spotting can reveal genuine vs performed apologies; connotation reveals subtle bias ("rebels" vs "freedom fighters"); inference lets the reader see what is implied but not stated. Marks: 1 selection of a clear context, 2 transfer of two skills, 1 specific example, 1 communication.
