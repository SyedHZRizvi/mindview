Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]A · RIASEC
Define the six Holland RIASEC interest types and give one occupation example for each.
Answer Key: Realistic — hands-on, practical, outdoor (electrician, paramedic, farmer). Investigative — analytical, problem-solving, research (biologist, software developer, statistician). Artistic — creative self-expression, design, performance (graphic designer, musician, architect). Social — helping, teaching, counselling (nurse, teacher, social worker). Enterprising — leading, persuading, selling (sales manager, entrepreneur, lawyer). Conventional — structured, data, organisation (accountant, administrator, logistics analyst). 0.5 mark per definition + 0.5 per valid example × 6.
2
[2]A · OSP
Name three of the nine Ontario Skills Passport essential skills and one of the three work habits.
Answer Key: Any three of: reading, writing, numeracy, oral communication, digital, problem solving, working with others, finding and using information, continuous learning. Any one work habit: working safely, working independently, using initiative. 0.5 marks each.
3
[2]A · Mindset
Carol Dweck's "growth mindset" is best defined as:
Answer Key: Option 3. Growth mindset = ability is developed (not fixed). Contrasts with fixed mindset (ability is innate). It is a method — when you fail, you collect specific information and try a new strategy — not toxic positivity.
4
[3]A · Concepts
Distinguish between interests, skills, and values. Give one sentence for each.
Answer Key: Interests = what you enjoy doing or are curious about (e.g., reading mystery novels, fixing bikes). Skills = what you can demonstrably do, built through practice (e.g., welding, public speaking, basic spreadsheet). Values = the qualities you treat as important — what matters most about how you live and work (e.g., helping others, autonomy, security). All three are necessary inputs to a good career plan; none is sufficient alone. 1 mark each.
5
[3]A · Stress
Explain the Lazarus & Folkman transactional model of stress. Why does it predict that two students with identical workloads might experience very different stress levels?
Answer Key: Stress = demand × appraisal (how threatening you judge the demand) × resources (what you have to meet it with — time, support, prior preparation, sleep, money). Same demand → different stress because two students may appraise the threat differently (one sees the test as data, the other as a verdict) and have different resources (study time, mentors, sleep). Intervention paths: change appraisal (cognitive reframing) or build resources. 1 mark each element.
Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
6
[4]A · Critique
A friend says: "I took one online RIASEC quiz and it told me to be a graphic designer. So that's settled — that's my career."
Critique your friend's reasoning. Identify two specific flaws in how they are using self-assessment, and recommend a better approach.
Answer Key: Flaws: (1) Single-source result — one quiz is not enough; the Ministry expects triangulation across multiple assessments (RIASEC, skills, values, learning preferences), external feedback, and reflective writing. (2) Treating the output as a verdict rather than a working hypothesis — RIASEC results should be tested in real settings (job-shadow, volunteer, club), not treated as fixed identity. (3) Skipping the interaction with values, skills, and labour-market evidence. Better approach: complete several assessments, get external feedback, do informational interviews, and test the hypothesis in low-stakes ways. 1 mark per flaw, 2 marks for a substantive recommendation.
7
[5]A · Profile Fit
Priya's RIASEC code is SAI (Social, Artistic, Investigative). Top values: helping others, learning, balance (family time). She is strong in oral communication and weaker in numeracy under time pressure.
Identify two careers that fit Priya's profile and one career that would not fit. Justify each briefly by referring to her code, values, and skills.
Answer Key: Good fits (examples): Speech-language pathologist — S (helping people communicate), A (designing therapy activities), I (assessment), aligns with helping/learning, predictable hours fit balance. School counsellor — S, A, helping, learning, balance (school calendar). Health-education researcher — S, I, A, helping/learning. Poor fit: Day trader / investment banker — high E and C, frequent time-pressure numeracy, long hours violate balance value. 1 per career fit (× 2) + 1 justification each + 1 for poor-fit reasoning.
8
[4]A · Mindset Application
A student says: "I'm just not a science person." Analyse this statement through the lens of fixed vs. growth mindset. What is the underlying belief, what are the likely consequences, and how would you redirect the thought?
Answer Key: Reflects a fixed mindset — science ability framed as an innate identity ("a science person") rather than a skill that develops. Likely consequences: lower effort, withdrawal at difficulty, mark slides further, verdict feels confirmed. Redirect using "yet" and a strategy question: "I haven't mastered this yet. What specifically went wrong on the last test — concept or conditions? What study strategy will I try differently?" 1 mark identify mindset, 1 underlying belief, 1 consequence, 1 redirect.
Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[5]A · Personal Profile
Write a one-paragraph (5–7 sentences) "personal profile" about yourself, suitable for your IPP. Include at least one interest, one skill (with brief evidence), one value, and one learning preference. Use clear, specific language — no slogans.
Answer Key: Look for: (1) at least one interest named specifically; (2) skill with brief evidence (not just a claim); (3) value named; (4) learning preference; (5) cohesive paragraph form; (6) absence of vague slogans ("I'm a people person"). 1 mark per element + 1 for conventions/no slogans.
10
[3]A · Skill Statement
Rewrite the following weak skill claim into a strong, evidence-backed statement suitable for a résumé or IPP: "I'm a great communicator."
Answer Key: Sample strong rewrite: "Oral communication: Designed and delivered a 5-minute presentation to a Grade 9 advisory class (28 students) on accessing school mental-health supports; the advisory teacher's Learning Skills feedback noted 'clear, well-organised, made eye contact, answered questions confidently.'" 1 mark named skill (OSP-style), 1 specific evidence (numbers/setting), 1 corroboration or result.
11
[4]A · Plain-Language Explanation
In 3–4 sentences, explain to a Grade 9 student why values matter for career planning. Use one concrete example.
Answer Key: Look for: (1) clear definition of values in plain language; (2) the idea of value conflict — a job that clashes with deep values produces dissatisfaction over time even if pay or interest seem fine; (3) concrete example (e.g., someone who values balance ending up in a 70-hour job; or someone who values helping ending up in a strictly profit-driven role); (4) clear communication for Gr 9 audience. 1 mark each.
Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]A · Self-Application
Apply the self-assessment process to your own situation. Identify your top three career hypotheses (occupations you would explore further). For each, name one element of your personal profile that supports the hypothesis (an interest, a value, a skill, or a Holland code). You don't need to commit — these are working hypotheses to test.
Answer Key: Look for: 3 named occupations + 1 grounded justification each (interest, value, skill, or Holland-code link). Honesty is more important than ambition — a student listing a job that genuinely matches their profile scores higher than one listing a "prestigious" job with no connection to themselves. 1 per hypothesis (× 3) + 2 marks for thoughtful justifications.
13
[4]A · Time / Stress Application
Apply the Covey Matrix to a current week of your own life. List two tasks in each quadrant (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4) and identify one specific change you will make this week to spend more time in Q2.
Answer Key: Look for: (1) plausible Q1 tasks (deadlines, crises); (2) plausible Q2 tasks (study, exercise, sleep, family, deep work); (3) Q3 tasks (notifications, others' loud requests); (4) Q4 (scrolling); (5) one concrete change — e.g., book a 1-hour weekly review on Sunday, set Focus mode during 4–5 pm study, reduce one Q4 source. 0.5 per task × 8 = 4 marks, or 0.5 per quadrant + 2 marks for substantive change.
14
[3]A · STSE / Real-World
STSE Application: Why might an employer care more about your work habits (responsibility, organisation, initiative) than about which subjects you got the highest marks in? 2–3 sentences.
Answer Key: Most jobs can train technical skills, but work habits are harder to teach. An employee who arrives on time, follows through, and takes initiative creates value reliably; one who scored high in school but is unreliable creates costs. Ontario's Learning Skills exist because employers and postsecondary educators consistently rate work habits as predictive of success. 1 mark per insight, max 3.

📝 Communication Category Rubric (instructor-graded)

Per Ontario's Growing Success (2010) Achievement Chart, Communication is one of the four equally-weighted categories (25% of the course mark). This rubric is applied to every student-written response (short-answer, extended-response, essay, lab report, deputation, etc.) on this assessment.

Criterion Level 1 — Limited (50–59%) Level 2 — Some (60–69%) Level 3 — Considerable (70–79%) Level 4 — Thorough (80–100%)
Expression and organisation of ideas Ideas expressed with limited clarity; little organisation. Ideas expressed with some clarity; some organisation. Ideas expressed clearly and organised effectively. Ideas expressed with clarity, precision, and a high degree of organisation.
Communication for different audiences and purposes Limited awareness of audience/purpose; tone often inappropriate. Some awareness of audience/purpose; tone occasionally inappropriate. Consistently appropriate tone, register, and address to audience. Tone, register, voice, and address are precisely matched to audience and purpose.
Use of conventions, vocabulary, terminology Frequent errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation; little discipline-specific vocabulary. Some errors that occasionally impede meaning; emerging use of discipline-specific vocabulary. Few errors; consistent use of discipline-specific vocabulary. Virtually error-free; precise and varied use of discipline-specific vocabulary.

Level 3 represents the Provincial Standard. Use the most-recent and most-consistent evidence across multiple Communication tasks (this assessment, prior FOR work, written responses on AS quizzes, oral presentations) when assigning a course-level Communication mark. Growing Success, 2010, p. 17.
