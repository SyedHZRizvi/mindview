Topics 2.1–2.2 — Communication Models & Listening
Question 1
In the Shannon-Weaver communication model, "noise" refers to:
Solution: Noise is any source of distortion — physical (background sound), semantic (ambiguous wording), psychological (the receiver is anxious or angry), or cultural (different norms about directness). Identifying which kind of noise is interfering is the first step to fixing a communication breakdown.
Question 2
Sergei wants to disagree with his manager's proposed visual direction for a client presentation. He has three minutes before the next meeting and his manager is in the office.
The best channel for this message is:
Solution: Sensitive disagreement should use a synchronous channel — in-person or call — because tone and nuance carry the relationship. Written disagreement reads as colder than spoken. Email may follow later to confirm the agreed direction.
Question 3
In Carl Rogers's active-listening practice, paraphrasing means:
Solution: Paraphrasing restates the message in your own words and invites the speaker to confirm or correct. It is one of four active-listening micro-skills (paraphrasing, open-ended questions, reflection of feeling, summarising). It forces accuracy and signals attention.
Topic 2.3 — Workplace Writing
Question 4
The principle "BLUF" — common in workplace writing — stands for:
Solution: BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front. Workplace readers are busy and scan. Put the request or conclusion in the first sentence; context in the next two or three; details below. A subject line that names the action needed makes the message scannable in seconds.
Question 5
Subject line options for asking your supervisor to confirm pricing on cells C12–C18 of a Q2 budget draft by Thursday at 5 pm:
Which subject line is most effective?
Solution: Option C names the action needed, the deadline, and the specific scope. The other options force the receiver to open the email and read to discover what is being asked. A workplace subject line should let the receiver decide priority without opening the message.
Topic 2.4 — Teamwork (Tuckman, Belbin)
Question 6
Tuckman's stages of group development are, in order:
Solution: Forming (polite, getting acquainted), Storming (conflict surfaces), Norming (the team agrees on how to work together), Performing (best work happens), Adjourning (the team disbands). Most school groups stall at Forming or Storming because they never explicitly negotiate norms.
Question 7
Adam's group has met four times. Everyone is friendly. Nobody is doing the work. Meetings end with vague "we'll figure it out later." The deadline is in two weeks.
Through Tuckman, what is happening?
Solution: Confusing friendliness with effectiveness is a classic Forming-stage trap. The group needs an explicit Norming move: agree in the next meeting on deliverables, individual tasks, deadlines, and how to flag delays. Without that, Storming or Performing never arrives.
Question 8
In Belbin's framework, a strong team:
Solution: Belbin's central insight is that diverse-role teams outperform homogeneous ones. Five Shapers fight; five Teamworkers are friendly and produce little. A balanced mix of action-oriented (Shaper, Implementer, Completer-Finisher), people-oriented (Coordinator, Teamworker, Resource Investigator), and thought-oriented (Plant, Monitor-Evaluator, Specialist) roles produces the best results.
Topic 2.5 — Feedback & Conflict
Question 9
The SBI framework for giving constructive feedback stands for:
Solution: Situation, Behaviour, Impact. Name when/where, describe the specific behaviour, explain the consequence. "In yesterday's meeting (S), when you presented our team's data without naming Maya's contribution (B), it felt like our shared work was being claimed (I)." Targets behaviour, not identity.
Question 10
In Thomas-Kilmann's five conflict styles, the style that takes the most time but produces the best long-term outcomes for ongoing relationships is:
Solution: Collaborating (high concern for own + high concern for other) takes the most time because it surfaces underlying interests behind stated positions. In ongoing workplace relationships, the time cost is more than repaid by better solutions and preserved trust. Competing and Avoiding suit specific situations but corrode relationships over time.
Topic 2.6 — Professionalism & Ethics
Question 11
Under the Ontario Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA), workers have three rights. They are:
Solution: OHSA gives workers three rights: right to know (about workplace hazards and how to handle them), right to participate (in joint health-and-safety committees), and right to refuse unsafe work. Knowing these is one of the most important Strand-B competencies for any Grade 11 student who works or will work in Ontario.
Question 12
Tomás's restaurant supervisor tells him to claim falsely that the chicken is locally sourced because customers tip better.
Applying the four-way ethical test (legal? policy-consistent? fair? publicly defensible?), Tomás should:
Solution: The instruction fails three of the four ethical tests (potentially misleading under consumer-protection law, contradicts implicit menu claims, deceives customers, not publicly defensible). The professional move is to decline politely, ask in writing, and escalate if pressure continues. Long-term reputation matters more than short-term tip pressure.

Q13. In 100–150 words, explain Workplace Communication in your own words.

Pay attention to clarity, organisation of ideas, use of discipline-specific vocabulary, and grammatical conventions. This question evaluates the Communication category of Ontario's Achievement Chart (25%). There is no single correct answer — your teacher will assess your response against the rubric below.

🔒 Communication Rubric (instructor-graded)
Level 1 — Limited (50–59%): Ideas expressed with limited clarity; frequent errors in conventions; little discipline-specific vocabulary.
Level 2 — Some (60–69%): Ideas expressed with some clarity; some errors that occasionally impede meaning; emerging discipline-specific vocabulary.
Level 3 — Considerable (70–79%) [Provincial Standard]: Ideas expressed clearly and organised effectively; few errors; consistent use of discipline-specific vocabulary.
Level 4 — Thorough (80–100%): Ideas expressed with clarity and precision; virtually error-free; precise and varied use of discipline-specific vocabulary; sense of voice / audience awareness.
