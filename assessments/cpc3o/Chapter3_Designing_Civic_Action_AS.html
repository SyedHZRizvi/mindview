Topic 3.1 — SMART Goals
Question 1
"SMART" in goal-setting stands for:
Solution: The SMART framework (Doran, 1981) converts vague aspirations into workable goals by requiring specificity, measurability, achievability, relevance, and a deadline.
Question 2
A "theory of change" is best described as:
Solution: A theory of change makes explicit the chain of reasoning by which the campaign's activities are expected to produce its impact. Without one, even SMART goals can produce activity without progress. The discipline is to test each "if-then" step.
Topic 3.2 — Audience & Message
Question 3
The "three Cs" of effective civic-action messaging are:
Solution: Clear messages can be summarised in one sentence; credible messages are supported by evidence the audience will recognise; concrete messages name what you want to happen. Vague messages produce vague responses.
Question 4
"Framing" in civic communication refers to:
Solution: Framing is the choice of which lens (safety, health, rights, economics, fiscal, identity) to present an issue through. The same facts framed differently activate different audiences. Lakoff's Don't Think of an Elephant popularised the insight that framing is unavoidable political work.
Topic 3.3 — Ethics & Inclusion
Question 5
"Nothing About Us, Without Us" originated as a slogan in:
Solution: The disability-rights movement popularised the phrase from the 1990s onward; it is now adopted across equity-driven civic action — Indigenous-led organising, mental-health advocacy, refugee-rights work — as the principle that affected people must co-design rather than merely be consulted.
Question 6
"Harm reduction" in civic-action planning means:
Solution: Harm reduction is the discipline of asking "who could be harmed by this tactic, and how can we reduce the harm while still acting?" Examples: informed consent for using lived testimony; legal observers at direct actions; post-action support for participants.
Topic 3.4 — Measuring Impact
Question 7
In the three-tier measurement model, "outputs" refers to:
Solution: Outputs are activity counts. Outcomes are world-changes that resulted from outputs. Impact is the deeper, longer-term change. Many campaigns over-claim by reporting only outputs and calling that impact.
Question 8
The most honest civic-action evaluation:
Solution: Few campaigns succeed entirely; most produce a mix of wins, partial wins, and unmet goals. Naming both is the discipline that produces credibility and learning. Over-claiming impact erodes future credibility with sophisticated audiences.
Topic 3.5 — Sustaining Engagement
Question 9
"Activist burnout" is best addressed by:
Solution: Burnout is not a sign of insufficient commitment; it is a sign of bad design. Care — pacing, distribution, rest, bonds, permission to step back — is what makes sustained engagement possible. Treating it as soft work is mistaken; care is what allows the hard work to continue.
Question 10
"Infrastructure" for a civic-action group (in the sense Chapter 3.5 uses) refers to:
Solution: Movement infrastructure is what distinguishes a sustained movement from a moment. A campaign that lives only in one organiser's head ends when that organiser moves on; a campaign with shared infrastructure can survive leadership changes.
Topic 3.6 — Contemporary Case Studies
Question 11
The 27 September 2019 Climate Strike in Montreal — part of the global youth climate movement — was estimated at:
Solution: Montreal's 27 September 2019 strike drew an estimated 500,000+ participants — at the time the largest climate march in Canadian history and one of the largest in the global day of action. Greta Thunberg attended and spoke.
Question 12
The Right to Read Inquiry's 2022 report was produced by:
Solution: The Ontario Human Rights Commission's public inquiry, led by Chief Commissioner Patricia DeGuire, examined whether Ontario reading instruction failed students with reading disabilities. Its 2022 report and 157 recommendations led to a 2023 curriculum revision mandating structured-literacy approaches.
