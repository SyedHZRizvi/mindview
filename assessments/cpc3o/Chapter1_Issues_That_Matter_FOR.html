Chapter 1: Identifying Issues That Matter — Diagnostic

Assessment FOR Learning · Prerequisite check · Strands A & B
Not Graded — Teacher Feedback Only
Purpose: Identify gaps in prior politics, civics, and inquiry-skill knowledge before starting Chapter 1. You will receive descriptive feedback, not a grade. Be honest about what is unclear so your teacher can prioritise it.
Question 1 Prereq · Politics
[3 marks]
In your own words, define each term and give one Canadian example: (a) political issue, (b) stakeholder, (c) civic engagement.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

(a) Political issue — a contested public decision affecting a community beyond the individual, shaped by an identifiable power-holder. Example: housing affordability in Toronto. (b) Stakeholder — any person or group whose interests are affected by a decision. Example: tenants, landlords, developers in a rent-control debate. (c) Civic engagement — informed, active participation in civic life. Example: voting, deputation, joining an advocacy organisation. Rubric: 1 mark each — definition + plausible example.

Question 2 Prereq · Government
[3 marks]
Name Canada's three formal levels of government and one major responsibility you associate with each. (You will refine these in the chapter — do your best now.)
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Federal — defence, criminal law, citizenship, currency, immigration (shared). Provincial — health-care delivery, education, highways, property and civil rights. Municipal — water, fire, police, transit, libraries, planning and zoning. Indigenous governments are increasingly recognised as a fourth pillar. Rubric: 1 mark each level with plausible responsibility.

Question 3 Prereq · Rights
[3 marks]
Have you heard of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms before? In one or two sentences, write what you know about it — what it does, when it was created, and one right you think it protects. (No penalty for not knowing — this question maps what you already bring.)
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Sample: The Charter is the part of the Canadian Constitution (Constitution Act, 1982) that protects rights and freedoms against government action. It was added when Canada patriated the Constitution in 1982. Rights it protects include freedom of expression, freedom of religion, the right to vote, equality without discrimination, and security of the person. Rubric: 1 mark for purpose; 1 mark for date (1982); 1 mark for any plausible right.

Question 4 Skills · Inquiry
[4 marks]
A friend says: "I want to do something about climate change for my civics class." Write 3–4 sentences that turn this vague concern into a more focused question your friend could actually research and answer in one school semester.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Sample: "Climate change" is a topic, not a question. To focus, ask: which decision? Which level of government? Whose action would matter? Example refined question: "Should the Toronto District School Board adopt a climate-action policy committing all of its schools to phase out fossil-fuel-powered heating by 2030, and what would the budget implications be?" This is open-ended, bounded (one school board, one policy), contestable, and significant. Rubric: 1 for naming the problem with the original; 1 for the move toward specificity; 1 for a feasible refined question; 1 for explaining why it is better.

Question 5 Skills · Critical Thinking
[4 marks]
"I don't read the news because it's all biased anyway."
A classmate posts this. Without taking a position on whether they should or should not read news, write 4–6 sentences that (a) identify what is reasonable in this view, (b) identify what it gets wrong about source evaluation, and (c) name one practical alternative habit (drawing on lateral reading or the CRAAP test if you have heard of them).
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Sample: It is reasonable to be skeptical that any source is fully neutral; all media carry framing choices. What the view gets wrong is treating "biased" as a binary that justifies disengagement — different sources have different biases, different track records, and different methods, and triangulating across multiple sources (CBC, Globe, local paper, an opposing-perspective source) gives a more accurate picture than reading none of them. A practical habit is lateral reading: when you encounter a claim, leave the source briefly and check what independent sources say about it. Disengaging from news entirely surrenders civic awareness; reading carefully across sources is the skill the course is teaching. Rubric: 1 for steel-manning what is reasonable; 2 for what the view gets wrong; 1 for the practical alternative.

Question 6 Knowledge · Indigenous
[3 marks]
What does "treaty" mean in the Canadian Indigenous–Crown context? Briefly explain how a treaty is different from an ordinary contract. (You will deepen this in the chapter; what do you already know?)
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Sample: A treaty in Canada is a constitutionally protected agreement between Indigenous Nations and the Crown (formerly the British Crown, now Canada). Treaties are recognised under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, are nation-to-nation in character, and persist over time (they are not ordinary commercial contracts). Examples include the Numbered Treaties (across the prairies and northern Ontario), the Robinson Treaties (Ontario), and modern self-government agreements. The Crown owes a "duty to consult" Indigenous Nations on decisions that may affect treaty rights. Rubric: 1 for constitutional/section-35 framing; 1 for nation-to-nation character; 1 for an example or duty-to-consult mention.

Question 7 Reflection
[ungraded]
Which one civic issue do you personally care most about? In two or three sentences, name it, and identify one thing you would want to know about it that you do not yet know. This will help your teacher align Chapter 1 with topics you actually want to investigate.
