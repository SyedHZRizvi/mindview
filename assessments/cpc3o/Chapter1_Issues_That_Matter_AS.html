Topic 1.1 — Political Inquiry & Concepts of Political Thinking
Question 1
Which of the following is the strongest inquiry question for a Grade 11 politics project?
Solution: A strong inquiry question is open-ended (no yes/no), bounded (Ontario, Grade 9 Geography, 2027), contestable (informed people will disagree), and significant (real-world policy stakes). Option B meets all four. A is fact-recall, C is a topic, D is a loaded yes/no.
Question 2
The concept of political thinking most useful for asking "Whose voices and values shape this issue?" is:
Solution: Political perspective is the lens that asks whose voices, beliefs, and values shape an issue. The other three concepts illuminate different aspects: significance (why it matters), objectives/results (intent vs. outcome), stability/change (what held vs. what shifted).
Topic 1.2 — Identifying Political Issues
Question 3
According to the sociological "personal trouble vs. public issue" distinction, an individual difficulty becomes a political issue when it:
Solution: A political issue meets three criteria: contested public decision, broad effect, and identifiable power-holder. Media coverage or scale alone are not sufficient — the structural test is whether a power-holder's decision shapes the outcome.
Question 4
The concept of "issue framing" refers to:
Solution: Framing is the choice of lens (safety, health, rights, economics, identity) through which an issue is presented. The same facts framed differently lead to different perceptions of which power-holders are responsible and which solutions appear reasonable — framing is itself political work.
Topic 1.3 — Stakeholders & Perspectives
Question 5
The discipline of "steel-manning" requires you to:
Solution: Steel-manning is the opposite of "straw-manning." It demonstrates that you have understood the strongest version of the opposing position, which is the prerequisite for any credible analytical engagement with it. Option D describes straw-manning.
Question 6
The constitutional duty to consult Indigenous peoples on Crown decisions affecting their rights was articulated by the Supreme Court of Canada in:
Solution: Haida Nation (2004) established the Crown's duty to meaningfully consult — and where appropriate, accommodate — Indigenous peoples on decisions that may adversely affect their rights. The doctrine was further developed in Tsilhqot'in Nation (2014). The other cases addressed different constitutional questions.
Topic 1.4 — Rights Frameworks
Question 7
A landlord refuses to rent an apartment to a single mother who receives Ontario Works social assistance.
Which legal framework primarily applies?
Solution: The Charter binds government; private parties are bound by provincial human-rights codes. Ontario's Code lists "receipt of public assistance" as a protected ground for housing accommodation specifically (s. 2(1)). The tenant should file a complaint with the Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario.
Question 8
UNDRIP (the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples) became operative in Canadian federal law through:
Solution: Canada endorsed UNDRIP in 2010 (as a non-binding declaration). The 2021 federal Act commits Canada to align federal law with UNDRIP, in consultation with Indigenous peoples. BC passed a parallel provincial Act in 2019. Section 35 protects different (constitutional) rights.
Topic 1.5 — Source Evaluation
Question 9
"Lateral reading" — the source-evaluation discipline that professional fact-checkers use — requires you to:
Solution: Wineburg and McGrew's 2017 Stanford study found that professional fact-checkers leave the source quickly and check what independent sources say about it. Vertical reading (option B) is what students traditionally are taught, and it often deceives because professional-looking websites can be unreliable.
Question 10
A Statistics Canada release of Canadian Income Survey data is best classified as a:
Solution: Statistics Canada is the producer of the underlying survey data; the release is primary. Commentary about the release (e.g., a news article interpreting the numbers) is secondary. Always check primary sources when a viral statistic is claimed.
Topic 1.6 — Issue Mapping
Question 11
The six elements of a complete issue map are:
Solution: The six-element issue map is the discipline that converts a concern into a project. It draws together the previous lessons of Chapter 1 (questioning, stakeholders, decision-makers, rights, evidence) and points forward to Chapters 2 and 3 (candidate actions).
Question 12
"I want the government to do something about youth mental health."
Which step of issue mapping is missing first from this concern?
Solution: "The government" is not a decision-maker. Youth mental health touches federal funding (Canada Health Transfer, Mental Health Commission of Canada), provincial delivery (Ministry of Health, Ministry of Education), municipal public-health units, school boards, and Indigenous Services Canada (for First Nations communities). Naming the specific decision-maker is the next step.
