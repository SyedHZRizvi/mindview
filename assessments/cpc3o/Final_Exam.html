Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[2]A · Inquiry
Name and briefly define the four concepts of political thinking.
Answer Key

(1) Political significance — why an issue matters and for whom. (2) Objectives and results — what political actors set out to do vs. what actually happened. (3) Stability and change — what has held constant vs. what has been transformed. (4) Political perspective — whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue.

2
[1]A · Inquiry
A strong inquiry question must be:
Answer Key

B. These four criteria distinguish a researchable inquiry question from a slogan or a fact-recall query.

3
[2]B · Charter
Name three categories of rights protected by the Charter, with one specific right in each.
Answer Key

Acceptable: Fundamental Freedoms (s. 2 — conscience & religion, expression, assembly, association); Democratic Rights (s. 3–5 — right to vote and run for office); Mobility (s. 6 — entry, exit, internal movement); Legal Rights (s. 7–14 — life/liberty/security of person, right to counsel, presumption of innocence); Equality (s. 15 — protection from discrimination); Language Rights (s. 16–22 — English/French in federal institutions); Minority Language Education (s. 23).

4
[1]B · Charter
The Charter binds:
Answer Key

B. Section 32(1). Private discrimination is addressed under provincial human-rights codes and the Canadian Human Rights Act, not the Charter directly.

5
[1]B · Rights
UNDRIP became operative in Canadian federal law through:
Answer Key

B. Canada endorsed UNDRIP in 2010 as a non-binding declaration; the 2021 federal Act commits Canada to align federal law with UNDRIP, in consultation with Indigenous peoples. BC passed a parallel provincial Act in 2019.

6
[1]B · Government
Education in Canada is constitutionally:
Answer Key

B. Education is provincial under s. 93. School boards are creatures of provincial statute. The federal government has jurisdiction over education on reserves and in the territories.

7
[2]B · Power
Define and distinguish: (a) coercive power, (b) persuasive power, (c) soft power.
Answer Key

(a) Coercive — capacity to compel through force or threat of force (state monopoly per Weber). (b) Persuasive — capacity to shape behaviour through incentives short of force (regulation, tax, contracts). (c) Soft — capacity to shape what people want through culture, narrative, and perceived legitimacy.

8
[1]C · Engagement
A federal e-petition under Standing Order 36 requires:
Answer Key

B. Paper petitions to the House of Commons require 25 signatures; e-petitions require 500.

9
[1]C · Charter
The right to strike was constitutionally recognised in:
Answer Key

B. SFL strengthened freedom of association under s. 2(d) to include the right to strike.

10
[2]C · Movements
For each, identify one civic contribution: (a) the Famous Five; (b) Tommy Douglas; (c) Viola Desmond; (d) Idle No More.
Answer Key

(a) Famous Five — won the 1929 Persons Case at the JCPC; articulated the "living tree" doctrine. (b) Tommy Douglas — universal hospital insurance in Saskatchewan (1947); medicare (1962); federal Medical Care Act (1966) followed. (c) Viola Desmond — 1946 anti-segregation challenge in New Glasgow, NS; first non-royal Canadian woman on a banknote (2018). (d) Idle No More — 2012 Indigenous-led movement responding to federal Bill C-45; contributed to the federal endorsement and 2021 legislative implementation of UNDRIP.

11
[1]C · Movements
The Right to Read Inquiry (2020–2022) was produced by:
Answer Key

B. OHRC public inquiry led by Chief Commissioner Patricia DeGuire; 2022 report with 157 recommendations led to a 2023 curriculum revision mandating structured-literacy approaches.

12
[1]A · Sources
"Lateral reading" is the source-evaluation discipline of:
Answer Key

B. Wineburg & McGrew (Stanford, 2017) found that professional fact-checkers leave sources quickly and check them against independent corroboration.

13
[2]A · Sources
Define and distinguish: misinformation, disinformation, and propaganda.
Answer Key

Misinformation — false information shared without intent to deceive. Disinformation — deliberately false information designed to deceive. Propaganda — information (true, false, or selectively framed) produced to advance a political agenda.

14
[1]C · Engagement
A 5-minute formal address to a municipal council on an item on the agenda is called a:
Answer Key

B. Deputations are typically registered in advance and follow the council's procedural rules.

15
[2]C · Action
Name and briefly define the five elements of a SMART goal.
Answer Key

Specific (what/where/who); Measurable (observable indicator); Achievable (within available resources/time); Relevant (tied to issue and theory of change); Time-bound (deadline).

16
[2]C · Measurement
Distinguish between outputs, outcomes, and impact in civic-action measurement. Give one example of each.
Answer Key

Outputs — activity counts (e.g., 800 signatures; 12 deputations). Outcomes — changes in the world that resulted from activity (e.g., council motion brought forward; provincial regulation revised). Impact — deeper underlying change ultimately desired (e.g., fewer evictions; broader access to mental-health programs).

17
[2]C · Ethics
"Nothing About Us, Without Us" — explain the principle in one sentence and identify the movement it originated in.
Answer Key

The principle that policies and campaigns affecting a community must be designed with that community as co-designers, not just for them or in consultation with them after the fact. Originated in the disability-rights movement and is now applied across equity-driven civic action (Indigenous-led organising, mental-health advocacy, refugee-rights work, climate justice).

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
18
[8]B + A · Case Analysis
In December 2012, the federal government passed omnibus Bill C-45, which made changes to the Navigation Protection Act (removing protection from many waterways), the Indian Act (changes to land-designation procedures), and various environmental statutes. In response, Idle No More — a movement led by four Indigenous women in Saskatoon — used round dances in shopping malls and public spaces, social-media organising (#IdleNoMore), a hunger strike by Chief Theresa Spence on Victoria Island in Ottawa, and a national day of action in December 2012.
Apply the four concepts of political thinking to Idle No More in a structured response. Address (i) political significance, (ii) objectives and results (so far as known), (iii) stability and change, (iv) political perspective. Aim for 10–14 sentences.
Answer Key

Significance: first major Indigenous-led grassroots movement of the social-media era in Canada; re-centred Indigenous voices in Canadian political discourse; built a generation of Indigenous and allied organisers whose work continued through Wet'suwet'en, residential-schools accountability, and the 2021 UNDRIP Act. Objectives and results: stated objective — block or modify Bill C-45 specifically — not directly met; the bill passed and the specific provisions were not reversed. Broader objective — change the political conversation, build movement infrastructure, contribute to longer-term Indigenous-rights advances — substantially met. Stability and change: stability in the Indian Act framework and federal-Indigenous status quo on many matters; change in public consciousness (the federal endorsement of UNDRIP in 2010 was reinforced by movement pressure; the 2021 federal Act, the BC 2019 Act, and the TRC implementation work all sit on infrastructure Idle No More helped build); change in how subsequent Indigenous-led campaigns are organised. Political perspective: Indigenous Nations (lead voice, with the four founders and many subsequent organisers); the federal Crown (target); settler Canadians (audience for political education); conservative critics (challenged the movement's tactics, particularly Chief Spence's hunger strike); the international Indigenous-rights community (allies); other social-justice organisations including environmental groups, who built durable coalitions with Indigenous land-defenders. Rubric: 2 each concept substantively applied; total 8.

19
[6]A · Inquiry
Construct a political-inquiry plan to investigate: "What civic-action steps should Ontario take to address mental-health gaps for students in secondary schools?" Walk through all five stages of the political inquiry process: (1) refine the question with one or two sub-questions; (2) name three credible sources of different genre you would consult; (3) name one analytical tool (cause-and-effect chain, stakeholder matrix, comparison table) and what it would compare; (4) name the strongest counterargument; (5) name the form (deputation, op-ed, infographic, briefing note) and audience for your final communication.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 for question refinement with sub-questions (e.g., "What is the current state of school mental-health programming? What works in comparable jurisdictions?"); 2 for three credible sources of different genre — primary (Statistics Canada Canadian Community Health Survey – Mental Health), secondary (academic research from CAMH or the Mental Health Commission of Canada), advocacy (Jack.org, Children's Mental Health Ontario), with reasoning for credibility of each; 1 analytical tool with specific application (stakeholder matrix comparing parents, students, teachers, school administrators, Ministry of Education, public-health units, and external partners; or comparison table of programs in Quebec, BC, and Ontario); 1 steel-manned counterargument (e.g., "school is for academic learning, not health services" — addressed by noting that schools already provide many non-academic services and that learning is impaired by untreated mental-health issues); 1 form-and-audience (briefing note to Ministry; op-ed for Toronto Star or Globe; deputation to Standing Committee on Social Policy).

20
[5]A · Source Evaluation
A viral social-media graphic claims: "STATSCAN: Crime in Canadian cities up 80% in 5 years."
Apply the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) with one specific lateral-reading question for each criterion. Conclude whether you would share the graphic and why.
Answer Key

Currency: what 5-year window does this cover, and was the underlying data release recent? Relevance: "crime" is broad — is this violent crime, property crime, or aggregate Crime Severity Index? Headline numbers often hide composition. Authority: what specific StatCan release is referenced, and can it be located on statcan.gc.ca? Or is the graphic citing StatCan in name only? Accuracy: 80% in 5 years is implausibly high for any major crime category in Canada — the Crime Severity Index rose roughly 8–15% over 2017–2022, not 80%. The number likely misrepresents or fabricates the underlying figure. Purpose: what is the goal of the post — to inform, to amplify a "crime is rising" narrative, to drive engagement? Conclusion: do not share until verified against the primary StatCan release. The number is almost certainly misrepresented or fabricated. Rubric: 1 each criterion + 0 sharing decision justified.

21
[3]B · Federalism
For each issue, identify the level(s) of government primarily responsible and explain briefly: (a) regulating cryptocurrency exchanges in Canada; (b) the operation of the local public library; (c) the curriculum used in your high school.
Answer Key

(a) Federal — financial-system regulation falls under federal jurisdiction (s. 91 — currency, banking, trade and commerce); the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre (FINTRAC) regulates anti-money-laundering for crypto businesses; the Canadian Securities Administrators (provincial securities regulators) coordinate on securities-aspect crypto regulation. (b) Municipal — public libraries are run by municipalities, with funding from municipal property tax, provincial transfers, and federal grants for some programs. (c) Provincial — Ontario Ministry of Education sets curriculum; school boards (creatures of provincial statute) implement. Rubric: 1 mark each.

22
[3]A + C
Explain why outputs, outcomes, and impact cannot be reduced to a single number, and give one practical example of why this matters for evaluating a Grade 11 civic-action project.
Answer Key

The three tiers measure different things at different timescales. Outputs (signatures, attendees) are easy to count but inflate easily and don't capture impact. Outcomes (a council motion brought forward) require attribution analysis (your campaign was one cause among many). Impact (lower greenhouse-gas emissions, fewer evictions, broader access) takes years and may never be cleanly attributable to a single campaign. Practical example: a Grade 11 project that collects 312 signatures and produces a council motion to study the proposal cannot honestly claim to have "reduced student mental-health crises" — that is impact, which depends on what happens after the study (often years). The discipline is to claim what you can demonstrate and to be honest about what you can't. Rubric: 1 distinction; 1 example; 1 honest evaluation insight.

[8]A · Communication
Write a 600–800 word op-ed for a Canadian newspaper on a civic issue of your choice. Follow op-ed conventions: hook (vivid first sentence), state your specific ask within the first three paragraphs, give two to three argumentative paragraphs with evidence, address one strong counterargument with a steel-man, close with a memorable sentence. 26
[2]A · Communication
Write a strong inquiry question for the topic "AI in schools." Apply the four criteria: open-ended, bounded, contestable, significant.
Answer Key

Sample: "Should the Ontario Ministry of Education require all secondary schools to develop a board-approved AI-acceptable-use policy by September 2027, and what should that policy include for student work, equity considerations, and accessibility?" Open-ended (no yes/no), bounded (Ontario, secondary, 2027), contestable (parents, teachers, ed-tech leaders, disability-rights groups will disagree), significant (real-world policy stakes for students and teachers). Weak versions: "Is AI bad?" (yes/no, not contestable) or "What is AI?" (fact-recall).

27
[2]C · Communication
In 3–4 sentences, explain to a Grade 9 student what an "inside" tool and an "outside" tool of civic action are, and why successful campaigns usually use both.
Answer Key

Sample: "Inside" tools work within the official decision-making process — petitions, deputations, formal consultation submissions, lobbying, voting. "Outside" tools work outside the process to put pressure on it — protests, rallies, social-media campaigns, boycotts, civil disobedience. Inside tools tend to be slower but more legitimate in the eyes of decision-makers; outside tools are faster at producing visibility and political pressure. Successful campaigns usually combine both — the outside pressure builds public legitimacy that makes the inside engagement credible; the inside engagement gives the politician a face-saving way to act on what the outside pressure surfaced. Rubric: 1 inside; 1 outside.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
28
[12]C · Capstone Civic-Action Plan
Capstone Application: Design a complete civic-action plan for a real local, national, or global civic issue. Your plan must answer all eight framework questions: (1) What is the issue (specific, bounded)? (2) Who is affected and how (brief stakeholder map)? (3) Whose decision is this (named decision-maker at the correct level)? (4) What evidence do we have (at least three sources, of different genre)? (5) What is our SMART goal (one sentence, all five elements)? (6) Two inside tools and two outside tools, with reasoning? (7) Two output measures and one outcome measure? (8) One ethical commitment (especially around "Nothing About Us, Without Us") and one reflection paragraph on the strongest counter-argument with how you would respond.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 each — issue bounded, stakeholders, decision-maker, evidence (3 of different genre), SMART goal, two inside tools, two outside tools, output measures, outcome measure, ethical commitment with named affected-community engagement, counter-argument response. 1 for overall coherence (the goal matches the issue, the tools match the decision-maker, the measurement matches the goal). Top-band plans show internal coherence and demonstrate that the issue is bounded, the decision-maker is correctly identified, the ask is specific and measurable, and the planner has engaged the strongest opposing view honestly. Adjust to 12 if all elements substantively addressed.

29
[7]B + C · Indigenous-Crown
In Spring 2026, your school board is considering a policy committing all secondary schools to operationalise the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 62(i): "Make age-appropriate curriculum on residential schools, Treaties, and Aboriginal peoples' historical and contemporary contributions to Canada a mandatory education requirement for Kindergarten to Grade Twelve students."
Apply what you have learned in 8–12 sentences: (a) identify two democratic values supporting adoption of the policy; (b) name one constitutional or international source (s. 35, UNDRIP, the TRC report itself) that strengthens the case; (c) identify one practical barrier the board must address (teacher training, curriculum materials, community partnership); (d) name one community partner the board should engage before deciding; (e) state your own conclusion on whether the board should adopt the policy, with reasoning that integrates the four concepts of political thinking.
Answer Key

Sample marking guide. (a) Reconciliation; equity (Indigenous students see their history, non-Indigenous students learn truthful history); the common good; inclusion. (b) Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognising Aboriginal and treaty rights; UNDRIP (Article 14 — Indigenous-controlled education; Article 15 — accurate representation in education; the federal 2021 UNDRIP Act); the TRC report itself, with Call 62(i) explicitly named. (c) Teacher training (most current Ontario teachers were not taught Indigenous history themselves); resourcing of age-appropriate texts; sustained community partnership beyond a single launch event. (d) Local First Nation, Métis Nation, or Inuit organisation; the board's Indigenous Education Advisory Committee; an Elder-in-residence partnership. (e) Conclusion should integrate political-thinking concepts: significance (TRC was a national reconciliation event; this is a binding response); objectives/results (the call's intent is structural education change; results require sustained implementation); stability/change (continuing the structural exclusion of Indigenous knowledge vs. transformative education shift); perspective (Indigenous Nations and educators lead voice; settler-Canadian education system as partner not author). Rubric: 1 (a); 1 (b); 1 (c); 1 (d); 3 well-reasoned conclusion integrating political-thinking concepts.

30
[6]A + B + C · Final Reflection
Synthesis: Reflect on what civic engagement means to you after completing CPC3O. In 8–12 sentences, identify: (a) one civic skill you developed in this course that you will use after the course ends; (b) one civic issue you commit to follow over the next year, and the specific way you will follow it; (c) one type of civic action you will personally take in the next six months; (d) one lesson from a Canadian case study (Famous Five, Tommy Douglas, Viola Desmond, Idle No More, Right to Read, youth climate movements) that you will carry into that action. Your answer should integrate language from the curriculum (democratic values, political inquiry process, the four concepts of political thinking) and demonstrate that civic engagement is a habit you have begun.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 each (a)–(d) substantively answered; 1 for integration of curriculum vocabulary and concepts; 1 for evidence that civic engagement is a habit, not an assignment. This is the capstone reflection — strong answers show that the course produced a habit (not just a grade), name specific issues and actions, and connect the past (case studies), the present (course skills), and the future (committed action). Adjust to 6 if all elements present.

📝 Communication Category Rubric (instructor-graded)

Per Ontario's Growing Success (2010) Achievement Chart, Communication is one of the four equally-weighted categories (25% of the course mark). This rubric is applied to every student-written response (short-answer, extended-response, essay, lab report, deputation, etc.) on this assessment.

Criterion Level 1 — Limited (50–59%) Level 2 — Some (60–69%) Level 3 — Considerable (70–79%) Level 4 — Thorough (80–100%)
Expression and organisation of ideas Ideas expressed with limited clarity; little organisation. Ideas expressed with some clarity; some organisation. Ideas expressed clearly and organised effectively. Ideas expressed with clarity, precision, and a high degree of organisation.
Communication for different audiences and purposes Limited awareness of audience/purpose; tone often inappropriate. Some awareness of audience/purpose; tone occasionally inappropriate. Consistently appropriate tone, register, and address to audience. Tone, register, voice, and address are precisely matched to audience and purpose.
Use of conventions, vocabulary, terminology Frequent errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation; little discipline-specific vocabulary. Some errors that occasionally impede meaning; emerging use of discipline-specific vocabulary. Few errors; consistent use of discipline-specific vocabulary. Virtually error-free; precise and varied use of discipline-specific vocabulary.

Level 3 represents the Provincial Standard. Use the most-recent and most-consistent evidence across multiple Communication tasks (this assessment, prior FOR work, written responses on AS quizzes, oral presentations) when assigning a course-level Communication mark. Growing Success, 2010, p. 17.
