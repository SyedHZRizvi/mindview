Chapter 2: Tools of Civic Engagement — Diagnostic

Assessment FOR Learning · Prerequisite check · Strands B & C
Not Graded — Teacher Feedback Only
Purpose: Identify gaps in your prior knowledge of power, decision-making, and tools of civic engagement before starting Chapter 2. You will receive descriptive feedback, not a grade.
Question 1 Prereq · Power
[3 marks]
In your own words, give one example each of: (a) a form of coercive power; (b) a form of persuasive (economic) power; (c) a form of soft (cultural) power. Use Canadian examples where you can.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Sample: (a) Coercive — a police officer arrests someone; a court orders a fine; deportation under immigration law. (b) Persuasive — Cabinet uses the federal carbon-tax to incentivise emission reductions; a corporation raises wages to attract workers; Statistics Canada publishes data that shapes policy attention. (c) Soft — the Famous Five "living tree" framing changed how Canadians understand the Constitution; an Indigenous land-acknowledgement at the start of public events changes the cultural frame for what follows. Rubric: 1 each form with a plausible example.

Question 2 Prereq · Government
[3 marks]
For each of these issues, name the level of government that has primary decision-making authority and one reason: (a) the speed limit on the road in front of your house; (b) the curriculum used in your school; (c) the federal carbon tax.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

(a) Provincial (Ontario Highway Traffic Act) for arterial roads; municipal for local streets within a provincial framework. (b) Provincial — Ontario Ministry of Education sets curriculum; school boards implement. (c) Federal — Parliament passed the Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act under its s. 91 jurisdiction (constitutionally upheld in References re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act, 2021). Rubric: 1 mark each.

Question 3 Prereq · Engagement
[3 marks]
Name three different ways a Canadian citizen could try to influence a public decision (besides voting in elections). Briefly describe each.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Acceptable: deputation to a council or board; petition (paper or e-petition to Parliament); letter to MP/MPP/councillor; op-ed or letter to the editor; participation in formal consultation; protest or rally; boycott; joining or starting an advocacy organisation; running for office; testifying before a standing committee. Rubric: 1 mark each — name + brief description.

Question 4 Skills · Critical Thinking
[4 marks]
"Protests never change anything. The 2019 climate strikes were huge and emissions are still going up."
A classmate says this. Without taking a position on the broader question, write 4–6 sentences identifying (a) what is reasonable in the claim, (b) what it gets wrong about how civic action actually produces change, and (c) one piece of evidence (Canadian if you can) that complicates the picture.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Sample: What is reasonable: large-scale political change rarely happens in a single news cycle, and the 2019 climate strikes did not produce immediate emissions reductions. What it gets wrong: civic action produces multiple kinds of effects, and short-term emissions are not the only measure. Soft-power effects — shifting the political acceptability of climate action — and policy effects — the 2019 climate-emergency declarations by the federal government and over 500 Canadian municipalities — count as changes even though emissions take longer. Evidence that complicates the picture: medicare in Canada took over 20 years from Tommy Douglas's first Saskatchewan hospital insurance (1947) to federal medicare (1966) — change is real but slow. Idle No More's tactical objectives in 2012 (blocking Bill C-45) were not met, but the movement contributed to the federal endorsement and 2021 legislative implementation of UNDRIP. Rubric: 1 steel-man; 2 critique; 1 evidence.

Question 5 Prereq · Charter
[3 marks]
Which Charter sections protect the right to protest? Briefly describe what each does.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Section 2(b) — freedom of expression — protects the speech act of protest. Section 2(c) — freedom of peaceful assembly — protects the right to gather in public space for political purposes. Section 2(d) — freedom of association — protects the right to organise, including in unions and movements. All three are subject to s. 1 reasonable limits, and protests that are not peaceful or that violate other laws (criminal trespass, mischief) may lose Charter protection. Rubric: 1 mark each section.

Question 6 Knowledge · Movements
[3 marks]
Name one Canadian social or political movement you have heard of (historical or current) and briefly describe what it was trying to change and one tactic it used.
Sample Answer & Marking Rubric

Acceptable: Famous Five (Persons Case, JCPC litigation); medicare (Saskatchewan CCF / Tommy Douglas, provincial legislation); same-sex marriage (legal challenges + advocacy + opinion shift); Idle No More (Indigenous-led, round dances + hunger strike + social media); youth climate strikes (mass mobilisation); #MeToo (hashtag-aggregated lived testimony); Right to Read Inquiry (parental advocacy → formal HRC inquiry); Wet'suwet'en land defence (encampment + legal challenges). Rubric: 1 movement; 1 objective; 1 tactic.

Question 7 Reflection
[ungraded]
Which form of civic action are you most curious about: (a) inside tools like petitioning and lobbying, (b) outside tools like protest and civil disobedience, (c) digital tools like social-media campaigns, (d) electoral politics, (e) something else? Briefly explain.
