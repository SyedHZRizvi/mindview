Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
Name the four concepts of political thinking and briefly define each.
Answer Key

(1) Political significance — why an issue matters and for whom. (2) Objectives and results — what political actors set out to do vs. what actually happened. (3) Stability and change — what has held constant and what has been transformed. (4) Political perspective — whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue. Rubric: 0.5 each.

2
[2]
In one sentence, distinguish: (a) misinformation, (b) disinformation, (c) propaganda.
Answer Key

(a) Misinformation — false content shared without intent to deceive. (b) Disinformation — deliberately false content designed to deceive. (c) Propaganda — content (true, false, or selectively framed) produced to advance a political agenda. Rubric: 2/3 for two correct.

3
[2]
The Charter of Rights and Freedoms binds:
Answer Key

B. Under s. 32(1), the Charter applies to Parliament, the legislatures, and the executive at every level. Private parties are bound by provincial human-rights codes and the Canadian Human Rights Act, not the Charter directly.

4
[2]
Define the following: (a) UDHR, (b) UNDRIP, (c) section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
Answer Key

(a) UDHR — Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), foundational UN declaration; not directly enforceable in Canadian courts but shapes interpretation. (b) UNDRIP — UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007); endorsed by Canada in 2010, implemented via federal legislation in 2021. (c) Section 35 — constitutional recognition of "existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada"; s. 35(2) explicitly includes First Nations, Métis, and Inuit.

5
[2]
Lateral reading is best described as:
Answer Key

B. The discipline of professional fact-checkers (Wineburg & McGrew, 2017). Opens new tabs, checks the source against independent corroboration before accepting any claim.

6
[3]
Name the six elements of a complete issue map and briefly explain why each is necessary.
Answer Key

(1) Issue as question — converts a concern into something an inquiry can answer. (2) Stakeholders — identifies who is affected, organising the political picture. (3) Decision-maker — names the specific power-holder whose decision matters; vague "the government" fails. (4) Rights/frameworks — anchors the issue in entitlements the audience may recognise. (5) Evidence base — supports the claims and shows triangulation. (6) Candidate actions — points to civic engagement (Chapters 2 and 3). Rubric: 0.5 each.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
7
[5]
A school district is considering a policy banning the use of generative-AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot) in any student work, with mandatory zero-credit penalties for detected use.
Apply all four concepts of political thinking to this case in a structured response (8–12 sentences total). Identify which stakeholders should be at the table, and name one Charter or Code right that might be engaged.
Answer Key

Significance: first wave of district-level decisions on a rapidly evolving technology; precedent for how schools handle AI more broadly; high stakes for students. Objectives and results: stated objective — academic integrity; possible results — disparate impact on students with disabilities for whom AI is an accommodation, students from low-resource homes where AI tutoring substitutes for paid tutoring, and risk of detection-tool false positives. Stability and change: stability — existing academic-integrity frameworks; change — the underlying technology changes monthly and policy may not keep up. Political perspective: students (especially those using AI for accommodation), teachers (workload of detection), administrators (consistency), parents (mixed), AI literacy advocates. Stakeholders at the table: student council, IEP coordinator, librarians, ed-tech leads, equity officer. Rights/frameworks: Ontario Human Rights Code (disability accommodation under s. 5); Charter s. 7 (procedural fairness in discipline) for the zero-credit penalty. Rubric: 1 each concept; 1 for stakeholder identification with equity awareness.

8
[4]
Identify two ways a vague concern ("I care about the environment") fails the criteria for a strong inquiry question. Then rewrite it as one strong inquiry question, and identify which of the four criteria (open-ended, bounded, contestable, significant) your rewrite satisfies and how.
Answer Key

"I care about the environment" is a feeling, not a question. It fails: (1) open-ended — has no claim to argue (it is a statement); (2) bounded — names no scope (which environment? whose action? what timeframe?); (3) contestable — there is nothing to disagree with; (4) significant — too diffuse to track. Rewrite example: "Should the Region of Waterloo require all new municipal infrastructure projects over $5 million to include a climate-resilience impact assessment by 2028, and what should that assessment include?" Open-ended (no yes/no), bounded (one region, one threshold, 2028), contestable (engineers and budget officials will disagree), significant (real procurement consequences). Rubric: 2 for diagnosis; 1 for rewrite; 1 for criteria mapping.

9
[4]
A graphic posted on social media claims: "Stats Canada: Cost of groceries up 47% in 2 years."
Apply the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) — one specific lateral-reading question for each criterion. Conclude whether you would share the graphic.
Answer Key

Currency: when was the underlying release? Is "2 years" 2023–2025, 2022–2024, or some other window? Relevance: is the 47% for all groceries or for a specific category (e.g., dairy or oils, which often have larger swings)? Headlines often hide composition. Authority: can the underlying Statistics Canada release be located on statcan.gc.ca? Which CPI-component is referenced? Accuracy: does StatCan's actual Food CPI show 47% over the cited window? (Realistically, Food CPI rose roughly 18–22% over 2021–2023, not 47%, which suggests the graphic is wrong.) Purpose: what is the goal of the post — to inform, to mobilise outrage, to drive engagement? Conclusion: do not share until verified against the primary StatCan release. The number appears implausibly high; cite the actual figure if used. Rubric: 1 each criterion.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences) explaining the difference between the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and the Ontario Human Rights Code. Use one concrete Canadian example.
Answer Key

Sample: The Charter and the Human Rights Code both protect rights, but they apply to different actors and operate through different processes. The Charter (Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982) binds governments — federal, provincial, territorial, and municipal — and is enforced through court challenges that can strike down legislation. The Ontario Human Rights Code applies to private parties (employers, landlords, service providers) and is enforced through complaints to the Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario. For example, if a Toronto police service refuses to investigate a hate crime against an LGBTQ+ resident, that engages the Charter (police are government); if a private restaurant refuses to seat an LGBTQ+ couple, that engages the Ontario Human Rights Code (the protected ground of sexual orientation in services). Both frameworks may overlap on some issues, but the Charter–Code distinction is the first question to ask. Rubric: 1 Charter definition; 1 Code definition; 1 example; 1 conventions.

11
[4]
In a paragraph (4–6 sentences), explain to a Grade 9 student what an "inquiry question" is in CPC3O and why it matters. Give one example of a weak inquiry question and one example of a strong one.
Answer Key

Sample: An inquiry question is the question that focuses an investigation in politics. A weak inquiry question like "Is climate change bad?" cannot really be answered — it is a yes/no with an obvious answer to most readers — and it doesn't point to any decision-maker or action. A strong inquiry question is open-ended, bounded, contestable, and significant: "Should the Ontario Ministry of Education make climate-adaptation a mandatory expectation in the Grade 9 Geography curriculum by 2027, and what content should be required?" The strong version names a specific decision-maker, a specific timeframe, a defined scope, and gives the inquiry something to actually investigate. The reason it matters is simple: without a focused question, research produces noise; with one, it produces a useful answer. Rubric: 1 for definition; 1 weak example with explanation; 1 strong example with explanation; 1 for conventions.

12
[4]
Write a 4–6 sentence explanation of why "Nothing About Us, Without Us" matters in civic action. Use at least one concrete example (disability-rights, Indigenous-led, youth-led, or another).
Answer Key

Sample: "Nothing About Us, Without Us" — originally a disability-rights slogan, now widely adopted across equity-driven civic action — insists that policies and programs affecting a community must be designed with that community, not just for them. For example, when the Ontario Human Rights Commission ran the Right to Read Inquiry into reading instruction for students with disabilities (2020–2022), parents and disability-rights organisations led the campaign that triggered the inquiry; their lived expertise shaped the recommendations. The practical reasons are that affected people know the issue better than outsiders, that solutions designed without them tend to fail in implementation, and that legitimacy depends on consent. The deeper reason is that civic action that bypasses affected people often reproduces the same exclusions that produced the issue. Rubric: 1 for principle definition; 2 for example; 1 for analysis of why it matters.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[6]
A coalition of Grade 11 students is concerned that homeless encampments in their downtown core are being routinely cleared by police, often without notice and sometimes during winter storms.
Apply the six-element issue mapping framework. Produce a one-paragraph issue map covering: (1) the issue as a question, (2) the affected stakeholders, (3) the responsible decision-maker(s), (4) the rights and frameworks engaged, (5) the evidence base they would need, (6) two candidate civic actions.
Answer Key

Sample: (1) Issue as question: Should our city require notice, alternative shelter, and adverse-weather suspension before any encampment clearing, and what should the policy say? (2) Stakeholders: people living in encampments; neighbouring residents; downtown businesses; outreach workers; police; municipal social services; Indigenous outreach organisations (Indigenous people are overrepresented). (3) Decision-maker: the City Council (bylaw on parks/public-spaces); the police-services board (operational practice); the local public-health unit (cold-weather alerts). (4) Rights: Charter s. 7 security of the person (Abbotsford v Shantz, 2015 — BC Supreme Court found a bylaw preventing overnight sheltering in public spaces violated s. 7); Ontario Human Rights Code s. 6 housing protections; UNDRIP for Indigenous homeless residents. (5) Evidence base: city encampment-clearing protocols; police data on number of clearings; Statistics Canada Point-in-Time homeless count; provincial coroner's reports on cold-weather deaths; comparison cases (Hamilton, Kitchener, Halifax). (6) Candidate actions: deputation to City Council demanding policy revision; partnership with an outreach organisation already engaged with affected residents; op-ed in the local paper. Rubric: 1 each element substantively addressed.

14
[6]
Apply the Political Inquiry framework: choose one issue you are interested in (it may be the one from Question 13 or a different one). Write a 6–8 sentence response identifying (a) one strong inquiry question, (b) three credible sources you would consult (with brief reasoning for why each is credible), (c) the strongest counter-argument to your tentative position, and (d) one ethical concern you would attend to in your inquiry.
Answer Key

Marking guide: 1 for a well-formed inquiry question (open-ended, bounded, contestable, significant); 2 for three credible sources, each with reasoning (e.g., a Statistics Canada release for empirical grounding; an academic journal article for peer-reviewed analysis; an advocacy organisation's brief for the perspective of affected people); 1 for a steel-manned counter-argument; 1 for an ethical concern with named mitigation (consent for using lived testimony; not reproducing the exclusion that produced the issue; harm reduction for vulnerable participants). Strong answers integrate the elements coherently rather than treating them as a checklist.
