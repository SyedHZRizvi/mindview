Topic 2.1 — Forms of Power
Question 1
Joseph Nye's concept of "soft power" refers to the capacity to:
Solution: Soft power (Nye, 1990) is the capacity to shape preferences through attraction and legitimacy — culture, ideas, perceived moral authority. Hard power compels through force or economic pressure; soft power persuades through appeal. Movements that change cultural framing exercise soft power.
Question 2
Max Weber defined the state as:
Solution: Weber's definition emphasises that the state's defining feature is its claim to legitimate coercive power — a claim that depends on public acceptance (legitimacy). Other actors (corporations, religions, families) exercise power, but only the state lawfully coerces.
Topic 2.2 — Levels of Government & Decisions
Question 3
Setting Ontario high-school graduation requirements (e.g., 30 credits, OSSLT, community-involvement hours) is the responsibility of:
Solution: Education is provincial under section 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867. School boards (which are creatures of provincial statute) implement the Ministry's policy. Aiming an education campaign at "the federal government" or at "City Hall" misses the actual decision-maker.
Question 4
A "regulator" in Canadian politics is best described as:
Solution: Regulators exercise delegated decision-making authority within a defined statutory frame. Many decisions never reach Cabinet — they are made by regulators. Effective civic action identifies whether the decision sits with the politician or the regulator.
Topic 2.3 — Inside Tools (Petitioning, Lobbying)
Question 5
A federal parliamentary e-petition under Standing Order 36 requires:
Solution: Federal e-petitions require 500 valid signatures, a sponsoring MP, and an open period of 30–120 days; once tabled, the government must respond within 45 days. Paper petitions to the House of Commons require 25 signatures. Online petitions on private platforms have no procedural status.
Question 6
Lobbying in Canada is:
Solution: The federal Lobbying Act (administered by the Commissioner of Lobbying) and provincial equivalents (Ontario's Lobbyists Registration Act) require registration and public disclosure. Unions, NGOs, disability-rights groups, and industry associations all lobby; lobbying is a legitimate part of democratic engagement when conducted transparently.
Topic 2.4 — Outside Tools (Protest, Civil Disobedience)
Question 7
Civil disobedience, as a defined civic-action tactic, requires:
Solution: Drawing on Thoreau, Gandhi, and King, civil disobedience is the public, non-violent breach of a law considered unjust, with willingness to accept the legal penalty as part of the moral statement. Anonymous law-breaking or property damage is ordinary law-breaking, not civil disobedience in this sense.
Question 8
The right to strike was recognised as constitutionally protected by the Supreme Court of Canada in:
Solution: SFL v. Saskatchewan (2015) held that the right to strike is constitutionally protected under section 2(d) freedom of association of the Charter — a major strengthening of labour rights in Canada.
Topic 2.5 — Digital Civic Action
Question 9
"Slacktivism" refers to:
Solution: Slacktivism is the worry that easy online expression can give participants the feeling of having "done something" without producing political change. Online action is not bad in itself — used well, it amplifies offline organising; used badly, it replaces it.
Question 10
Algorithmic distortion, in the context of platform-mediated civic action, refers to:
Solution: Platforms optimise for engagement; engagement correlates with strong emotion (outrage, fear, identity). The result is systematic over-representation of inflammatory content in feeds — a structural problem for civic discourse that goes beyond any individual user's choices.
Topic 2.6 — Canadian Case Studies
Question 11
Idle No More (2012–) emerged in response to:
Solution: Idle No More was started in November 2012 by four women — Sylvia McAdam, Jessica Gordon, Sheelah McLean, Nina Wilson — in Saskatoon, with Bill C-45 as the immediate catalyst. The movement broadened to challenge the wider Indigenous-Crown relationship and continues today.
Question 12
The Famous Five (Persons Case, 1929) won their challenge at:
Solution: Before 1949, the JCPC in London was Canada's final court of appeal. The 1929 Lord Sankey decision reversed the Canadian Supreme Court and held that women are "persons" eligible for Senate appointment under s. 24 BNA Act — articulating the "living tree" doctrine of constitutional interpretation.
