Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[3]
Define and distinguish: (a) coercive power, (b) persuasive power, (c) soft power.
Answer Key

(a) Coercive — capacity to compel through force or threat of force; concentrated in states. (b) Persuasive (institutional/economic) — capacity to shape behaviour through incentives short of force (money, regulation, contracts). (c) Soft — capacity to shape what people want, through culture, narrative, and perceived legitimacy. Rubric: 1 each.

2
[2]
A federal e-petition under Standing Order 36 requires:
Answer Key

B. 500 valid signatures, sponsoring MP, open period 30–120 days, government response within 45 days of tabling. Paper petitions to the House of Commons require 25 signatures.

3
[2]
Which Charter section was held in Saskatchewan Federation of Labour v. Saskatchewan (2015) to protect the right to strike?
Answer Key

C. SFL v. Saskatchewan held the right to strike is protected under s. 2(d) freedom of association — a major strengthening of labour rights in Canadian constitutional law.

4
[2]
Civil disobedience, as a defined civic-action tactic, requires three elements. Name them.
Answer Key

(1) Public — the act is visible, not anonymous; (2) Non-violent — physical force is not used against people or property; (3) Willingness to accept legal consequences — the actor does not flee prosecution, because accepting the penalty is part of the moral statement. (Some accounts add: targeted at the unjust law and aimed at moral persuasion.)

[2]
Idle No More (2012–) was started in November 2012 by four women in:
Answer Key

C. Sylvia McAdam, Jessica Gordon, Sheelah McLean, and Nina Wilson founded the movement in Saskatoon in response to federal Bill C-45.

6
[2]
Public-transit systems in Ontario (e.g., the TTC in Toronto, GRT in Waterloo Region) are primarily the responsibility of:
Answer Key

C. Municipalities operate transit; provinces create the legal framework (e.g., Metrolinx in the GTA, the Municipal Act in Ontario); the federal government contributes capital through programs like the Permanent Public Transit Fund. Most public services involve more than one level.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [13 marks]
7
[5]
A high-school student group is concerned that the province is reducing per-pupil funding for special-education resources by 8% over the next two budget years.
Design a civic-action strategy using both inside and outside tools. In a structured response (8–12 sentences), name (i) the decision-maker and any relevant body or regulator; (ii) two specific inside tools you would use, with reasoning; (iii) two specific outside tools, with reasoning; (iv) how the inside and outside tools would reinforce each other; (v) one ethical commitment you would attend to.
Answer Key

Decision-maker: Ontario Minister of Education and Cabinet (legislative authority over special-education funding allocations through the Grants for Student Needs); the Treasury Board (budgetary approvals); ultimately the Premier's Office and Cabinet. Inside tools: (1) deputation to the Standing Committee on Social Policy (Bill review); (2) formal submission to the public consultation on the Grants for Student Needs (Education Funding) regulation. Outside tools: (1) coordinated province-wide rally on a single date with allied parent groups (e.g., Ontario Federation of Home and School Associations) and educator unions; (2) op-ed campaign in local papers and social-media mobilisation. How they reinforce: outside tactics produce media coverage and public legitimacy; the deputation becomes more credible because the public concern is visible; the politician has political room to reverse course because of the visible public position. Ethical commitment: "Nothing About Us, Without Us" — co-design with families and students with lived special-education experience, not as a token but as design partners; protect anonymity of students who do not want to be publicly identified. Rubric: 1 decision-maker; 1 each inside tool (2); 1 each outside tool (2); 1 integration; 1 ethics. Adjust to 5 if integrated coherently.

8
[4]
Identify two affordances of digital platforms for civic action and two liabilities. For each, give one concrete civic-action implication.
Answer Key

Affordances (any two): Speed — coordination is fast (Implication: rapid response to breaking issues like an unannounced bylaw); Scale — single users can reach millions (Implication: amplification of marginalised voices that traditional media ignores); Documentation — phone-camera evidence (Implication: George Floyd recording transformed the political response). Liabilities (any two): Algorithmic distortion — emotional/divisive content rises (Implication: a careful argument may lose to an inflammatory one); Disinformation — falsehoods move fast (Implication: campaigns need rapid-correction protocols); Slacktivism — easy expression can substitute for hard organising (Implication: digital metrics must not replace meaningful outcomes); Surveillance — digital traces invite reprisal (Implication: brief organisers on digital security). Rubric: 1 each — affordance/liability + implication.

9
[4]
Apply the four concepts of political thinking to the Right to Read Inquiry (2020–2022). One sentence on each concept.
Answer Key

Significance: first major use of an Ontario Human Rights Commission public inquiry to address a systemic curriculum issue; high stakes for students with reading disabilities and for evidence-based instruction practice. Objectives and results: the OHRC's objective — document discrimination and produce binding recommendations — was met (2022 report, 157 recommendations); the implementation result is partial: the Ontario Grade 1–3 Language curriculum was revised in 2023 to require structured-literacy approaches, but classroom implementation is uneven. Stability and change: stability — the OHRC inquiry framework as a civic-action lever; change — Ontario reading instruction shifted from balanced literacy / cueing to structured literacy / phonics. Political perspective: parents and disability-rights groups (lead voice); reading-instruction practitioners (mixed); curriculum policy-makers (responsive); structured-literacy researchers (validated). Rubric: 1 each.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences) explaining the difference between an online petition (e.g., on Change.org) and a formal parliamentary petition under Standing Order 36. Conclude with a recommendation about when each is appropriate.
Answer Key

Sample: Online petitions on private platforms like Change.org have no procedural status in Parliament; they require no government response and impose no legal obligation. Formal parliamentary e-petitions under Standing Order 36 require 500 valid signatures, a sponsoring Member of Parliament, and an open period of 30 to 120 days, after which the petition is tabled and the government must respond within 45 days. The online petition is useful for building visibility, demonstrating public scale, and feeding media coverage. The parliamentary petition is the formal channel that produces a government-on-the-record response. Effective campaigns often use both — the online platform to build the public coalition, the formal petition to convert that coalition into a procedurally meaningful step. Use the online petition for early-stage mobilisation; use the parliamentary petition once a sponsoring MP has agreed and the coalition is mature enough to draw 500+ verified signatures. Rubric: 1 online petition; 1 formal petition; 1 use of both; 1 conventions.

11
[4]
Write a short response (4–6 sentences) to a first-time voter who says "Voting doesn't matter — my one vote can't change anything." Use specific evidence from the Canadian electoral context.
Answer Key

Sample: A single vote rarely changes a national election outcome, but vote share — the cumulative effect of many individual votes — does. Ontario's 2022 election had ridings decided by under 100 votes; municipal elections, where turnout typically falls under 40%, often hinge on hundreds of votes; school-trustee races sometimes by dozens. Voting is also the most universal civic action a Canadian can take — the right to vote (Charter s. 3) is one that millions of people elsewhere are still fighting for. Beyond the immediate outcome, voting signals to elected representatives that you are a constituent they must account to — non-voters are systematically less responded to by their MPs and MPPs. Your one vote matters, your vote share matters, and the habit of voting compounds over a lifetime. Rubric: 1 for vote-share argument; 1 for concrete close-race example; 1 for non-voter response; 1 for conventions.

12
[4]
In a paragraph (4–6 sentences), explain to a Grade 9 student what a "deputation" to a municipal council is, and how a 5-minute deputation is typically structured.
Answer Key

Sample: A deputation is a short structured address that any member of the public can give to a municipal council (or to a council committee, a school board, or some standing committees of the Legislative Assembly) on an item on the meeting agenda. In Ontario, most municipal council deputations are 5 minutes — about 600–700 spoken words — and require registration in advance. A strong 5-minute deputation has five parts: (1) respectful opening to the chair and councillors; (2) speaker's name, role, and connection to the issue; (3) the specific ask in a single sentence; (4) three reasons with evidence (data, comparison cases, or lived experience); (5) a short close repeating the ask. The deputation is the formal, on-the-record way a citizen shapes a municipal decision — and one of the most important inside tools for civic action at the local level. Rubric: 1 definition; 2 structure; 1 conventions.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[6]
The federal government is proposing to amend the Online Streaming Act regulations to require platforms (YouTube, Spotify, Netflix) to show a certain percentage of Canadian content. A youth coalition wants to engage the policy process — both to support strong Canadian-content protection and to flag concerns about how "Canadian content" is defined, particularly for Indigenous and emerging creators.
Apply the engagement-tools toolkit. In 8–10 sentences, identify: (a) which level of government and which regulator has jurisdiction; (b) two inside tools the coalition should use; (c) two outside tools it should pair with them; (d) one ethical commitment, particularly around Indigenous creator inclusion; (e) one realistic SMART outcome target for one school semester.
Answer Key

(a) Federal — Parliament for the legislative frame; the CRTC (Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission) for the actual regulations; Canadian Heritage policy lead. (b) Inside tools: formal CRTC consultation submission (CRTC opens periodic public consultations on streaming regulations); appearance as witness at the Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage. (c) Outside tools: coordinated social-media campaign with creator-coalitions (Indigenous Screen Office, emerging artist networks); op-ed series in major papers and on platforms (the strategic irony of using platforms to argue about platform regulation). (d) Ethical commitment: Indigenous-creator inclusion in any "Canadian content" definition must be Indigenous-led — design the coalition with Indigenous Screen Office leadership and follow OCAP (ownership, control, access, possession) principles for any Indigenous-data use. (e) SMART target: "By 31 May 2027, submit a coalition brief to the CRTC consultation co-authored with the Indigenous Screen Office and signed by at least 30 youth creators, reflecting the coalition's recommendations on Canadian-content definition." Rubric: 1 jurisdiction; 2 inside; 2 outside; 1 ethics with explicit Indigenous-creator commitment; 1 SMART target. Adjust to 6 if integrated.
