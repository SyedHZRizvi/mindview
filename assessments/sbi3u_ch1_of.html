Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
A microorganism is single-celled, lacks a nucleus, and contains peptidoglycan in its cell wall. Which domain does it belong to?
Answer Key

B (Bacteria). Peptidoglycan is unique to bacterial cell walls. Archaea lack peptidoglycan and have distinct membrane lipids; eukaryotes have nuclei.

2
[3]
List the taxonomic hierarchy from broadest to narrowest. Use the example of Homo sapiens to show how the levels apply.
Answer Key

Domain Eukarya → Kingdom Animalia → Phylum Chordata → Class Mammalia → Order Primates → Family Hominidae → Genus Homo → Species Homo sapiens.

3
[2]
Which feature is shared by ALL chordates?
Answer Key

C. All chordates have a dorsal hollow nerve cord, notochord, pharyngeal slits, and post-anal tail at some life stage. The vertebral column is only in vertebrates (a subgroup of Chordata).

4
[3]
For each kingdom, state one defining characteristic: Plantae, Animalia, Fungi.
Answer Key

Plantae: multicellular autotrophs with cellulose cell walls and chloroplasts (photosynthesis). Animalia: multicellular heterotrophs that ingest food, no cell walls, develop from a blastula. Fungi: mostly multicellular heterotrophs that absorb nutrients (saprotrophs/parasites), chitin cell walls.

5
[3]
Match each phylum to its diagnostic feature: Porifera, Cnidaria, Arthropoda.
Answer Key

Porifera (sponges): asymmetric, no true tissues, choanocyte feeding. Cnidaria (jellyfish, corals, hydras): radial symmetry, two tissue layers, nematocysts (stinging cells). Arthropoda (insects, crustaceans, arachnids): chitin exoskeleton, segmented body, jointed appendages.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[4]
A patch of forest contains the following counts: 200 sugar maples, 100 red oaks, 50 white pines, 50 yellow birches. Calculate Simpson's diversity index D = 1 − Σ(pi)². Show all work.
Answer Key

Total = 400. Proportions: 0.50, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125. Σp² = 0.25 + 0.0625 + 0.015625 + 0.015625 = 0.34375. D = 1 − 0.344 ≈ 0.66. A community with four equally abundant species would give D = 0.75. The actual D = 0.66 indicates moderate biodiversity with sugar maple dominance lowering evenness.

7
[4]
A new species is discovered. It is multicellular, has a chitin exoskeleton, segmented body with jointed appendages, three body regions, and six legs. Place it in: (a) Domain, (b) Kingdom, (c) Phylum, (d) Class. Justify each.
Answer Key

(a) Eukarya (multicellular, must have eukaryotic cells). (b) Animalia (heterotrophic, no cell walls). (c) Arthropoda (chitin exoskeleton, jointed appendages, segmentation). (d) Insecta (three body regions — head, thorax, abdomen — and six legs, the diagnostic features of insects).

8
[4]
Design a brief experimental protocol to compare the biodiversity of two woodlots (a 20-year-old plantation vs a 150-year-old old-growth forest). State the sampling technique, what data you would collect, how you would calculate diversity, and one controlled variable.
Answer Key

Sampling: lay out replicate 10 m × 10 m quadrats (≥ 5 per woodlot) using random GPS coordinates. Data: identify every tree species and count individuals in each quadrat. Calculation: compute Simpson's diversity index D for each woodlot and compare means with a t-test. Controlled variables: quadrat size, time of year, observer, identification method (key or app). Hypothesis: D for old-growth > D for plantation due to greater species richness and evenness over time.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
9
[4]
Explain, with reference to Carl Woese's 1977 work, why classification moved from a five-kingdom system to a three-domain system. Use proper terminology.
Answer Key

Woese compared rRNA sequences across prokaryotes and found that organisms previously lumped together as "Monera" actually belong to two genetically distinct lineages — Bacteria and Archaea — that diverged billions of years ago. Archaea share several molecular features (membrane lipids, certain genes, transcription machinery) with eukaryotes, not with bacteria. To reflect this, taxonomists added a new rank above kingdom — Domain — giving three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. The five-kingdom view masked the deep divergence between Bacteria and Archaea.

10
[4]
Distinguish homologous, analogous, and vestigial structures. Give one biological example of each.
Answer Key

Homologous: same evolutionary origin, possibly different function (mammal forelimb plan: human arm, whale flipper, bat wing — same bones, different uses). Analogous: same function, different evolutionary origin (insect wing vs bird wing — both fly, evolved independently). Vestigial: reduced structure inherited from an ancestor, no current function (human appendix, whale hip bones, hind-limb buds in snake embryos). Homology supports common ancestry; analogy supports convergent evolution.

11
[4]
Draw or describe a labelled cladogram showing four taxa (fish, amphibians, reptiles, mammals) with an outgroup (lancelet). Identify two synapomorphies and at one node where each evolved.
Answer Key

Outgroup: lancelet (basal). First node: vertebral column (defines fish + tetrapods). Next node: tetrapod limbs with digits (fish branch off; amphibians + amniotes share four-legged ancestor). Next node: amniotic egg (amphibians branch off; reptiles + mammals share amniote ancestor). Final node: mammary glands and hair (defining mammals). Two key synapomorphies: vertebrae (at the vertebrate node) and amniotic egg (at the amniote node).

Part D: Application [13 marks]
12
[5]
Case study — Emerald ash borer: Since the early 2000s, ash trees across Ontario have been devastated by an invasive Asian beetle, the emerald ash borer. (a) Identify the principal HIPPO category. (b) Outline how the borer reduces forest biodiversity. (c) State one short-term and one long-term conservation response.
Answer Key

(a) Invasive species (the "I" in HIPPO). (b) Borer larvae bore galleries under the bark, severing phloem and starving the tree; mortality exceeds 99% in untreated ash within a few years. Loss of mature ash reduces tree species diversity, alters forest structure, eliminates habitat for ash-specialist invertebrates, and can cascade to ash-dependent songbirds. (c) Short-term: chemical treatment (TreeAzin) for high-value urban ash, biological control (release of parasitoid wasps), strict quarantine on firewood movement, removal of infested trees. Long-term: breeding or genetic engineering of resistant ash, restoring forest cover with diverse native species, monitoring with traps.

13
[4]
Conservation reasoning: Two patches of forest can be protected — one is a small isolated remnant with high endemism, the other a larger but more degraded patch. Argue which to prioritise and why. Include the concepts of habitat corridors and minimum viable populations.
Answer Key

A defensible answer prioritises the small endemic patch in the short term because losing endemic species means global extinction (not just regional loss), but pairs the protection with active habitat restoration of the degraded patch AND wildlife corridors connecting both. Reasoning: small isolated populations are at risk of falling below the minimum viable population size, suffer inbreeding depression, and lose alleles to drift. Corridors allow gene flow between patches, maintaining genetic diversity. A larger restored patch can buffer ecological processes (fire regimes, predator-prey dynamics) and provide source populations. The strongest answer integrates both rather than choosing one.

14
[4]
STSE — Indigenous-led conservation: Many Indigenous Nations in Canada have established Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas (IPCAs). Identify two reasons IPCAs are often more effective than conventionally managed parks at conserving biodiversity.
Answer Key

(1) Indigenous knowledge systems integrate centuries of place-based observation of species behaviour, seasonal cycles, ecological interactions, and disturbance regimes (e.g., traditional fire management). This complements Western science with longer ecological time-series. (2) IPCAs are tied to lived stewardship and cultural responsibility, so local communities directly enforce protection, monitor change, and adapt management — rather than relying on a distant agency. Additional points: IPCAs often span the full traditional territory of culturally important species, preserving migration routes that smaller conventional parks cannot. Co-management agreements between First Nations and the Canadian government have shown demonstrably better outcomes for at-risk species in several provinces.
