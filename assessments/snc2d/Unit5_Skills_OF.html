Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]

[2]
Which is required for a hypothesis to be scientific?
Answer Key

B. Falsifiability is the demarcation criterion proposed by Karl Popper: a hypothesis is scientific only if there is some imaginable observation that would show it wrong. Theories that "explain everything" by being unfalsifiable are not science.

2
[3]
Define and give one example each of: independent variable, dependent variable, controlled variable.
Answer Key

Independent variable (IV): factor you change deliberately. Example: temperature in an enzyme experiment. Dependent variable (DV): factor you measure. Example: reaction rate (bubbles/min, absorbance, time to colour change). Controlled variable: factor you keep constant. Example: substrate concentration, pH, enzyme amount, mixing — all kept the same across trials.

3
[3]
Identify the WHMIS pictogram for each and give one example of a chemical it applies to: (a) flame, (b) corrosion, (c) skull and crossbones.
Answer Key

(a) Flame: flammable. Examples: ethanol, propanone (acetone), gasoline, methane. (b) Corrosion (test tube dripping on hand and surface): corrosive to skin and metals. Examples: concentrated HCl, \(H_2SO_4\), NaOH (lye), bleach (sodium hypochlorite). (c) Skull and crossbones: acutely toxic. Examples: cyanide salts, methanol (very toxic if drunk), arsenic compounds, hydrogen cyanide.

4
[3]
Distinguish between random error and systematic error. Give an example of each in a science lab.
Answer Key

Random error: scatter from limits of measurement precision and inherent variability. Example: slightly different stopwatch start times across trials in a reaction-rate experiment; can be reduced by averaging many trials. Systematic error: consistent bias in one direction; cannot be reduced by averaging — only by improving design. Example: a thermometer that reads 2 °C too high; using uncalibrated equipment; consistently overshooting an endpoint in titration.

5
[2]
Which is true about percent error?
Answer Key

B. Percent error compares experiment to a known accepted value. It is taken as the absolute value × 100% / accepted. Uncertainty (range of plausible values from your data) is different — they may both be small or both large depending on the experiment.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[6]
Design a full controlled experiment to test whether the type of cooking oil (canola, olive, sunflower) affects the temperature at which oil begins to smoke. Identify: hypothesis, IV, DV, controlled variables (at least 3), replicate plan, expected outcome, one safety consideration.
Answer Key

Hypothesis: If oils differ in chemical composition (saturation, free fatty-acid content), then their smoke points will differ; refined oils with low free fatty acid (canola, sunflower) will have higher smoke points than less-refined olive oil. IV: type of oil. DV: temperature at which smoke first appears continuously. Controlled: same mass/volume of oil (e.g., 20 mL), same heating rate (constant burner setting), same pan/beaker (same material and thickness), same thermometer placement, same ambient ventilation, same person observing. Replicate: 3 trials per oil; average smoke temperature. Expected: olive ~190 °C, canola ~205 °C, sunflower ~225 °C (varies with refinement). Safety: hot oil splatter and ignition risk — use long-handled thermometer, keep fire blanket nearby, do not heat oil above smoke point, work under fume hood. Wear safety goggles and lab coat.

7
[3]
A student measures the volume of a metal cube to be 8.0 cm³ with uncertainty ±0.2 cm³, and its mass to be 21.6 g with uncertainty ±0.1 g. (a) Calculate the density and its uncertainty. (b) The literature density of aluminium is 2.70 g/cm³ and of zinc is 7.13 g/cm³. Which metal is most consistent with the measurement?
Answer Key

(a) Density ρ = m/V = 21.6 / 8.0 = 2.7 g/cm³. Relative uncertainty: \((0.1/21.6) + (0.2/8.0) = 0.005 + 0.025 = 0.030\) (3%). Absolute uncertainty: 2.7 × 0.030 ≈ 0.08 g/cm³. Result: (2.7 ± 0.1) g/cm³. (b) This value matches aluminium (2.70 g/cm³) almost exactly; far below zinc (7.13). The metal is aluminium.

8
[3]
A pharmaceutical study claims a new drug reduces blood pressure based on n = 3 subjects. List three reasons why this evidence is weak.
Answer Key

(1) Sample size too small — three people cannot represent the variability of a population; results may be due to chance. (2) No control group mentioned — without a placebo group, you cannot isolate the drug effect from natural variation, regression to the mean, or placebo effect. (3) Other potential confounders — without random assignment or matching, factors like diet, exercise, and pre-existing conditions could explain the result. (4) No blinding — if subjects/researchers know who is taking the drug, expectations affect outcomes (placebo effect, observer bias). (5) Short duration — long-term safety and effects must be tested. A rigorous claim requires a multi-hundred-subject randomised, controlled, double-blind trial.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
Write a one-paragraph summary explaining the role of WHMIS in keeping science labs safe. Include: hazard identification, training, PPE, and emergency response.
Answer Key

WHMIS (Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System) is Canada's regulatory framework for communicating chemical hazards in workplaces and schools. It requires that hazardous products carry standardised labels (with pictograms and signal words "Danger" or "Warning") and that Safety Data Sheets (SDS) be available for detailed information. Students and workers must be trained to recognise the nine pictogram categories (flammable, corrosive, acutely toxic, etc.) and to follow safe-handling procedures. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) — safety goggles, gloves, lab coats, closed-toe shoes — provides the last layer of defence between hazard and student. Emergency response is built into the system: eyewash stations and safety showers for chemical splashes; fire extinguishers, fire blankets, and clear exits for fires; designated waste containers prevent improper disposal; and incident reporting ensures lessons learned. Together, these layered controls make modern school labs vastly safer than they were a generation ago.

11
[4]
Choose one career that interests you from this course. Write a paragraph explaining (a) what the role does day-to-day, (b) what high-school courses prepare you for it, and (c) the post-secondary pathway.
Answer Key

(Example: registered nurse.) A registered nurse (RN) provides direct patient care in hospitals, clinics, schools, or community settings: assessing patients, administering medications, performing procedures, monitoring vital signs, educating patients and families about their health, and collaborating with physicians and other professionals. The work combines deep biological knowledge with applied skills, communication, and ethics. Preparation: Grade 11 and 12 biology and chemistry are essential; mathematics is required; English and social-science courses help with communication and broader perspective. The standard pathway in Ontario is a 4-year Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BScN) at a university (e.g., Toronto Metropolitan, Western, Ottawa, McMaster), which includes extensive clinical placements, followed by the national NCLEX-RN licensing exam and provincial registration. Alternatives: 2-year RPN diploma at a community college (Registered Practical Nurse), which can later bridge to RN. Specialisations (critical care, palliative, mental health) typically require additional certifications or graduate study. Median Ontario RN salary is roughly $70,000–95,000 (2024), with opportunities for management, teaching, or independent practice.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Integration Case Study: Climate change is causing earlier spring melts in Ontario lakes. Connect this fact to at least three other strands of SNC2D — explain how it relates to biology (ecosystems), chemistry (acidification), and physics (light/optics or other).
Answer Key

Earlier melt affects all four strands of SNC2D. Biology: Many species' life cycles are timed by water temperature and ice-out — for example, smallmouth bass spawning, walleye reproduction, and aquatic-insect emergence. Earlier melt can desynchronise predator-prey relationships (mismatched timing of fish and their plankton or insect prey), reducing reproductive success. Wildlife dependent on ice (loons, common goldeneye nesting) shift ranges. Chemistry: Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, stressing fish. Warming and more rainfall increase nutrient runoff (nitrogen, phosphorus from agriculture), which can cause algal blooms and lower pH locally. \(CO_2\) from increased emissions also dissolves into freshwater, marginally lowering pH. Physics (optics): Ice cover has very high albedo (reflects most sunlight, ~0.6); open water has low albedo (~0.1), so earlier ice-out exposes more sunlight to the water, warming it further — the ice-albedo positive feedback we studied in Unit 3. Optical properties also change as algal blooms reduce water clarity, affecting underwater visibility for predators and altering light penetration to aquatic plants. Skills (Strand A): Investigating these changes requires careful long-term monitoring (Ontario's Provincial Water Quality Monitoring Network), statistical analysis of trends, controlled lab experiments on individual factors, and respectful engagement with Indigenous knowledge holders whose communities have observed these changes for generations.

13
[4]
STSE — Indigenous Knowledge: Explain what is meant by Indigenous knowledge in the context of science. Give two examples of how it contributes to environmental science and biology, and discuss one ethical principle for respectful collaboration with Indigenous communities.
Answer Key

Indigenous knowledge refers to knowledge systems developed by First Nations, Inuit, and Métis peoples over millennia of close observation, oral tradition, and stewardship of specific lands and waters. It is empirical (based on observation), iterative (refined over generations), holistic (integrated with cultural and spiritual knowledge), and place-based (specific to local ecosystems). Examples: (1) Inuit hunters' detailed observations of sea-ice timing and animal behaviour have provided crucial data on Arctic climate change, complementing instrumental records and revealing changes Western scientific instruments cannot capture (such as ice texture or wildlife body condition). (2) Cree fire-stewardship practices on the prairies (controlled burns) maintained grassland ecosystems and reduced catastrophic wildfire — knowledge now informing modern wildland fire management. (3) Coast Salish clam-garden engineering increased shellfish yields sustainably for thousands of years and is informing modern aquaculture. Ethical principles for respectful collaboration: (i) Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC, recognised under UNDRIP) — Indigenous communities should be consulted before research on their lands or knowledge begins. (ii) Acknowledge and credit knowledge holders (do not appropriate). (iii) Share benefits of research equitably. (iv) Recognise that Indigenous knowledge is its own valid system, not "data" to be extracted into Western science. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) provide guidance.
