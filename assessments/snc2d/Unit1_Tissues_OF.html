Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
A cell containing chromosomes lined up across the centre of the cell is in:
Answer Key

Metaphase. Chromosomes align along the metaphase plate (cell equator) with spindle fibres attached at the centromeres.

2
[3]
List the four primary animal tissues. Give one example of an organ where each is found.
Answer Key

(1) Epithelial — skin (stratified squamous), small intestine lining (simple columnar). (2) Connective — bone, blood, cartilage, adipose, tendons. (3) Muscle — skeletal (biceps), cardiac (heart wall), smooth (gut, blood vessels). (4) Nervous — brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves.

3
[2]
In which structures of the plant does most photosynthesis occur, and what tissue type are they?
Answer Key

Most photosynthesis occurs in leaf mesophyll — specifically the palisade mesophyll, made of densely packed parenchyma cells rich in chloroplasts. Some occurs in spongy mesophyll (also parenchyma) below.

4
[3]
Trace the path of a glucose molecule absorbed in the small intestine until it reaches the brain. Name the organs and major vessels.
Answer Key

Small intestine (villi) → portal vein → liver → hepatic vein → inferior vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → carotid artery → brain.

5
[3]
Differentiate prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells by listing three structural features that differ.
Answer Key

(1) Nucleus: prokaryotes have no membrane-bound nucleus (DNA in nucleoid); eukaryotes have a true nucleus. (2) Size: prokaryotes 1–10 µm; eukaryotes 10–100 µm. (3) Organelles: prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi); eukaryotes have them. Other valid: ribosome size (70S vs 80S), DNA shape (circular vs linear chromosomes).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[5]
A student counts 200 onion-root cells under the microscope. She finds 180 in interphase, 8 in prophase, 4 in metaphase, 5 in anaphase, and 3 in telophase. If the full cell cycle is 24 hours, estimate the duration of each phase. Show your reasoning.
Answer Key

The fraction of cells in each phase × total time = duration. Interphase: 180/200 × 24 = 21.6 h. Prophase: 8/200 × 24 = 0.96 h ≈ 58 min. Metaphase: 4/200 × 24 = 0.48 h ≈ 29 min. Anaphase: 5/200 × 24 = 0.6 h ≈ 36 min. Telophase: 3/200 × 24 = 0.36 h ≈ 22 min. Total mitosis ≈ 2.4 h, consistent with the rough 90/10 split between interphase and mitosis.

7
[4]
Design a controlled experiment to test the effect of caffeine on cell division in onion-root tips. Identify variables, controls, and predicted results.
Answer Key

IV: caffeine concentration (e.g., 0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 mM). DV: mitotic index (% cells in mitosis / total counted) after 24 h exposure. Controls: same onion variety, same root-tip stage, same temperature (20 °C), same growth medium, same staining protocol; control group = 0 mM (water only). Replicate: 3–5 root tips per concentration, count ≥200 cells per slide. Hypothesis: caffeine inhibits mitosis (it interferes with cell-cycle checkpoints), so mitotic index will decrease with increasing caffeine concentration. Plot mitotic index vs concentration; look for dose-response trend.

8
[3]
Predict what would happen if a cell's checkpoint proteins (such as p53) stopped working. Give two specific consequences.
Answer Key

p53 (and other checkpoint proteins) normally pauses the cell cycle to repair DNA damage, or triggers apoptosis if damage is severe. Without it: (1) cells with damaged DNA continue dividing, accumulating mutations; (2) damaged cells fail to self-destruct (apoptosis), allowing growth to continue unchecked. The combination is a hallmark of cancer — p53 is mutated in about half of all human cancers, earning it the name "guardian of the genome".

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
Describe (with labels) the four major phases of mitosis. Include the key events of each phase. (A labeled written description is acceptable in place of a diagram.)
Answer Key

Prophase: chromatin condenses to visible chromosomes; nuclear envelope disassembles; spindle forms from centrosomes. Metaphase: chromosomes align on the equatorial (metaphase) plate; spindle attaches at centromeres. Anaphase: sister chromatids separate; spindle fibres shorten, pulling chromatids to opposite poles. Telophase: chromatids reach poles, decondense; new nuclear envelopes form around each daughter nucleus; cytokinesis begins.

10
[4]
Construct a comparison table for the three plant tissue systems (dermal, ground, vascular). Include for each: location, key cell types, primary function.
Answer Key

Dermal: outermost layer of stems/leaves/roots; epidermis (with cuticle, stomata, guard cells, root hairs); protection, water-loss control, gas exchange. Ground: between dermal and vascular; parenchyma (storage/photosynthesis), collenchyma (flexible support), sclerenchyma (rigid support); makes up the bulk of the plant body. Vascular: in the plant's interior; xylem (dead, hollow, lignified tubes — water/minerals up), phloem (living sieve-tubes with companion cells — sugars to sinks); transport.

11
[4]
Explain how the structure of an alveolus is adapted to its function of gas exchange. Use the terms surface area, diffusion distance, and capillary network.
Answer Key

The lungs contain about 300 million alveoli, providing a vast total surface area (~70 m², a tennis court). Each alveolus wall is a single squamous epithelial cell thick, and the adjacent capillary wall is also a single endothelial cell thick — total diffusion distance is less than 1 µm, fast for diffusion (Fick's law: rate ∝ area / distance × concentration difference). Each alveolus is wrapped in a dense capillary network, so blood constantly arrives oxygen-poor and leaves oxygen-rich. The thin moist layer inside dissolves \(O_2\) for diffusion. Adaptation: large area, short path, rich blood supply — all maximise gas exchange.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Case Study — Cystic Fibrosis: Cystic fibrosis (CF) is caused by a mutation in the CFTR gene that affects chloride-ion channels in epithelial cells. Explain why CF affects multiple organ systems, and identify two body systems most affected.
Answer Key

CFTR channels regulate the movement of Cl⁻ ions out of epithelial cells. Without functional CFTR, Cl⁻ stays inside; water follows osmotically, so the mucus that coats epithelial linings becomes thick and sticky. Because CFTR is expressed in epithelia throughout the body, CF affects: (1) respiratory system: thick mucus clogs airways, traps bacteria, leads to recurrent infections (Pseudomonas, S. aureus) and chronic lung damage — the leading cause of mortality in CF; (2) digestive system: pancreatic ducts are blocked, so digestive enzymes cannot reach the small intestine; nutrient absorption suffers, requiring enzyme-replacement therapy. Other affected: sweat glands (high salt in sweat — the original diagnostic test), reproductive (male infertility from blocked vas deferens), liver. The systemic effects illustrate how a single molecular defect in a widely-expressed protein can damage many integrated body systems.

13
[4]
STSE — Organ Donation: Ontario has a "donor registry" (opt-in) system. Some countries use "opt-out" (presumed consent). Argue for or against switching to an opt-out system, addressing one scientific, one ethical, and one practical consideration.
Answer Key

(A defensible argument either way; key is multi-dimensional reasoning.) Sample for: scientific — organ shortages cause hundreds of deaths annually in Canada; transplant outcomes have improved dramatically with immunosuppression, so more transplants would save more lives. Ethical — individual autonomy is preserved through the opt-out option; presumed consent reflects the strong public majority who support donation in surveys. Practical — countries with opt-out (Spain, Belgium) consistently have ~30–50% higher donation rates than opt-in. Sample against: scientific — the largest gains come from better identification of potential donors and family conversations, not from changing the default. Ethical — opt-out can be seen as the state claiming the body by default, violating bodily autonomy and possibly religious beliefs. Practical — public trust in the medical system can decline if people feel coerced; family override clauses can blunt the policy's effect anyway. A complete answer engages all three dimensions and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

14
[3]
Application — Stem Cells: A spinal-cord injury patient might benefit from injected stem cells that differentiate into new neurons. Why are pluripotent stem cells (e.g., iPSCs) preferred over multipotent adult stem cells for this application?
Answer Key

Multipotent adult stem cells are restricted to lineages — hematopoietic stem cells make blood cells; neural stem cells can make neurons and glia but are very limited in quantity and hard to access. Pluripotent stem cells can become any of the ~200 cell types of the body, including specific neuron subtypes needed for spinal-cord repair. iPSCs (induced pluripotent stem cells) offer two further advantages: (1) they can be derived from the patient's own skin or blood cells, eliminating immune rejection; (2) no embryo is destroyed, sidestepping the ethical objections that attached to embryonic stem cells. The main concern with iPSCs is the risk of incomplete differentiation (residual pluripotent cells can form teratomas), which clinical-trial protocols must carefully manage.
