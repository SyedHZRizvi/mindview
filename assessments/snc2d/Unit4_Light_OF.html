Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [13 marks]
1
[2]
The speed of light in vacuum is:
Answer Key

B. \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\) m/s in vacuum. (D is also numerically equal but in km/s — be careful with units.)

2
[3]
List, in order of increasing wavelength, the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. Give one common use for each.
Answer Key

From shortest λ to longest: gamma rays (medical radiotherapy, cosmic-ray astronomy) → X-rays (medical imaging, security screening) → UV (sterilisation, vitamin-D synthesis in skin) → visible light (vision, photography, fibre optics) → infrared (thermal imaging, remote controls, heat lamps) → microwaves (microwave ovens, radar, Wi-Fi) → radio waves (broadcasting, mobile phones, MRI signals).

3
[3]
Calculate the wavelength of a 100 MHz FM radio signal.
Answer Key

\(\lambda = c/f = (3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}) / (100 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz})\) = 3.00 m. Reasonable for an FM antenna scale (rooftop antennas are typically about half this length).

4
[2]
For a plane mirror, the image is:
Answer Key

C. Light rays appear to come from behind the mirror (virtual), image is upright and same size; left and right appear swapped (lateral inversion is actually a swap of front and back, perceived as left-right).

5
[3]
Define and give an example of: (a) virtual image, (b) total internal reflection, (c) magnification.
Answer Key

(a) Virtual image: image from which light only appears to come (it does not actually converge there) — e.g., the image in a plane mirror or a magnifying glass. (b) Total internal reflection: complete reflection back into a denser medium when the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle — e.g., light bouncing along a fibre-optic cable. (c) Magnification: ratio of image size to object size, \(m = h_i/h_o = -d_i/d_o\) — e.g., a microscope might give 400×.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
6
[4]
Light passes from glass (\(n = 1.50\)) into water (\(n = 1.33\)). The ray strikes the boundary at 25° from the normal in glass. (a) Find the angle of refraction in water. (b) Will total internal reflection occur at this angle? If not, find the critical angle for this interface.
Answer Key

(a) \(n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2\): \(1.50 \sin 25° = 1.33 \sin\theta_2\) → \(\sin\theta_2 = 1.50(0.4226)/1.33 = 0.477\) → \(\theta_2 = \arcsin(0.477) = \textbf{28.5°}\). Ray bends away from the normal (entering a less-dense medium). (b) Since the ray refracted into water, no total internal reflection — that only happens above the critical angle. \(\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1 = 1.33/1.50 = 0.887\) → \(\theta_c = \arcsin(0.887) = \textbf{62.5°}\). Only incidence angles above 62.5° would totally reflect.

7
[4]
An object is placed 6 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. (a) Find the image location. (b) Find the magnification. (c) Describe the image (real/virtual, upright/inverted, larger/smaller).
Answer Key

(a) \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i\): \(1/10 = 1/6 + 1/d_i\) → \(1/d_i = 1/10 - 1/6 = 3/30 - 5/30 = -2/30 = -1/15\) → \(d_i = \textbf{−15 cm}\) (negative means virtual, behind mirror). (b) \(m = -d_i/d_o = -(-15)/6 = \textbf{+2.5}\) (positive, upright). (c) Virtual, upright, larger (2.5×). This is how a shaving or makeup mirror works when held close to the face: when object is inside f, the image is enlarged and right-side up.

8
[4]
Design an experiment to verify Snell's law using a ray box, a semicircular glass block, and a protractor. Identify variables, controls, and a way to calculate the refractive index of the glass.
Answer Key

IV: angle of incidence \(\theta_1\) (e.g., 10°, 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60°). DV: angle of refraction \(\theta_2\). Setup: shine ray box at flat face of semicircular block (light enters perpendicular to curved face, so no refraction there); measure incident angle (from normal) and refracted angle inside the glass. Controlled: same glass block, same ray-box position, same room lighting (to see rays clearly). Record \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) for each setting. To find \(n_{glass}\): from Snell's law \(\sin\theta_1 = n_{glass} \sin\theta_2\), so plot \(\sin\theta_1\) (y) vs \(\sin\theta_2\) (x). The slope should be a straight line through the origin with slope = \(n_{glass}\) — typically about 1.50 for crown glass, 1.52 for window glass. Replicate three times; calculate average slope and uncertainty.

Part C: Communication [13 marks]
9
[5]
Describe how a ray diagram for a converging lens is constructed using the three principal rays. Use the case of an object placed beyond \(2f\) to illustrate.
Answer Key

Three principal rays for a converging lens: (1) Parallel ray: leaves the top of the object parallel to the principal axis, refracts through the far focal point F on the other side of the lens. (2) Centre ray: passes through the optical centre of the lens undeviated (straight through). (3) Focal ray: passes through the near focal point on the object side, exits parallel to the principal axis. Where the three rays meet on the far side is the top of the image. For an object beyond \(2f\): the rays converge between \(f\) and \(2f\) on the far side, forming a real, inverted, smaller image. This is the configuration used in a camera or in the human eye.

10
[4]
Compare and contrast plane mirrors and converging lenses using a table. Include: function, type of image produced (typical), real or virtual, equation/principle used.
Answer Key

Plane mirror: reflects light according to the law of reflection (\(\theta_i = \theta_r\)); image is virtual, upright, same size, laterally inverted; located equal distance behind mirror. Converging lens: refracts light according to Snell's law at curved surfaces; image properties depend on object position relative to f and 2f (real and inverted for object beyond f; virtual and magnified for object inside f); described by thin-lens equation \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i\). Both: produce useful images. Differ in: type of interaction (reflection vs refraction), variety of image types, and applications.

11
[4]
Explain the structure and function of the human eye, including how it forms an image and how it can focus on objects at different distances.
Answer Key

Light enters through the cornea, which provides most of the focusing (~70%). The pupil (controlled by the iris) adjusts opening to control light intensity. The crystalline lens fine-tunes focus by changing shape — ciliary muscles squeeze the lens fatter for close objects (more refractive power) and relax it flatter for distant ones; this is called accommodation. Light converges on the retina at the back of the eye, producing a real, inverted image. Rods (peripheral, dim-light, monochrome) and cones (concentrated in the fovea, bright-light, three subtypes for colour) convert light into nerve impulses. Signals travel through the optic nerve to the visual cortex of the brain, which interprets the inverted image as upright. The blind spot is where the optic nerve exits — no photoreceptors there.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
12
[5]
Case Study — Corrective Lenses: A patient's far point (the most distant point she can see clearly) is only 0.50 m. (a) Identify the vision defect. (b) Calculate the focal length and power of the lens needed to correct it. (c) Explain physically why this lens works.
Answer Key

(a) Her far point should be infinity but is only 0.50 m — she cannot see distant objects clearly. This is myopia (short-sightedness). (b) To allow her to see distant objects (\(d_o = \infty\)), the corrective lens must place the image at her far point (0.50 m on the same side, virtual, \(d_i = -0.50\) m). Thin-lens equation: \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 0 + 1/(-0.50) = -2.0\) m⁻¹. So \(f = -0.50\) m, power \(P = 1/f = \textbf{−2.0 dioptres}\) (negative = diverging lens). (c) The diverging lens spreads incoming parallel rays from a distant object before they reach the eye. The diverged rays then appear to come from a virtual source at 0.50 m — within her far point — so her eye can focus the image on the retina. The prescription would be written as "−2.0 D".

13
[4]
Application — Fibre Optics: Describe how fibre-optic cables transmit data over long distances. Use total internal reflection in your explanation. Why are fibre-optic communications increasingly replacing copper wire?
Answer Key

A fibre-optic cable has a glass core of high refractive index (typically \(n \approx 1.48\)) surrounded by a cladding of lower refractive index (\(n \approx 1.46\)). Light entering the end of the core strikes the core-cladding boundary at angles above the critical angle and undergoes total internal reflection — no light escapes through the boundary. By repeated total internal reflections, light bounces along the fibre kilometres without significant loss. Digital data are encoded as pulses of light (often near-infrared) generated by lasers or LEDs at one end and detected by photodiodes at the other. Advantages over copper: (1) much higher bandwidth (terabits per second per fibre); (2) very low signal loss over long distances (signals can travel ~80 km without amplification); (3) immune to electromagnetic interference; (4) lighter, thinner, less metal use; (5) more secure (taps are easier to detect). Disadvantages: more brittle than copper; bend radius limits; specialised equipment needed for splicing.

14
[3]
STSE — LED vs Incandescent Lighting: Canada and the EU are phasing out incandescent bulbs in favour of LEDs. Argue that this is or is not justified, considering at least one efficiency, environmental, and consumer-cost aspect.
Answer Key

(Justified answer with multi-dimensional reasoning.) Efficiency: LEDs convert ~30–40% of electrical energy to visible light; incandescents convert about 5% (most energy becomes heat). For the same brightness, LEDs use roughly 1/8 the electricity. Environmental: lower electricity demand reduces greenhouse-gas emissions; LEDs last 25,000+ hours (vs 1,000 for incandescent), reducing waste. They contain small amounts of electronic components but no mercury (unlike CFLs). Consumer cost: LEDs cost more up front (~5–10× per bulb) but lifetime savings vastly exceed this (electricity savings + fewer replacements). Counter-considerations: some early LEDs had poor colour rendering or harsh blue spectra (improved now); manufacturing has its own footprint; rural areas with seasonal use see slower payback. Overall: the LED transition is one of the most cost-effective climate policies available, with substantial benefits to consumers, the grid, and the climate. Defensible position: justified, with attention to product quality and equitable access to higher up-front cost.
