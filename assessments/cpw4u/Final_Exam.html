[2]B · Power
Max Weber's classical definition of the state is:
B. Weber, "Politics as a Vocation" 1919. Foundational definition for modern political analysis.

[3]B · Governance
Identify three constitutional features of Canadian Westminster parliamentary government.
Examples: responsible government (Cabinet must hold confidence of elected lower house); fused executive-legislative (PM and Cabinet from Parliament); federal-provincial division of powers (ss. 91/92 Constitution Act, 1867); the Crown (formal head of state through GG); judicial review (s. 52 Constitution Act, 1982); the Charter (Part I, Constitution Act, 1982); s. 33 notwithstanding clause; reserve powers of the Crown. Any 3 with brief explanation.
3
[3]B · Charter
Identify: (a) the Oakes test (1986); (b) section 33 of the Charter; (c) the dialogue theory of judicial review.
(a) Oakes test — section 1 framework: law's objective must be pressing/substantial; means must be proportional (rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionate effect). (b) Section 33 — notwithstanding clause; legislatures can override sections 2 and 7-15 for renewable 5-year periods. (c) Dialogue theory (Hogg & Bushell 1997) — Court strikes a law, Parliament reformulates, Court considers reformulation; iterative.

[2]B · Ideology
According to Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser (2017), populism is best defined as:
B. The leading academic definition. Populism is "thin-centred" and combines with thicker ideologies (left-populism: Chávez, Sanders; right-populism: Trump, Le Pen, Orbán).
5
[3]C · Rights
Identify: (a) the UDHR (1948); (b) the ICCPR and ICESCR (1966); (c) John Humphrey's role.
(a) UDHR — Universal Declaration of Human Rights; adopted UN General Assembly 10 December 1948 (48-0 with 8 abstentions); 30 articles; foundational. (b) ICCPR & ICESCR — binding follow-up treaties on civil/political and economic/social/cultural rights; Canada ratified 1976. (c) John Humphrey — Canadian; first Director of UN Division of Human Rights; wrote UDHR's first ~400-page draft.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 2015 Final Report concluded the residential-school system constituted:
B. TRC chaired by Justice Murray Sinclair; ~150,000 children attended residential schools 1883-1996; documented mortality 4,000-6,000+; 94 Calls to Action.

The classical just-war framework distinguishes:

[2]D · IHL
The four 1949 Geneva Conventions:
A. The four conventions (wounded soldiers I, sea II, POWs III, civilians IV) + Additional Protocols I (international) and II (non-international, 1977) and III (Red Crystal, 2005).
10
[3]D · R2P
Identify: (a) the ICISS Report (2001); (b) the 2005 World Summit Outcome Document; (c) R2P's three pillars.
(a) ICISS Report — International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty, chaired by Gareth Evans & Mohamed Sahnoun on Canadian initiative; reported December 2001; introduced R2P. (b) 2005 World Summit — paragraphs 138-139 unanimously adopted R2P at UN General Assembly. (c) Three pillars: (1) state responsibility to protect own population from mass atrocities; (2) international community's duty to assist states; (3) collective response via UNSC when states "manifestly fail."
Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
11
[8]B + D · Document Analysis
Document 1: "The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law of Canada, and any law that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect." — Section 52(1), Constitution Act, 1982
Document 2: "Parliament or the legislature of a province may expressly declare in an Act of Parliament or of the legislature, as the case may be, that the Act or a provision thereof shall operate notwithstanding a provision included in section 2 or sections 7 to 15 of this Charter." — Section 33(1), Charter
Compare these two provisions. In 7-8 sentences, analyse: (i) the constitutional principle each establishes; (ii) the tension between them; (iii) the historical context of the 1981 "Kitchen Accord" that produced this compromise; (iv) apply the concept of stability and change to the post-2015 increase in section 33 use.
Section 52 establishes constitutional supremacy and authorises judicial review of all legislation; section 33 permits legislatures to override sections 2 and 7-15 for renewable five-year periods. Tension: section 52 vests final authority in courts; section 33 preserves residual parliamentary sovereignty. 1981 context: the "Kitchen Accord" between federal and seven provincial governments (excluding Quebec) produced the compromise — provincial premiers (especially Lévesque, Lougheed, Blakeney) demanded section 33 as a check on judicial activism in exchange for entrenchment of the Charter; Quebec did not sign and operated under retroactive blanket s.33 invocation 1982-85. Stability: judicial review has been the dominant Charter-enforcement mode since 1982. Change: section 33 use was rare 1982-2015 (Quebec retroactive 1982-85; Saskatchewan 1986; Alberta 2000) but has accelerated post-2018 (Quebec Bill 21 2019, Bill 96 2021, Ontario back-to-work 2021, Saskatchewan Bill 137 2024). The eventual SCC reference on Bill 21 (likely 2026-27) will define future doctrine. Marks: 2 each provision, 2 tension/history, 2 stability-change application.
12
[8]C + D · Ethical Analysis
Apply the concept of political perspective across multiple decades. In 8-10 sentences, compare Canada's response to three civilian-protection crises: (1) the 1939 MS St. Louis turn-back; (2) the 1994 Rwandan genocide; (3) the 2022– Russia-Ukraine war. Identify continuities in mechanism, differences in scope, and the evolving role of normative frameworks (UDHR, Geneva Conventions, R2P).
Continuities: all three involved decisions about how Canadian state action affects civilian populations; all three were shaped by domestic political constraints and great-power dynamics. Differences in scope: St. Louis 1939 — 907 Jewish refugees turned back; ~254 later died in Holocaust; reflected antisemitic Canadian immigration policy ("none is too many" — F.C. Blair); no normative framework yet existed. Rwanda 1994 — 800K-1M deaths in 100 days; Canada led UNAMIR (Dallaire); warnings ignored despite UDHR (1948), Genocide Convention (1948), Geneva Conventions (1949); failure to act became founding trauma of R2P doctrine (2001 ICISS, 2005 World Summit). Ukraine 2022– — full-scale Russian invasion; Canada responded with substantial assistance ($22B+ through 2024), 210K CUAET admissions, Operation Reassurance, sanctions on Russian central bank assets ($280B+ frozen); ICC arrest warrants for Putin (March 2023). Normative frameworks: from absence (1939) to existence-but-not-enforcement (1994) to substantial but conditional enforcement (2022) — but Ukraine response is bilateral/coalition-based, not UN-mandated R2P; Syria 2011-24 demonstrates the limits when great-power consensus is absent. The 2018 federal apology for the St. Louis refusal explicitly invoked historical accountability. Marks: 2 each case description, 2 continuities/differences, 2 normative framework evolution.
13
[5]A · Source Criticism
A 2025 social-media post claims: "A leaked Canadian government memo reveals that the Liberal government has signed a secret agreement giving the World Health Organization authority to mandate vaccines and lockdowns in Canada."
Apply Caulfield's SIFT method (Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims). In 5 sentences, evaluate this claim.
Stop: do not reshare; emotional content + unverified source. Investigate the source: check the account history, public profile, named author; many such posts originate from anonymous accounts. Find better coverage: search reliable sources (CBC, Globe and Mail, National Post, Reuters, Hansard) for any reference to such an agreement; legitimate intergovernmental agreements would have parliamentary record. Trace claims: the WHO does not have authority to "mandate" measures in Canada — IHR 2005 is binding but does not give WHO sovereign authority over states; Pandemic Accord negotiations 2022– are public, not secret, and require state-by-state ratification. Without source authentication, document linkage, or parliamentary verification, the claim should be treated as misinformation. Marks: 1 per SIFT step + 1 synthesis.
14
[4]E · Multilateralism
Apply the concept of political significance: in 4-5 sentences, rank the following five contemporary issues for their potential significance to multilateral cooperation in the 2030s, and justify your ranking: (a) climate change post-Paris; (b) frontier AI governance; (c) pandemic preparedness post-COVID; (d) the post-2017 trade-system fragmentation; (e) the future of UNSC reform.
Any defensible ranking with justification. Strong responses might rank: (1) climate change as most consequential — physical inevitability, longest time horizon, distributional politics (developed/developing/island states); (2) frontier AI governance as next — capabilities scaling rapidly, currently weak institutional infrastructure, transformational economic and security effects; (3) pandemic preparedness as significant — predictable recurrence; Pandemic Accord status by 2025-26 will shape global health architecture; (4) trade fragmentation as significant but partially manageable through bilateral and pluri-lateral mechanisms; (5) UNSC reform as significant but procedurally constrained — Charter Article 108 makes reform nearly impossible. Other rankings defensible with strong justification. Marks: 1 per ranked item with brief justification, 1 for synthesis quality.

[15]B + C + D + E · Major Essay
Major Task — Write a political-analysis essay (450–600 words) responding to ONE of the following prompts:

Option 1: "Trace the trajectory of Canadian Charter rights since 1982. Has section 33 served as a legitimate safety valve or as a threat to constitutional rights protection? Use specific cases and recent developments (Bill 21, Bill 96, Saskatchewan Bill 137)."
Option 2: "Evaluate the claim that Canada's foreign policy has moved 'from middle-power multilateralism to alliance-led participation.' Use specific evidence across the strands (UN peacekeeping, NATO, NORAD, R2P, the Indo-Pacific Strategy 2022)."
Option 3: "Compare and contrast Canada's response to two refugee crises: the MS St. Louis (1939) and the 2015–16 Syrian resettlement. What changed in Canadian institutional capacity, normative frameworks, and political will? Apply at least three concepts of political thinking."
Option 4: "Evaluate the post-1945 multilateral order as a Canadian project. Has Canada's distinctive contribution (Pearson peacekeeping, Humphrey UDHR, Axworthy R2P, Canadian middle-power identity) been adequately renewed for the 21st century?"

Include: introduction with thesis, 3 body paragraphs (each evidence-supported), conclusion. Embed at least 4 specific historical references (with dates and key actors). Demonstrate disciplinary register expected of a Grade-12 university-prep course.
K/U (4): correct understanding of relevant institutions, events, actors, dates. Thinking (5): quality of argument; counter-arguments; concepts of political thinking applied. Communication (4): clear thesis-driven introduction; well-organised paragraphs; specific evidence with dates and actors; conclusion synthesises beyond restating; voice and conventions appropriate to university-prep writing. Application (2): transfer of political-thinking concepts to disciplinary writing; relevance of evidence to claim. Sample first sentences for Option 1: "From Lord Sankey's 1929 'living tree' doctrine through the 1982 Charter to the post-2018 expansion of section 33, the trajectory of Canadian constitutional rights has moved through three phases — judicial supremacy under Charter section 1, legislative override under section 33, and the contemporary contestation that the eventual Supreme Court reference on Bill 21 (likely 2026-27) will define." Sample first sentences for Option 2: "Canada's foreign policy has moved from Lester Pearson's 1956 invention of UN peacekeeping at Suez to Stephen Harper's 2014 framing of Canada as a 'reliable ally' to the post-2022 framework of $73 billion in Defence Policy Update commitments — a trajectory better described as 'from agenda-setter to agenda-recipient' than as straightforward decline."

[8]A + E · Inquiry Application
Major Application Task — Design a political-inquiry project on a current Canadian or international political issue (e.g., section 33 future, AI governance, reconciliation implementation, Arctic security, post-COVID pandemic preparedness, climate-finance commitments).

Provide:
(a) one open-ended political-inquiry question (2 marks),
(b) two primary sources you would consult (with provenance — where and how to find them) (2 marks),
(c) two secondary sources you would engage (with citations) (2 marks),
(d) the concept of political thinking most relevant to your inquiry, with a 2-sentence justification (2 marks).
(a) Question: open-ended, specific, bounded, defensible by evidence. (b) Primary sources: parliamentary record (Hansard, Library of Parliament reports), Supreme Court decisions, government department publications, official UN/WHO/UNHCR data, party platforms, leadership speeches. (c) Secondary sources: peer-reviewed academic publications (Canadian Public Policy, Canadian Journal of Political Science, Canadian Journal of Public Health, Canadian Foreign Policy Journal), Yellowhead Institute, the Macdonald-Laurier Institute, IRPP Choices, recognised public-policy think tanks. (d) Concept: significance, objectives-and-results, stability-and-change, or political-perspective — justification must explain why this concept best fits the question. Marks as above; reward sophistication in primary-source provenance and secondary-source quality.

[5]A + B–E · Cross-Strand Application
Application — Cross-Strand: In 6-8 sentences, identify one substantive thematic connection between two strands of CPW4U. For example: the relationship between Indigenous-rights advocacy (Strand C — UNDRIP, TRC, section 35) and Canadian foreign-policy positioning (Strand E — UNDRIP international advocacy, Canada's R2P role). Or: the relationship between political ideologies (Strand B — populism, conservatism) and global trade fragmentation (Strand E — tariff politics, WTO degradation). Use specific evidence from both sides.
Strong responses identify a substantive thematic connection, use specific evidence from each of the two strands, and produce a synthesis that does work beyond restating the parallel. Examples: (1) Indigenous-rights (C) and Canadian foreign policy (E) — the 2021 UNDRIP Act operates as both domestic constitutional architecture and international-positioning instrument; Canada hosted the 2022 COP15 (biodiversity) with Indigenous-led conservation prominent; (2) Populism (B) and trade fragmentation (E) — the 2018-25 post-Brexit, Trump, Bolsonaro, Modi wave shows ideological populism producing trade-policy reversals that fragment the WTO Appellate Body and re-erect tariffs; the 2024-25 Trump tariffs against Canada illustrate the domestic political logic. (3) Charter rights (B) and refugee protection (C) — the 2019 Frank v. Canada SCC ruling extended voting rights to non-resident citizens, including refugees who became citizens, illustrating how Charter section 3 expansion intersects with refugee-integration policy. Marks: 1 connection identification, 2 evidence on each side (2), 1 synthesis, 1 sophistication.

[7]A · Course-Wide Application
Final Synthesis: In 8-10 sentences, articulate the most important insight you take from CPW4U about Canadian politics, world politics, or both. Draw on at least three of the five strands (A, B, C, D, E). Use one specific example from a text, document, court case, or event. Identify one way this insight will transfer beyond political-science class — to civic life, future university studies, or current events.
K/U (2): accurate use of three strands. Thinking (2): substantive insight, not generic. Communication (2): clear organisation, specific examples, register. Application (1): explicit transfer to non-classroom context. Strong responses tie a structural insight (e.g., the contingent nature of rights protection; the long arc of reconciliation; the imperfect-but-real value of multilateralism; the role of political institutions in mediating ideological conflict) to a defensible claim about civic responsibility, university-study direction, or contemporary engagement. Examples of strong syntheses: (1) "Canadian political institutions are remarkable for their resilience but vulnerable in ways the post-1982 settlement did not foresee — the post-2018 expansion of section 33, the 2024-25 carbon-pricing reversal, and the 2022 Convoy invocation of the Emergencies Act each illustrate the continuing need for active civic engagement to preserve institutional health." (2) "International cooperation is harder than at any time since 1945 but no less necessary, and the political choices we make as citizens, voters, and future professionals will shape what comes next." (3) "Indigenous nationhood predates and survives the Canadian state — the 1763 Royal Proclamation, the 1763 Treaty of Niagara, the 1982 section 35 entrenchment, and the 2021 UNDRIP Act together represent not concessions but recognition of a constitutional reality that political theory is still catching up to." Marks: as above.
