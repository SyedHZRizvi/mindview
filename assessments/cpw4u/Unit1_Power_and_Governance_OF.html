Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) Max Weber's classical definition of the state; (b) the Peace of Westphalia (1648); (c) the Montevideo Convention (1933).
(a) Weber — the state is "a human community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory" (Politics as a Vocation, 1919). (b) Westphalia 1648 — peace treaty ending the Thirty Years' War; founding moment of the modern territorial state system; principles of cuius regio and non-intervention. (c) Montevideo 1933 — codified four legal criteria of statehood: defined territory, settled population, government, capacity to enter international relations.
2
[2]
Hannah Arendt's three defining features of totalitarianism are:
B. Arendt's Origins of Totalitarianism (1951) — ideology, mass party, terror. Authoritarianism shares some features but tolerates depoliticised private life; totalitarianism does not.
3
[3]
In 2–3 sentences, distinguish between section 91 and section 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867. Give one example of a power in each.
Section 91 enumerates federal legislative powers (trade and commerce, criminal law, banking, immigration, defence, POGG); section 92 enumerates provincial legislative powers (property and civil rights, municipalities, hospitals, administration of justice, education in s.93). Examples: federal — criminal law (s.91(27)) and POGG; provincial — property and civil rights (s.92(13)).
4
[3]
Define each: (a) responsible government; (b) the Oakes test (1986); (c) section 33 of the Charter.
(a) Responsible government — the doctrine that the executive (Cabinet) must hold the confidence of the elected lower house; if it loses a confidence vote it must resign or seek dissolution. (b) Oakes test — the two-stage section 1 framework: the law's objective must be pressing and substantial; the means must be proportional (rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionate effect). (c) Section 33 — the "notwithstanding clause"; allows Parliament or a provincial legislature to declare a law operates notwithstanding sections 2 and 7–15 for renewable five-year terms.

[4]
Identify two SCC cases (with year) that have shaped Canadian Charter doctrine, and briefly state the constitutional principle each established.
Examples (any two): R. v. Oakes (1986) — section 1 limits framework; R. v. Morgentaler (1988) — section 7 reproductive autonomy; Andrews v. LSBC (1989) — substantive-equality test for section 15; Vriend v. Alberta (1998) — sexual orientation as analogous ground under section 15; R. v. Sharpe (2001) — section 2(b); R. v. Latimer (2001) — section 12; Sauvé v. Canada (2002) — prisoner voting; Reference re Same-Sex Marriage (2004); Carter v. Canada (2015) — section 7 physician-assisted dying; R. v. Jordan (2016) — section 11(b) trial-delay ceilings; R. v. Sharma (2022) — section 15 Indigenous offenders; Reference re GGPPA (2021) — POGG/carbon pricing. Marks: 2 per case (1 case, 1 principle).
Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[6]
"The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law of Canada, and any law that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect." — Section 52(1), Constitution Act, 1982
Analyse the constitutional implications of section 52(1). Apply the concept of stability and change: how has the relationship between Parliament and the courts evolved since 1982, and what role does section 33 play in moderating that relationship? (6–7 sentences)
Section 52(1) makes the Constitution supreme law and authorises judicial review. Pre-1982, judicial review existed for federal-provincial division of powers (since Hodge v. The Queen 1883) but rights review was narrow. Post-1982, the SCC has become a major political actor — Oakes 1986, Morgentaler 1988, Vriend 1998, Carter 2015 each reshaped policy. Section 33 was the negotiated price of provincial agreement to 1982 — it preserves residual parliamentary sovereignty on sections 2 and 7–15 for renewable five-year periods. Stability: judicial review has remained the dominant mode of Charter enforcement; the "dialogue theory" (Hogg & Bushell 1997) describes Court-Parliament iteration. Change: section 33 was rarely used 1982–2015 but has become more frequent (Quebec Bill 21 2019, Bill 96 2021, Ontario back-to-work 2021), suggesting a return to a more parliamentary-sovereignty model in some jurisdictions. Marks: 2 for section 52 analysis, 2 for stability-and-change application, 2 for section 33 explanation, 1 for examples.
7
[5]
Apply the concept of political perspective: in 5 sentences, evaluate Quebec's Act respecting the laicity of the State (Bill 21, 2019). Identify two distinct legitimate political perspectives on the law and the constitutional principles each prioritises.
Bill 21 prohibits certain public-sector employees in positions of authority from wearing religious symbols; it explicitly invokes section 33 to operate notwithstanding ss. 2 and 7–15. Two perspectives: (1) Quebec-nationalist / Westminster-traditional perspective — supports the law as a democratic expression of Quebec's distinct society and a legitimate use of legislative sovereignty under section 33; emphasises parliamentary supremacy. (2) Civil-liberties / equality perspective — argues the law violates section 15 (disproportionate impact on Muslim women) and section 2(a) (religious freedom); emphasises Charter rights and substantive equality. A third nuance: the prospective SCC reference (the English Montreal School Board appeal) will define the future scope of section 33. Marks: 1 description of law, 2 first perspective, 2 second perspective.
8
[4]
Apply the concept of objectives and results: in 4 sentences, evaluate Reference re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act (SCC 2021). What was the federal government's stated objective? What did the SCC conclude? What were the political/legal results?
Objective: federal carbon-pricing backstop ($20/t in 2019 rising to $170/t in 2030) to ensure a minimum national carbon price. SCC conclusion (6–3, Wagner CJ majority): climate change is a "matter of national concern" under POGG because effects transcend provincial boundaries; federal jurisdiction therefore valid. Results: carbon pricing operated as backstop in non-pricing provinces 2019-25; consumer levy was dismantled in March 2025 by the new Liberal government; industrial pricing (OBPS) remains. The dissenting judges (Brown, Côté, Rowe) warned the decision threatens federalism's balance. Marks: 1 objective, 1 SCC conclusion, 1 result, 1 dissent/nuance.

Write a coherent analytical paragraph (7–9 sentences) on the claim: "The Westminster parliamentary system gives Canadian executives more concentrated power than presidential systems give to their executives." Use specific Canadian and comparative examples; embed at least one historical or contemporary case.
Look for: (1) a clear thesis agreeing, disagreeing, or qualifying the claim; (2) Canadian example — the 2008 prorogation crisis, the use of omnibus budget bills, party discipline; (3) comparative example — US gridlock (Biden-Republican House 2023-24; Trump 2025 transition); (4) institutional analysis — Westminster fusion of executive/legislative vs. US separation of powers; (5) qualification — Westminster PM is dependent on caucus and confidence; US president has constitutional checks and term limits; the answer is not symmetrically "concentrated" but distributed differently; (6) analytical close — the comparison depends on which dimension of power (legislative-passing, war-making, judicial appointment, foreign policy). Marks: 1 thesis, 2 Canadian evidence, 2 comparative evidence, 1 qualification, 1 communication.
10
[8]
Write a structured 8–10 sentence response analysing the Canadian "dialogue theory" of judicial review (Hogg and Bushell, 1997). Use specific examples of dialogue from Canadian constitutional history; assess whether the theory accurately describes the Canadian model; identify one limitation.
Strong responses: (1) define dialogue theory — the iterative Court-Parliament exchange; (2) give at least two examples — Morgentaler 1988 → Bill C-43 1989-91 → continued open framework; Carter 2015 → Bill C-14 2016 → Truchon 2019 → Bill C-7 2021 → 2027 MAID extension; R. v. Jordan 2016 → court-capacity investment; (3) assess accuracy — dialogue is real but uneven (Parliament cannot always respond; some areas have no legislative response); (4) limitation — the dialogue depends on parliamentary capacity and political will; section 33 represents a parliamentary override of dialogue, not a dialogue mechanism; (5) recognition that the US "judicial supremacy" model is the contrast. Marks: 2 definition, 3 examples, 1 assessment, 1 limitation, 1 communication.
Part D: Application [15 marks]
11
[6]
In April 2024, Saskatchewan passed the Parents' Bill of Rights (Bill 137) requiring schools to obtain parental consent before changing pronouns or names used for students under 16, and pre-emptively invoking section 33 of the Charter. The Saskatchewan Court of King's Bench in February 2024 (UR Pride) had granted an injunction.
Apply your understanding of section 33, the Charter, and judicial review. In 6–7 sentences, analyse: (a) the constitutional issue at stake; (b) the legitimacy of pre-emptive section 33 use; (c) one defensible argument for the law AND one defensible argument against. Take a position.
Constitutional issue: Bill 137 implicates section 15 (equality, with sexual orientation/gender identity as protected grounds) and section 7 (security of person); the pre-emptive section 33 invocation prevents the SCC from ruling on the Charter compliance directly. Pre-emptive use: the original 1982 negotiations contemplated section 33 as a response to a Court ruling, not as a pre-emptive policy tool; pre-emptive use is constitutionally permitted (section 33 doesn't require prior judicial ruling) but politically contested. For: elected legislatures should be able to set policy on contested social issues; parental consent reflects traditional family-authority values; section 33 is part of the constitutional bargain. Against: minor rights (especially of transgender youth) are typically protected against majoritarian preferences; pre-emptive section 33 use erodes Charter protection; the Saskatchewan court found a serious issue and granted injunction. Position: students should take a clear position and defend it with reference to constitutional principles. Marks: 2 constitutional issue, 1 pre-emptive analysis, 1 for argument, 1 against argument, 1 position/synthesis.
12
[5]
Application: Apply the concept of political significance: in 5 sentences, evaluate the political significance of the 2014 SCC Nadon Reference (rejection of Justice Nadon's appointment to the Supreme Court). What constitutional principle did it establish? What does it imply about judicial independence?
In Nadon Reference (2014), the SCC rejected the Harper government's appointment of Federal Court Justice Marc Nadon to one of the three Quebec seats on the SCC, holding that section 6 of the Supreme Court Act required Quebec justices to be from the Quebec bar or current Quebec courts. Significance: (1) the SCC asserted its own institutional structure as an unwritten constitutional principle; (2) the decision limited prime-ministerial discretion in SCC appointments; (3) it required reformulation of the SCC appointment process (which Trudeau reformed in 2016); (4) it confirmed Quebec's distinctive role within the federation. The decision illustrates judicial independence as both individual (security of tenure) and institutional (the court's own structural integrity). Marks: 2 description, 2 significance analysis, 1 communication.
