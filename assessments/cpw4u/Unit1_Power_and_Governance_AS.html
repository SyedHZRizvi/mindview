Topics 1.1–1.2 — State, Sovereignty, and Political Systems
Question 1
According to Max Weber's classical definition, a state is "a human community that successfully claims":
Solution: Weber's 1919 lecture "Politics as a Vocation" gives the canonical definition. The state is constituted by the monopoly of legitimate violence within a territory — not by cultural unity, UN recognition, or economic dominance.
Question 2
The 1933 Montevideo Convention codified four legal criteria of statehood. They are:
Solution: The Montevideo Convention specified territory, population, government, and capacity for international relations. These remain the standard legal test of statehood, invoked in debates over Kosovo, Palestine, and Taiwan.
Question 3
"Society in every state is a blessing, but Government, even in its best state, is but a necessary evil." — Thomas Paine, Common Sense, 1776
Paine's view of the state is closest to which contemporary classification?
Solution: Paine's "necessary evil" view of government reflects classical-liberal scepticism of state power — government is needed but should be limited. This is the lineage from Locke through Jefferson to Mill and contemporary libertarians.
Question 4
Hannah Arendt's three defining features of totalitarianism (1951) are:
Solution: Arendt's Origins of Totalitarianism identifies ideology, mass party, and terror as the distinguishing features. Authoritarianism shares some features but tolerates a depoliticised private life; totalitarianism does not.
Topics 1.3–1.4 — Canadian Federalism and Westminster
Question 5
In the Constitution Act, 1867, section 91 lists:
Solution: Section 91 enumerates federal powers; section 92 enumerates provincial. POGG ("peace, order, and good government") is the federal residual / national-concern power, expanded in the 2021 Reference re GGPPA carbon-pricing case.
Question 6
In a Westminster parliamentary system, the Prime Minister:
Solution: In Westminster systems (Canada, UK, Australia, India), the public elects MPs; the PM is the party leader who can command confidence in the House. Canadians don't directly elect the PM — a common misconception.
Question 7
In December 2008, Prime Minister Stephen Harper sought prorogation of Parliament. This was politically significant because:
Solution: Governor General Michaëlle Jean granted prorogation after a 2-hour delay. The unprecedented use to avoid a confidence vote sparked debate about the GG's reserve powers and the limits of executive prerogative in Westminster systems.
Topic 1.5 — The Charter
Question 8
In R. v. Oakes (1986), the Supreme Court established the section 1 test. A Charter-infringing law is justified if:
Solution: The Oakes test has two stages: a pressing/substantial objective, and proportional means (rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionality of effect). Almost every major Charter case turns on whether section 1 saves the impugned law.
Question 9
Section 33 of the Charter (the "notwithstanding clause"):
Solution: Section 33 allows legislatures to override sections 2 and 7–15 (but not 3-6, 16-23, 28) for renewable five-year terms. It was rarely used before 2015 but has become more common (Quebec Bill 21 2019, Bill 96 2021, Ontario back-to-work 2021).
Topic 1.6 — Judicial Review
Question 10
The "dialogue theory" of Canadian judicial review (Hogg and Bushell, 1997) holds that:
Solution: Dialogue theory describes how Court-Parliament exchange shapes Canadian rights jurisprudence. Examples: post-Morgentaler Bill C-43 (failed); post-Carter Bill C-14; post-Jordan court-capacity investment. Distinguishes the Canadian model from the more confrontational US model.
Question 11
In Reference re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act (2021), the SCC held that the federal carbon-pricing law:
Solution: The 2021 ruling upheld federal carbon pricing as a valid POGG "national concern" matter — climate change has effects across provincial boundaries that no single province can address. The decision was 6–3, with strong dissents on federalism grounds.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain why section 33 has become more politically prominent since 2018 and what political-thinking concept best captures the development.
Sample: Since 2018, section 33 has been invoked far more frequently — most prominently in Quebec's Bill 21 (2019) and Bill 96 (2021), and in pre-emptive Saskatchewan and Ontario uses. The development reflects a shift in how provincial legislatures see the balance between parliamentary sovereignty (a Westminster value) and Charter-rights protection (a liberal-democratic value). The political-thinking concept of stability and change best captures it: the section 33 norm of restraint that prevailed 1982–2015 has changed substantially, and the change has consequences for the future of Canadian constitutional politics.
