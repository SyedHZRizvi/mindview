Unit 1: Power and Governance — Diagnostic

Assessment FOR Learning · Prerequisite check · Strand B — B1.1, B1.2, B1.3
Not Graded — Teacher Feedback Only
Purpose: Identify gaps in your prior knowledge of Canadian political institutions, the Charter, and comparative government before starting Unit 1. You will receive descriptive feedback, not a grade. Be honest about what is unclear — that is the point of a diagnostic.
Question 1 Prereq · Civics
[3 marks]
Briefly define each: (a) Parliament; (b) Prime Minister; (c) Governor General. What is the role of each in the Canadian system?
Question 2 Prereq · Constitution
[3 marks]
In 2–3 sentences, describe what you know about Canada's Constitution. What is patriation? What is the Charter?
Question 3 Prereq · Federalism
[3 marks]
"The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law of Canada, and any law that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect." — Section 52(1), Constitution Act, 1982
What does this section say about the relationship between the Constitution and ordinary legislation? What does it imply about judicial review?
Question 4 Prior Knowledge
[3 marks]
Name three rights you know are protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Where appropriate, identify the section number. What does it mean for a right to be "Charter-protected"?
Question 5 Skills · Comparative
[4 marks]
Compare a parliamentary democracy (like Canada or the UK) with a presidential democracy (like the US). Identify two key institutional differences and one consequence of each difference.
Question 6 Skills · Critical Thinking
[3 marks]
A friend says: "Democracies are inherently better than authoritarian governments because they protect freedom." In 3–4 sentences, identify one strong argument for this view AND one important nuance or qualification that political theory raises.
Question 7 Reflection
[ungraded]
Which of these Unit 1 topics do you feel least confident about: (a) the modern state and sovereignty; (b) types of political systems; (c) Canadian federalism; (d) Westminster vs. presidential; (e) the Charter and section 33; (f) judicial review and the dialogue with Parliament? Explain what specifically is unclear.
📖 Sample Answers and Marking Rubric (Instructor View)

Q1 (3 marks): Parliament — bicameral federal legislature (House of Commons elected, Senate appointed); makes federal laws. PM — head of government; leader of party able to command House confidence; chairs Cabinet. GG — Crown's representative in Canada; gives Royal Assent; appoints PM; in extraordinary circumstances may exercise reserve powers. (1 each)

Q2 (3 marks): Constitution = Constitution Acts 1867 + 1982 + unwritten conventions. Patriation = 1982 transfer of constitutional amendment power from UK Parliament to Canada with new amending formula. Charter = Part I of Constitution Act 1982; entrenched rights binding government action. (1 each)

Q3 (3 marks): Section 52(1) makes the Constitution supreme law; unconstitutional legislation is "of no force or effect"; implies courts have power and duty of judicial review for constitutional compliance. (1 supremacy, 1 invalidation, 1 judicial review implication)

Q4 (3 marks): Examples: fundamental freedoms s.2 (expression, religion); democratic rights s.3 (voting); legal rights ss.7–14 (life liberty security, search/seizure, counsel); equality s.15; language ss.16–22. "Charter-protected" = the Charter binds government action and unconstitutional infringements can be remedied through judicial review unless saved by s.1 or invoked through s.33. (1 per right + 1 for binding-government clarification)

Q5 (4 marks): Differences: parliamentary fuses executive/legislative (responsible government) vs. presidential separates them; PM is leader of legislative party vs. President is directly elected; PM-led Cabinet vs. Cabinet of presidential appointees; no fixed term in parliamentary (confidence convention) vs. fixed term in presidential. Consequences: parliamentary allows rapid action and quick changes of government but concentrates executive power in the PM; presidential provides separation-of-powers checks but can produce gridlock. (1 per institutional difference + 1 per consequence)

Q6 (3 marks): Argument for: democracies protect basic rights, allow peaceful change of government, generally produce better long-term outcomes. Nuance: (i) "democracy" is contested — Dahl's polyarchy requires more than elections; (ii) democracies can have illiberal currents (rights violations of minorities); (iii) some authoritarian regimes have produced economic development (Singapore, China since 1978 — though at clear rights costs); (iv) democracy requires social and economic preconditions; etc. (1 argument, 1 nuance, 1 sophistication of qualification)

Q7 (ungraded): Use to inform teaching priorities. Common gaps: distinguishing s.1 from s.33; understanding POGG; the dialogue theory.

Marking guidance: This is a diagnostic, not a graded assessment. Use codes: "Mastered" (clear understanding); "Developing" (partial); "Needs Foundation" (significant gaps). Direct students to specific lesson notes for foundation gaps.
