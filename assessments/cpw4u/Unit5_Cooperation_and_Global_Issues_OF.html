Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) the UN Charter (1945); (b) the UN Security Council; (c) the P5.
(a) UN Charter — founding treaty of the United Nations; signed San Francisco 26 June 1945; entered force 24 October 1945; 111 articles. (b) UN Security Council — UN principal organ with primary responsibility for international peace and security; 15 members (5 permanent + 10 elected non-permanent); Chapter VII gives binding authority over sanctions and use of force. (c) P5 — Permanent Five with veto power: China, France, Russia, UK, USA; ensures no UN coercive action over active opposition.
2
[2]
The Paris Agreement (2015) requires each state to:
B. Paris replaced Kyoto's top-down structure with a bottom-up NDC model. Each state determines its own commitments; submits every five years; commitments must show progression; there is no enforcement mechanism. Headline target: "well below 2°C" + pursue 1.5°C.
3
[3]
Define each: (a) ICJ; (b) ICC; (c) WTO.
(a) ICJ — International Court of Justice; UN principal judicial organ; tries cases between states; in The Hague; 15 judges; jurisdiction is consent-based. (b) ICC — International Criminal Court; created by Rome Statute (1998, in force 2002); tries individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression; 124 state parties as of 2024. (c) WTO — World Trade Organization; established 1995 (Marrakesh Agreement 1994); 164 members; administers global trade rules; Appellate Body effectively shut down since 2017.
4
[3]
Define each: (a) the WHO; (b) the IHR (2005); (c) PHEIC.
(a) WHO — World Health Organization; UN specialised agency since 1948; 194 member states; HQ Geneva; coordinates international public health responses, sets norms (Essential Medicines List, FCTC). (b) IHR (2005) — International Health Regulations; binding treaty requiring states to report outbreaks; under 2024-25 reform. (c) PHEIC — Public Health Emergency of International Concern; WHO Director-General declaration triggering coordinated response; recent examples include H1N1 2009, Ebola 2014-16, Zika 2016, COVID-19 30 Jan 2020 – 5 May 2023.
5
[4]
Briefly describe four major Canadian trade agreements or international institutions (E2 mention: G7/G20/USMCA/CPTPP/CETA/AfCFTA observership). For each: name; year; key feature.
Examples (any 4): USMCA (Canada-US-Mexico Agreement, 2020) — trilateral free trade; replaced NAFTA 1994; subject to 6-year review next 2026. CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, 2018) — 11 Pacific economies. CETA (Canada-EU Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement, 2017 provisional, 2024 fully ratified). G7 — Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, UK, USA; established 1976; informal but consequential coordination. G20 — established 1999, leaders since 2008; major economy coordination. WTO (1995-) — Canada founding member; ~600 disputes since 1995. NATO (1949-) — Canada founding member; primary collective-defence commitment. UN (1945-) — Canada has been on UNSC 7 times (last 1999-2000). Marks: 1 per institution.
Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[6]
"The post-1945 multilateral order is under unprecedented stress." — adapted from contemporary commentary, 2025
Analyse this claim. Apply the concept of stability and change: in 6-8 sentences, identify the durable features of the multilateral order and the recent (post-2017) sources of stress. Take a position on whether the order can be reformed or must be replaced.
Durable features: UN Charter framework continues; ICJ and ICC functional; major multilateral treaties (UDHR, Paris Agreement, NPT) remain in force; trade interdependence remains substantial. Sources of stress: WTO Appellate Body shutdown since 2017 (US blocked appointments); Doha Round abandoned; tariff politics 2024-25; UNSC paralysis over Syria 2011-24 and Ukraine 2022-; Russia-Ukraine war challenge to European security; US-China strategic rivalry; post-Trump 2.0 transactional foreign-policy approach; climate target trajectory (1.5°C exceeded in 2024); pandemic preparedness gaps; AI governance lag. Reform vs. replacement: liberal-institutionalist view sees reform feasible; realist view sees multipolar adjustment underway; civilisational view sees BRICS+ alternative. A Level-4 position recognises that "reform vs. replacement" is too binary — the actual evolution is partial decay, partial renewal, partial creation of new institutions (BRICS+, AIIB, regional frameworks). Marks: 2 stability, 2 stress sources, 1 position-taking, 1 sophistication.
7
[5]
Apply the concept of objectives and results: in 5 sentences, evaluate the Paris Agreement (2015). What was the stated objective? What is the observed trajectory? What is one strength and one limitation?
Stated objective: hold the increase in global average temperature to "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels" and pursue 1.5°C. Observed trajectory: 2024 was the first calendar year on record to exceed 1.5°C of warming (per Copernicus, January 2025); current NDCs collectively put the trajectory closer to 2.7°C. Strength: nearly universal participation (198 parties); allows national-circumstance flexibility; produced major financial flows (Loss and Damage Fund 2022); has driven substantial decarbonisation investment. Limitation: no enforcement mechanism; commitments are non-binding; political reversibility (Trump 2017-20 US withdrawal; Canada 2025 carbon-pricing reversal); 1.5°C target effectively impossible without rapid acceleration. Marks: 1 objective, 1 trajectory, 1 strength, 1 limitation, 1 synthesis.
8
[4]
Apply the concept of political perspective: in 4 sentences, evaluate UN Security Council reform proposals. Identify the principal positions and assess one obstacle.
Principal positions: G4 (Brazil, Germany, India, Japan) — adding permanent seats with veto for themselves and African representation; Uniting for Consensus (Italy, Pakistan, South Korea, Mexico, Argentina, Egypt) — expanding non-permanent seats without new vetoes; African Union Ezulwini Consensus (2005) — two permanent African seats with veto, plus expanded non-permanent. P5 position: cautious of any reform that would dilute their position. Obstacle: Charter Article 108 requires reform to be ratified by 2/3 of UNGA + all P5 — making any substantive reform extraordinarily hard. Result: 20+ years of reform debate without charter amendment. Marks: 2 positions identified, 1 obstacle analysis, 1 synthesis.
Part C: Communication [15 marks]
9
[7]
Write a coherent analytical paragraph (7-9 sentences) on the claim: "The COVID-19 pandemic both tested and revealed the limits of global health governance." Use specific examples from the WHO, COVAX, the TRIPS waiver debate, and Canadian or G7 response.
Look for: (1) clear thesis (agreement, disagreement, or qualification); (2) WHO PHEIC declaration timeline (30 Jan 2020 declaration; widely criticised as slow but legally correct); IHR 2005 compliance gaps documented; (3) COVAX delivered ~2B doses to ~146 countries 2020-23 but was strained by vaccine nationalism; high-income countries had 2-3× more doses than low-income by mid-2021; (4) TRIPS waiver South Africa/India proposal 2020 — limited Ministerial Decision MC12 2022 covering patents only, excluding therapeutics; (5) Canadian response — ~$82B CERB; 414M doses procurement (most diverse portfolio internationally); ~120M doses donated to COVAX; National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI); 2023 PHAC restructuring; (6) post-COVID architecture — 2022 Pandemic Accord negotiations; 2024 Pandemic Fund $2B+; 2024 federal pandemic-preparedness investment $573M; (7) analytical close — global health governance is real but conditional on state political will and resource flows. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 WHO/COVAX evidence, 2 Canadian evidence, 1 analysis, 1 communication.
10
[8]
Write a structured 9-11 sentence response analysing Canada's "middle power" foreign-policy identity in the 2020s. Compare the Pearson-era invention of UN peacekeeping (1956) with contemporary Canadian engagement (Indo-Pacific Strategy 2022; Africa Strategy 2023; Defence Policy Update 2024). Take a position on whether Canada can sustain middle-power influence in a polarised international system.
Look for: (1) Pearson-era characterisation: middle-power multilateralism, normative innovation, UN-focused, peacekeeping invention. (2) Contemporary engagement: Indo-Pacific Strategy 2022 ($2.3B over 5 years); Africa Strategy 2023; Defence Policy Update 2024 ($73B+); Operation Reassurance (Latvia battle group expanded to brigade); CETA full ratification 2024; substantial CUAET (~210K Ukrainians) and Afghan resettlement. (3) Tensions: peacekeeping numbers at historical low (~33 deployed 2024); 2% GDP NATO target target by 2032 only; reduced international development assistance as % of GNI (~0.31% in 2023, down from peak); 2024 Indo-Pacific implementation slowed by India-Canada diplomatic crisis. (4) Position — defensible either direction. Sustainable: substantial institutional engagement; diversified multilateral footprint; 2.3% world GDP gives material influence; broad public consensus on multilateralism. Unsustainable: declining substantive resources; polarised global system reduces middle-power maneuvering space; US-China rivalry forces choosing; Indigenous-rights and reconciliation framework reshapes foreign-policy legitimacy. Marks: 2 Pearson era, 2 contemporary engagement, 2 tensions, 2 position with evidence.
Part D: Application [15 marks]
11
[7]
In 2024, the Bletchley Declaration and Seoul Statement on AI safety produced the first multilateral commitments on frontier AI; the EU AI Act began phased entry into force in 2025; the 2024 OECD AI Principles update integrated generative AI considerations.
Apply your understanding of multilateral cooperation. In 7-8 sentences: (a) why is AI governance a "global issue"; (b) which existing institutions address it; (c) which gaps remain; (d) take a position on whether Canada should be a leading voice in AI governance and how.
(a) Global issue: frontier AI capabilities can scale rapidly across borders; safety risks (autonomous weapons, biological-weapons information, election interference, deepfake harms) are transnational; economic effects (labour-market disruption, productivity gains) affect all countries; no single state can govern unilaterally. (b) Existing institutions: Bletchley Declaration 2023 (29 states); Seoul Statement 2024; OECD AI Principles (2019, 2024 update); EU AI Act 2024; UN's AI Advisory Body 2024; G7 Hiroshima AI Code of Conduct 2023. (c) Gaps: no binding international treaty; major gaps between democratic and authoritarian governance approaches; private-sector frontier-AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, xAI) have disproportionate technical capacity; uneven verification and compliance. (d) Position: defensible either direction. For Canadian leadership: Canada has strong AI research community (Vector Institute Toronto, Mila Montreal); Yoshua Bengio is a senior figure in the field; CSIRO and CRTC have governance capacity; UK and EU have moved further. Against leadership: Canada lacks the regulatory enforcement scale of EU; private-sector AI capacity is concentrated in US; Canadian middle-power influence is limited by resource constraints. Marks: 1 global issue, 1 institutions, 1 gaps, 2 position taking, 2 evidence.

In 5-6 sentences: (a) identify the challenge; (b) name which UN organ or institution would primarily address it; (c) apply one concept of political thinking; (d) take an informed position.
Examples: (1) Sudan civil war 2023- (RSF-SAF; ~10M displaced; famine warnings; ICJ The Gambia v. UAE proceedings; UNSC; African Union; IGAD; UN Department of Peace Operations). Position: Canada should increase humanitarian assistance + diplomatic engagement + sanctions on RSF leadership. (2) Arctic governance — Arctic Council (1996); UNCLOS continental-shelf disputes; climate-driven accessibility; Russian Northern Fleet; Chinese polar ambitions; apply stability-and-change (the institutional regime has been remarkably stable but is under stress); position varies. (3) Critical minerals supply — IEA Critical Minerals Outlook; the 2022 Minerals Security Partnership; Canadian Critical Minerals Strategy; apply objectives-and-results; position varies. Marks: 1 challenge identification, 1 institution, 1 concept, 2 position with evidence.
