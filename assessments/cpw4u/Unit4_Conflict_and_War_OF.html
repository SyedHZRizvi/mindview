Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) Waltz's three levels of analysis; (b) the security dilemma; (c) democratic peace theory.
(a) Waltz's three levels — individual (psychology, leader choices), state (regime type, internal politics), system (anarchic international structure). (b) Security dilemma (Herz 1950, Jervis 1978) — in anarchic systems, defensive actions by one state appear offensive to others; produces arms races between states that may both prefer peace. (c) Democratic peace theory — Doyle 1983, Russett 1993; claim that mature democracies do not fight one another; contested empirically and conceptually but influential.
2
[2]
The Geneva Conventions of 1949 are:
B. The four 1949 Geneva Conventions (with 1977 Additional Protocols I and II) form the core of IHL. Their universal ratification makes them among the most accepted international treaties.
3
[3]
Define each: (a) jus ad bellum; (b) jus in bello; (c) jus post bellum.
(a) Jus ad bellum — justice of going to war; criteria include just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, reasonable chance of success. (b) Jus in bello — justice in war; principles of discrimination (non-combatants protected) and proportionality (means proportionate to military objective). (c) Jus post bellum — justice after war; peace terms, reconstruction obligations, accountability for war crimes, reparations.
4
[3]
Define each: (a) R2P (Responsibility to Protect); (b) the ICISS; (c) the 2005 World Summit Outcome Document.
(a) R2P — sovereignty-as-responsibility framework for preventing mass atrocities (genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity); three pillars (state responsibility; international assistance; collective response). (b) ICISS — International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty, chaired by Gareth Evans and Mohamed Sahnoun on a Canadian government initiative; reported December 2001. (c) 2005 World Summit Outcome Document — paragraphs 138-139 unanimously adopted R2P by the UN General Assembly.
5
[4]
Identify and briefly describe four key Canadian military or peacekeeping operations or institutions from 1949 to 2025.
Examples (any 4): NATO membership (1949–) — founding member; Article 2 ("Canadian article"); current Article 5 invocation post-9/11. NORAD (1958–) — binational US-Canada continental air defence; 2022 $38.6B modernisation. UNEF/Suez 1956 — Pearson's invention of UN peacekeeping; 1957 Nobel. Cyprus peacekeeping (1964-93) — long-standing CAF deployment. Former Yugoslavia (1992-95) — UNPROFOR; ~2,000 CAF deployed. Rwanda UNAMIR (1993-94) — Dallaire's command; warnings ignored. Afghanistan 2001-14 — ~40,000 rotated, 158 dead, largest deployment since WWII. Operation Reassurance 2017– — NATO Latvia battle group. Indo-Pacific Strategy 2022 — $2.3B 5-year commitment. Defence Policy Update 2024 — 2032 target to reach NATO 2% GDP target. Marks: 1 per operation/institution (name + brief description).
Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[6]
"NATO is not weakened; NATO is restored. And that is the legacy of the Russian invasion of Ukraine." — adapted from contemporary commentary, 2024
Analyse this claim. Apply the concept of stability and change: in 6-8 sentences, evaluate NATO's evolution since 1949. What is the durable feature? What has changed in response to Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine?
Durable features: NATO has been continuously the centerpiece of Western collective defence since 1949; Article 5 collective-defence commitment remains; bureaucratic and military infrastructure continues; the alliance has had only one Article 5 invocation (post-9/11). Changes since 2022: Finland joined 2023; Sweden joined 2024 (both abandoning long-standing neutrality); the Enhanced Forward Presence has expanded to brigade strength on the eastern flank (Canada-led Latvia battle group); NATO defence spending has risen — 23 of 32 members at 2% GDP target by 2024 (vs. ~6 in 2014); the alliance has resumed conventional-defence planning largely abandoned in the 1990s; the Indo-Pacific has been added to NATO's strategic frame. Stability: alliance has reasserted its core mission; change: it has been reorganised for great-power competition. Marks: 2 stability identification, 3 changes identified, 1 analytical synthesis.
7
[5]
Apply the concept of political perspective: in 5 sentences, evaluate Canada's defence spending (current ~1.4% GDP; 2032 target 2% NATO commitment). Identify two distinct legitimate political perspectives on Canadian defence spending.
Two perspectives: (1) NATO commitment / Atlanticist perspective — Canada has consistently underspent the 2% GDP NATO target; the 2014 Wales Summit commitment was political; failure undermines alliance credibility and free-rides on US security; the 2024 Defence Policy Update's 2032 target is overdue and aggressive enough. (2) Fiscal / "smart spender" perspective — defence spending should be evaluated on outcomes, not GDP percentages; Canada contributes substantially through NORAD, intelligence-sharing (Five Eyes), training (Ukraine, Latvia), and operations; the 2% target is a political commitment, not a substantive military analysis; competing fiscal priorities (healthcare, housing, climate) matter. Both perspectives recognise that the Canadian fiscal commitment must rise; they differ on pace, mix, and rationale. Marks: 2 first perspective, 2 second perspective, 1 synthesis.
8
[4]
Apply the concept of political significance: in 4 sentences, evaluate the November 2024 ICC arrest warrants for Netanyahu and Gallant. Why is this politically significant for the international legal order? What is one contested aspect?
The November 2024 warrants represent the first ICC warrants against sitting Western-allied leaders, testing whether ICC obligations apply equally regardless of state alignment. Significance: tests the resilience of the 1998 Rome Statute project; affects state practice on cooperation; raises the cost of unconditional Western support. Contested aspect: US under both administrations rejected the warrants; some EU members affirmed cooperation (notably Belgium, Spain, Ireland); enforcement depends on Netanyahu's travel patterns and host-state choices. Israel rejects ICC jurisdiction (not a party). Canada has not stated a clear position. Marks: 1 description, 2 significance, 1 contested aspect.
Part C: Communication [15 marks]
9
[7]
Write a coherent analytical paragraph (7-9 sentences) on the claim: "The international response to civilian-protection crises has improved substantively since 1994." Use specific cases (Rwanda 1994, Bosnia 1992-95, Kosovo 1999, Libya 2011, Syria 2011-24, Ukraine 2022-).
Look for: (1) clear thesis (agreement, disagreement, or qualification); (2) cases — Rwanda 1994 (no intervention, 800K-1M dead); Bosnia 1992-95 (belated 1995 NATO intervention after Srebrenica); Kosovo 1999 ("illegal but legitimate" NATO intervention); Libya 2011 (R2P-authorised but expanded to regime change); Syria 2011-24 (UNSC blocked, no R2P action, 500K+ deaths); Ukraine 2022- (massive bilateral support, no UN-mandated military action); (3) trajectory analysis — civilian-protection norms have strengthened (R2P doctrinal development, ICC, ICTY/ICTR); enforcement remains conditional on great-power consensus; (4) qualification — Syria and Gaza show the limits even of stronger norms; (5) analytical close — improvement is real but incomplete and conditional. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 cases, 2 analysis, 1 qualification, 1 communication.
10
[8]
Write a structured 9-11 sentence response analysing the just-war framework's application to the post-October 2023 Israel-Hamas-Gaza war. Apply jus ad bellum, jus in bello, and jus post bellum branches. Identify the strongest considerations on each side. Take a position.
Look for: (1) framing — the war has multiple parties (Israel, Hamas, broader regional dimensions); the just-war framework applies to all. (2) Jus ad bellum: Hamas's 7 October 2023 attack was a clear initiation of hostilities; Israel's military response was framed as self-defence (UN Charter Article 51); contested on whether the response stays within the proportionality criterion. (3) Jus in bello: Hamas's targeting of Israeli civilians on 7 October was a clear violation of distinction; Israel's operations have been argued to target Hamas military infrastructure within civilian areas (a Hamas violation of distinction but not eliminating Israeli targeting obligations); civilian death toll (~46K+ Palestinian by Jan 2025) raises proportionality questions; ICJ 2024 provisional-measures order required Israel to prevent acts that could amount to genocide; ICC 2024 issued arrest warrants. (4) Jus post bellum: unclear; post-war Gaza governance, reconstruction, Israeli-Palestinian political relationship all unresolved. (5) Position: defensible either direction; the strongest analysis recognises violations by multiple parties and the inadequacy of binary "just/unjust" labels. Marks: 1 framing, 2 jus ad bellum, 2 jus in bello, 1 jus post bellum, 2 position with evidence.
Part D: Application [15 marks]
11
[7]
In 2024, Canada and the United States announced major NORAD modernisation totalling $38.6 billion over 20 years, replacing the 1980s North Warning System with new over-the-horizon radar and air-domain awareness infrastructure.
Apply your understanding of Canada's defence posture. In 7-8 sentences: (a) explain why NORAD modernisation matters; (b) connect to Arctic sovereignty; (c) apply one concept of political thinking; (d) identify one consequence for Canadian foreign-policy autonomy. Take a position on whether NORAD modernisation strengthens or compromises Canadian sovereignty.
(a) Why matters: NORAD's surveillance infrastructure has been aging; Russia's modernised long-range bombers and hypersonic missiles, plus emerging Chinese aerospace capability, require updated air-domain awareness; cyber and space-domain awareness now part of NORAD mandate. (b) Arctic sovereignty connection: climate-driven Arctic accessibility opens new threats (Russian Northern Fleet activity, Chinese polar interests); NORAD modernisation includes Canadian Arctic infrastructure (over-the-horizon radar, airfields, surveillance); supports Canadian sovereignty claims. (c) Concept applied: stability-and-change shows that NORAD has been continuously bilateral since 1958 but its capabilities are being substantially modernised; the basic alliance architecture is durable. (d) Foreign-policy autonomy consequence: deeper integration with US air-defence systems, including those that may serve missile-defence functions; Canada's traditional non-participation in US ballistic-missile defence remains formally in place but is under pressure. Position: defensible either direction. Strengthens: Canada gains technological capability and Arctic surveillance that domestic resources alone could not provide; alliance provides force multiplier. Compromises: deeper integration may make independent Canadian foreign policy harder; cost (~$38.6B over 20 years; ~$19.3B Canadian share) is substantial. Marks: 1 why-matters, 1 Arctic connection, 1 concept application, 2 autonomy consequence, 2 position with evidence.

[5]
Application: Apply your understanding of R2P. In 5 sentences, analyse whether R2P could/should be invoked for one current crisis (e.g., Sudan 2023–; Myanmar Rohingya; DRC ongoing conflict; Ethiopia Tigray 2020-22; another contemporary case). Identify the case, apply R2P's three pillars, and take a position.
Examples: (1) Sudan 2023– (RSF-SAF civil war; documented mass atrocities; UN warnings of imminent famine and genocide in Darfur; UNSC Russia veto blocks coercive action). Apply R2P pillars: (i) Sudanese state has failed to protect; (ii) regional and international assistance has been limited; (iii) UNSC veto blocks pillar-3 collective coercive action. Position: R2P should be invoked through non-coercive mechanisms (sanctions, humanitarian assistance, refugee protection) where coercive measures are blocked. (2) Myanmar Rohingya (since 2017): same R2P logic; ICJ Gambia case 2019- shows alternative routes through international law. Marks: 1 case identification, 3 pillar application, 1 position.
13
[3]
Cross-strand application: Compare one feature of Canadian foreign policy in 2025 to a foreign-policy decision Canada made in 1956 (Suez/UNEF). In 3-4 sentences, identify one continuity and one change, with one analytical implication.
Continuity: middle-power multilateralism remains a Canadian self-conception; both 1956 UNEF and 2017 Operation Reassurance show Canada acting through international institutions. Change: 1956 Canada led normative innovation (Pearson's UNEF invention); 2025 Canada operates within an alliance-led framework (NATO, Indo-Pacific Strategy) with less originality. Implication: middle-power capacity in 2025 is real but more constrained; the era of distinctive Canadian foreign-policy initiative is harder to replicate in a more polarised international system. Marks: 1 continuity, 1 change, 1 implication.
