Topics 5.1–5.2 — UN System and International Courts
Question 1
The UN's six principal organs are:
Solution: The six principal organs are set out in the UN Charter. The Trusteeship Council was suspended 1994 after Palau independence. Specialised agencies (WHO, UNHCR, UNESCO, FAO, ILO, IAEA, UNICEF, UNDP) are separate from the principal organs.
Question 2
The Permanent Five (P5) of the UN Security Council are:
Solution: The P5 reflects 1945 geopolitics. Each holds veto power, ensuring no coercive UN action can proceed over active opposition of any P5 member. Reform proposals (G4: Brazil, Germany, India, Japan; African Union Ezulwini) have been blocked.
Question 3
"All Members shall settle their international disputes by peaceful means in such a manner that international peace and security, and justice, are not endangered." — Article 2(3), UN Charter
The ICJ (International Court of Justice):
Solution: The ICJ has 15 judges elected for 9 years; jurisdiction is consent-based; enforcement depends on UN Security Council. Recent cases include Bosnia v. Serbia (2007), Wall Advisory (2004), and South Africa v. Israel (2024). The ICJ is distinct from the ICC.
Question 4
The WTO Appellate Body has been:
Solution: The US blocked appointments to the Appellate Body starting 2017, leaving it without quorum. The Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA, 2020) provides limited appeal capacity for 51 members including Canada. The Doha Round has been effectively suspended.
Topics 5.3 — Climate Governance
Question 5
The Paris Agreement (2015, in force 2016) replaced Kyoto's "top-down" structure with:
Solution: NDCs are state-determined commitments, submitted every five years, with progression requirement but no enforcement mechanism. The Agreement's headline target is to hold warming "well below 2°C" and pursue 1.5°C; 2024 was the first calendar year to exceed 1.5°C of warming.
Question 6
Canada's 2025 carbon-pricing reversal:
Solution: The March 2025 suspension affected consumer-facing carbon pricing under the post-2018 federal backstop. Industrial pricing under OBPS remains in place. The shift reflects political reversal despite the SCC's 2021 Reference re GGPPA ruling upholding the federal jurisdiction.
Topic 5.4 — Global Health
Question 7
The 2005 International Health Regulations (IHR):
Solution: The 2005 IHR is binding on WHO member states. PHEICs declared include H1N1 (2009), Ebola West Africa (2014-16), Zika (2016), Ebola DRC (2019), COVID-19 (30 January 2020 to 5 May 2023), and mpox. The IHR is undergoing 2024-25 reform alongside Pandemic Accord negotiations.
Question 8
During COVID-19 (2020-23), the COVAX vaccine-equity facility:
Solution: COVAX delivered ~2 billion doses to ~146 countries 2020-23. Canada contributed ~120 million doses and substantial funding. The facility was strained by high-income-country vaccine nationalism and supply constraints.
Topics 5.5–5.6 — Migration, Trade, Future
Question 9
USMCA (2020, replacing NAFTA 1994):
Solution: USMCA (Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement) replaced NAFTA in 2020. It is subject to joint review every 6 years (next 2026) and termination if not renewed. Canada-US trade represents 65-75% of Canadian exports. The 2024-25 Trump-administration tariff threats test USMCA's durability.
Question 10
Canada admitted ~485,000 permanent residents in 2024 and ~1.2 million temporary residents (international students plus temporary workers). The 2024 federal cap on international study permits:
Solution: The 2024 study-permit cap (Marc Miller / Liberal government, January 2024) was a major policy shift after a decade of expansion. Coupled with restrictions on post-graduation work permits and temporary foreign workers, it reflects post-2023 political pressure on immigration scales.
Question 11
"Middle power" in Canadian foreign-policy discourse:
Solution: Middle power describes states like Canada (~1.5% of world GDP) that influence outcomes through multilateral institutions rather than unilateral dominance. Pearson's era epitomised this. Contemporary Canadian foreign policy continues to invoke middle-power identity even as Canadian peacekeeping has declined.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2-3 sentences, identify one global issue (climate, pandemics, AI, trade, migration, critical minerals) where multilateral cooperation is essential, and explain why. Cite one current institution or treaty.
Sample: Climate change is the paradigmatic case: no state can decarbonise unilaterally because emission reductions in one state are easily offset by emissions in others (carbon leakage); only collective commitments produce credible global trajectories. The UNFCCC framework (1992) and Paris Agreement (2015) attempt to coordinate NDCs across ~196 parties. The post-2024 Loss and Damage Fund (operationalised at COP28 2023) addresses the developing-country financing gap. Canada participates through the federal carbon-pricing framework (now reduced), the Indo-Pacific Strategy commitments, and the Paris Agreement contribution.
