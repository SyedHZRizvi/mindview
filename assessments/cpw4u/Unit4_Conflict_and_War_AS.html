Topics 4.1–4.2 — Causes of War and Just War Theory
Question 1
Kenneth Waltz's three "levels of analysis" of war causation (1959) are:
Solution: Waltz's Man, the State, and War (1959) distinguishes the three classical levels: individual (leaders, psychology); state (regime type, economy, internal politics); system (anarchic structure, polarity, alliance dynamics). Different international-relations schools emphasise different levels.
Question 2
The "security dilemma" (Herz 1950; Jervis 1978) describes how:
Solution: The security dilemma is a structural explanation: in anarchic international systems, every state must provide its own security; defensive build-ups look offensive to neighbours; the result is escalation. The 2014-24 NATO-Russia dynamic over Ukraine illustrates the dilemma.
Question 3
"All citizens are liable to military service for the defence of their country, and I conceive that the battle for autonomy is being fought today on the plains of France and of Belgium." — adapted
The classical just-war framework distinguishes:
Solution: Classical just-war theory (Augustine, Aquinas, Grotius, Walzer) distinguishes three branches addressing different moments: jus ad bellum (going to war), jus in bello (conduct in war), and jus post bellum (just settlement).
Question 4
Michael Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars (1977) is:
Solution: Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars (1977) is the modern foundational work, written in response to Vietnam. He extends the classical tradition (Cicero, Augustine, Aquinas, Vitoria, Grotius) into the post-1945 nuclear-era and humanitarian-intervention contexts.
Topics 4.3–4.4 — IHL and R2P
Question 5
The four 1949 Geneva Conventions and their 1977 Additional Protocols form the core of:
Solution: The 1949 Geneva Conventions (I — wounded soldiers; II — sea; III — POWs; IV — civilians) plus the 1977 Additional Protocols I (international armed conflict) and II (non-international) form IHL. All 196 states are party to the four Conventions — among the most universally ratified treaties.
Question 6
The International Criminal Court (ICC), established by the 1998 Rome Statute, has jurisdiction over:
Solution: The ICC tries individuals (not states); the ICJ tries states. The ICC's four core crimes are genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression. Canada ratified the Rome Statute in 2000 and implemented domestically through the Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act.
Question 7
The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine was developed by:
Solution: The International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS), chaired by Gareth Evans and Mohamed Sahnoun, was a Canadian-led initiative. Its 2001 report developed the R2P framework, adopted at the 2005 World Summit (paragraphs 138-139 of the Outcome Document).
Topics 4.5–4.6 — Cases and Canada
Question 8
The Rwandan genocide (April-July 1994) killed approximately:
Solution: 800,000-1,000,000 deaths in 100 days. UNAMIR was prevented from acting; Dallaire's January 1994 "genocide fax" was effectively ignored. The 1999 Carlsson Report and Dallaire's Shake Hands with the Devil (2003) are essential sources. The genocide directly catalysed R2P.
Question 9
The Srebrenica massacre (July 1995):
Solution: Srebrenica was declared a UN "safe area" in 1993; Dutch UN troops (Dutchbat) were present but vastly outgunned. Bosnian Serb forces under Mladić executed 8,372 men and boys. The ICTY (1993-2017) tried 161 people including Karadžić (40 years) and Mladić (life sentence); ICJ 2007 confirmed genocide.
Question 10
Canadian peacekeeping was effectively invented in:
Solution: When Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt over Suez nationalisation, Pearson (External Affairs) proposed UNEF — the first armed UN peacekeeping mission. He won the 1957 Nobel Peace Prize. Canadian peacekeeping has since declined dramatically — ~33 deployed in 2024, the lowest in Canadian history.
Question 11
Canadian forces in Afghanistan (2001-14):
Solution: Canada's Afghanistan mission (Operation Athena and successors) was the largest Canadian combat operation since Korea. ~40,000 rotated, 158 dead, over 2,000 wounded. The 2014 formal end coincided with the 2014-21 Taliban resurgence; Kabul fell August 2021. The mission reshaped CAF doctrine, equipment, and post-deployment policy.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2-3 sentences, compare the international response to Rwanda 1994 with the response to Ukraine 2022-. What changed about civilian-protection norms and great-power dynamics?
Sample: In Rwanda 1994 the UNAMIR force was reduced (from ~2,500 to 270 in May), no Western military intervention occurred, and aid during the genocide was ~$20M. In Ukraine 2022-, there has been no UN-mandated military intervention (Russian veto) but ~$300B+ in Western and other assistance, ~$280B in frozen Russian central-bank assets, ICC arrest warrants for Putin, and massive refugee resettlement (4M in Poland; 210K under CUAET to Canada). The continuity is that great-power dynamics constrain humanitarian action; the change is that civilian-protection has become more politically central when strategic interests align — Ukraine matters to European security in a way Rwanda did not.
