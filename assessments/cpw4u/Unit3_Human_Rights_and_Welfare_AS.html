Topics 3.1–3.2 — UDHR and Refugee Law
Question 1
The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by:
Solution: The UDHR was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948. The Soviet bloc, South Africa, and Saudi Arabia abstained. Canadian John Humphrey wrote the first draft as director of the UN Division of Human Rights.
Question 2
The Canadian John Humphrey's role in the UDHR was:
Solution: Humphrey was the first director of the UN Division of Human Rights and produced the first 400-page draft. René Cassin (France) was the principal redrafter; Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the Commission. The Canadian contribution is foundational and often understated.
Question 3
"No Contracting State shall expel or return ('refouler') a refugee in any manner whatsoever to the frontiers of territories where his life or freedom would be threatened on account of his race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion." — Article 33, 1951 Refugee Convention
This is the principle of:
Solution: Non-refoulement is the core of the modern refugee-protection regime. It binds all 1951 Convention parties (149 states) and is also a customary international-law obligation applicable to non-parties.
Question 4
Canada's 2015–16 Syrian refugee resettlement program:
Solution: The Liberal government's commitment of 25,000 Syrians by 31 December 2015 was extended; by year-end 2016 ~40,000 had arrived. Canada's distinctive Privately Sponsored Refugees (PSR) model allowed unprecedented community mobilisation. The model was repeated for Ukrainians (CUAET 2022, ~210,000) and Afghans.
Topic 3.3 — The Canadian Human Rights Framework
Question 5
In Andrews v. Law Society of British Columbia (1989, the first Charter section 15 case), the SCC adopted:
Solution: Andrews rejected the US-style formal-equality approach in favour of substantive equality: section 15 is violated when a law creates a distinction on enumerated or analogous grounds that imposes a burden or denies a benefit on a discriminatory basis. This has been the consistent SCC approach.
Question 6
In Vriend v. Alberta (1998), the SCC:
Solution: Cory and Iacobucci JJ. unanimously held sexual orientation was an analogous ground; Alberta's silence amounted to discrimination; the remedy was to "read in" sexual orientation to the protected grounds. Premier Klein threatened section 33 but did not invoke it.
Topic 3.4 — TRC and UNDRIP
Question 7
The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 2015 Final Report concluded that the residential-school system constituted:
Solution: The TRC's Final Report (Volume 1) used "cultural genocide" to describe the federally-administered, church-operated residential schools (1883-1996), which enrolled ~150,000 Indigenous children with documented mortality of 4,000-6,000+ (revised upward by post-2021 ground-penetrating radar findings).
Question 8
UNDRIP (UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples) was:
Solution: UNDRIP was adopted 13 September 2007. Canada initially voted no (with the US, Australia, NZ); all four later endorsed. The federal UNDRIP Act received Royal Assent 21 June 2021 (National Indigenous Peoples Day); the Action Plan was published 21 June 2023.
Topics 3.5–3.6 — Welfare State and Measurement
Question 9
The 1984 Canada Health Act's five principles are:
Solution: Section 7 of the Canada Health Act sets out the five principles. Provinces must operate health systems that meet all five to receive federal cash contributions. The CHA's enforcement is fiscal (withholding of contributions) — rare but real.
Question 10
Esping-Andersen's Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism (1990) classifies welfare states as:
Solution: Esping-Andersen's typology remains the standard comparative framework. Canada is typically classified as liberal-tilting but with substantial social-democratic features (Medicare, CCB, CPP, EI) that resemble Nordic models.
Question 11
The Gini coefficient measures:
Solution: The Gini (1912) ranges 0 to 1. Canada's after-tax Gini was 0.305 in 2022 — more unequal than Scandinavia (0.25-0.27) but less than the US (0.39). OECD average ~0.314.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2-3 sentences, explain why measuring "welfare" matters politically, with a Canadian or comparative example.
Sample: Choice of indicator shapes policy and political conclusion. GDP per capita suggests the US (~$82,000) is "richer" than Canada (~$55,000), but the US has lower life expectancy (76 vs. 83 years), worse inequality (Gini 0.39 vs. 0.305), and worse health outcomes despite spending twice as much per capita. Indicator choice is therefore political: emphasising GDP supports one story; emphasising HDI plus inequality plus life expectancy supports another. A Level-4 analysis recognises that indicator choice is itself a contested political move.
