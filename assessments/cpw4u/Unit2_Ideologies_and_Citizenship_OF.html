Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) John Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689); (b) Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790); (c) Marx and Engels's Communist Manifesto (1848).
(a) Locke (1689) — foundational text of classical liberalism; argued individuals have natural rights (life, liberty, property) prior to the state; legitimate government rests on consent; state's purpose is to protect rights. (b) Burke (1790) — foundational text of traditional conservatism; opposed French revolutionaries' abstract rationalism; argued societies are organic, institutions embody accumulated wisdom, gradual change better than revolutionary. (c) Marx and Engels (1848) — opening text of revolutionary Marxist socialism; argued history is class struggle; capitalism produces wealth but concentrates ownership; the proletariat will overturn capitalism.

[2]
According to Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser (2017), populism is best defined as:
B. The leading academic definition. Populism is "thin-centred" because it attaches to thicker host ideologies — left-populism (Chávez, Sanders, Mélenchon) and right-populism (Trump, Le Pen, Orbán) share the people-elite frame but combine with different thicker commitments.
3
[3]
Define each: (a) Marshall's three layers of citizenship; (b) FPTP (first-past-the-post); (c) social capital (Putnam).
(a) Marshall — civil citizenship (legal equality, due process, 17th-18th c.), political citizenship (vote, office-holding, 19th-early 20th c.), social citizenship (welfare-state minimum, 20th c.). (b) FPTP — single-member plurality electoral system; awards each riding to candidate with most votes (not majority); favours regionally concentrated parties. (c) Social capital — Putnam's concept; trust and norms of reciprocity produced by voluntary-association involvement; argued essential for democratic health.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[6]
"Conservatism in Canada has always been a hybrid: the Burkean attachment to tradition; the Smithian commitment to markets; the post-2022 populist rejection of elites." — adapted from contemporary commentary, 2025
Analyse this characterisation. Apply the concept of political perspective: in 6-8 sentences, identify the three strands of contemporary Canadian conservatism mentioned, evaluate whether they are mutually compatible, and assess whether the post-2003 Conservative Party of Canada has integrated them successfully.
Three strands: (1) Burkean — gradual change, tradition, established institutions (Joe Clark, Brian Mulroney, the Red Tory tradition of George Grant); (2) Smithian/neoliberal — markets, lower taxes, deregulation (the post-1970s Hayek/Friedman influence; Mulroney's free trade; Harper's GST cut and corporate tax reductions); (3) Populist — anti-elite, common-people-vs-establishment (the post-2022 Poilievre style; Reform/Canadian Alliance roots). Compatibility: tensions exist — Burkean prudence vs. populist disruption; market liberalism vs. economic nationalism; tradition vs. anti-establishment. Integration: the CPC has held the three together through electoral coalition, but tensions are real (e.g., the post-2018 Bernier defection to the People's Party; the post-2024 internal debates on tariffs, identity politics, and federal spending). Marks: 2 description of three strands, 2 compatibility analysis, 2 integration assessment.
7
[5]
Apply the concept of stability and change: in 5 sentences, evaluate the post-2010 trend toward "democratic backsliding" globally (V-Dem 2024 report shows 42 countries autocratising, 18 democratising). What is one mechanism by which mature democracies erode? What is one Canadian implication?
Mechanism: legal-institutional erosion through constitutional amendment, court-packing, electoral rule changes, media restriction, and harassment of opposition — typically maintained within nominally democratic forms (e.g., Hungary's Orbán since 2010; Türkiye's Erdoğan post-2016; Modi's India in some assessments). Stability: democracy has remained the world's normative aspiration; over half of states still hold elections. Change: the share of world population in autocracies is at its highest since the 1990s; democratic erosion no longer requires coups but works through legal channels. Canadian implications: section 33 expansion, attack-on-media rhetoric, electoral-reform fatigue all bear on questions about Canadian democratic resilience; the 2022 Convoy and post-Convoy debates raise questions about norms of dissent. Marks: 2 mechanism, 1 Canadian implication, 2 analysis quality.
8
[4]
Apply the concept of political significance: in 4 sentences, evaluate the political significance of Vine Deloria Jr., Taiaiake Alfred, and Glen Coulthard for Canadian political theory. Why does Indigenous political thought matter for understanding Canadian politics?
Deloria Jr. (Custer Died for Your Sins 1969), Alfred (Peace, Power, Righteousness 1999), and Coulthard (Red Skin, White Masks 2014) developed political theory from Indigenous traditions (Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace, Anishinaabe clan governance) rather than as a footnote to Western frameworks. Key concepts: nationhood as relationship to land and ancestors rather than as Westphalian state; sovereignty as ongoing self-determination; governance as distributed across clan/family/community; reconciliation as ongoing political relationship. Significance for Canadian politics: section 35 (1982), UNDRIP (2007/2021 Act), and the TRC framework operate not as concessions but as recognition that Canadian sovereignty was always layered over pre-existing Indigenous nationhood. Marks: 1 identification, 2 conceptual contribution, 1 Canadian relevance.
Part C: Communication [15 marks]

[7]
Write a coherent analytical paragraph (7-9 sentences) on the claim: "The Canadian first-past-the-post electoral system distorts the relationship between popular vote share and parliamentary seats." Use specific Canadian examples; embed at least one specific electoral statistic.
Look for: (1) clear thesis (agreement, disagreement, or qualification); (2) specific examples — Bloc Québécois consistently wins 30-40 seats with ~7% national vote (concentrated); Greens win ~6% but 1-2 seats (dispersed); 2019 federal election produced Conservative-most-votes-but-Liberal-most-seats; 2021 similar pattern; the 1993 PC collapse from 169 seats to 2 with 16% vote; (3) the FPTP "manufactured majority" — most majority governments hold under 40% of vote; (4) consequences — disproportionate regional representation, strategic-voting incentives, third-party challenges; (5) counter-points — FPTP simplifies government formation, produces local-MP accountability; (6) analytical close — the system has been studied (1971-72 Trudeau election reform talks; 2015 Liberal electoral-reform commitment abandoned 2017). Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 analysis, 1 counter-point, 1 communication.
10
[8]
Write a structured 9-11 sentence response comparing three social movements: the 1960s-70s women's movement, Idle No More (2012-13), and the 2022 Freedom Convoy. Identify one common feature and three substantive differences. Use specific historical detail.
Common feature: all three combined extra-parliamentary mobilisation (protests, marches, hunger strikes, blockades, occupations) with attempts to influence formal political institutions; all three reframed national political conversation. Differences: (1) Ideological orientation — women's movement was progressive-liberal-feminist with social-democratic strands; Idle No More was Indigenous-led, anti-colonial, advocating section 35 / UNDRIP rights; Convoy was populist-right, anti-mandate, anti-Trudeau-government, with extremist fringes. (2) Institutional success — women's movement achieved constitutional entrenchment (sections 15 and 28 of Charter 1982); Idle No More shifted public discourse and contributed to TRC implementation but most C-45 reforms were not reversed; Convoy succeeded in re-energising Conservative populism but Emergencies Act was invoked, leaders prosecuted. (3) State response — women's movement worked largely through commissions, courts, and electoral pressure; Idle No More faced police monitoring but limited direct repression; Convoy faced unprecedented Emergencies Act invocation (first peacetime use). (4) Demographic/social base — women's movement was middle-class with progressive social bases; Idle No More was First Nations communities and allies; Convoy was trucking, rural, libertarian Conservative bases. Marks: 1 common feature, 1 per substantive difference (3), 2 evidence integration, 2 communication.
Part D: Application [15 marks]
11
[7]
In the 2025 federal political environment, Pierre Poilievre's Conservative Party has emphasised "common sense" politics, criticism of "gatekeepers," and direct-to-camera communication.
Apply your understanding of political ideologies, populism, and Canadian party systems. In 7-8 sentences: (a) classify Poilievre's political style using the Mudde/Rovira Kaltwasser populism framework; (b) compare to historical Canadian populist movements (Progressives, Social Credit, Reform); (c) identify one substantive policy area where populist rhetoric and traditional Conservative policy align AND one where they tension; (d) take a position on whether contemporary Canadian populism strengthens or weakens democratic norms.
(a) Classification: Poilievre's style fits the Mudde/Rovira Kaltwasser populist definition — pitting "common people" against "gatekeepers/elites" — combined with thicker right-conservative content (lower taxes, reduced regulation, traditional family). Distinguishable from fascism (no leader cult, mass violence, single ideology). (b) Comparison: Canadian populism has a long tradition — 1920s Progressives (United Farmers of Ontario), Social Credit (Aberhart 1935-43; Caouette's Quebec wing), Reform/Canadian Alliance (Manning 1987-2003). Like these, Poilievre uses anti-establishment framing; unlike them, he operates within an integrated Conservative party rather than as a third party. (c) Alignment: populist rhetoric aligns with traditional Conservative emphasis on lower taxes, reduced federal spending, deregulation. Tension: populist anti-elite framing tensions with traditional Conservative respect for institutions (courts, civil service, Crown); also tensions with Conservative commitment to free trade in a period of populist-right tariff politics. (d) Position: defensible answer either way — strengthens democracy if it brings new voters into the political system and addresses grievances institutions had failed to address; weakens democracy if its monistic frame ("the people" = our voters) erodes pluralism. The classroom-appropriate answer takes a position and defends it with evidence. Marks: 2 classification, 1 historical comparison, 1 alignment, 1 tension, 2 position-taking with evidence.

13
[3]
Cross-strand application: Compare one feature of citizenship in Canada to citizenship in one other country you've studied or read about (e.g., the US, the UK, France, India, China, a country of your family origin). In 3-4 sentences, identify one similarity and one difference, with one implication for the comparison.
Examples: (1) Canada vs. US — both grant citizenship by birth (jus soli); both grant citizenship by descent; both have written constitutions with rights protections. Difference — Canadian voting rights extend to prisoners (post-Sauvé) and long-term non-residents (post-Frank); US has more restrictive rules in many states. Implication — Canadian citizenship doctrine emphasises inclusivity in voting; US is more variable across states. (2) Canada vs. China — Chinese citizenship is exclusively jus sanguinis (no birthright); Canadian system reflects immigrant-society logic; political participation in China is single-party. (3) Canada vs. France — France has both jus sanguinis and limited jus soli; both protect language rights but France emphasises laïcité while Canada protects religious expression. Marks: 1 similarity, 1 difference, 1 implication.
