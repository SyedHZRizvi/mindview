Unit 2: Ideologies and Citizenship — Diagnostic

Assessment FOR Learning · Prerequisite check · Strand B — B2.1, B2.3, B3.1, B3.2
Not Graded — Teacher Feedback Only
Purpose: Identify gaps in your prior knowledge of political ideologies, the Canadian party system, citizenship, and civil society before starting Unit 2. You will receive descriptive feedback, not a grade.
Question 1 Prereq · Vocabulary
[3 marks]
In one sentence each, define: (a) liberalism; (b) conservatism; (c) socialism. Don't worry about being precise — your goal is to indicate what you think they mean.
Question 2 Prior Knowledge
[3 marks]
"The terms 'left' and 'right' in politics come from the 1789 French National Assembly, where supporters of revolutionary change sat on the presiding officer's left and supporters of the king sat on the right."
In 2–3 sentences, give one example of a contemporary political issue and explain how you would place different positions on the Left-Right spectrum.
Question 3 Prior Knowledge · Canadian Politics
[3 marks]
Name three Canadian federal political parties active today. For each, describe one position or value you associate with it.
Question 4 Prereq · Citizenship
[3 marks]
A friend asks: "What does it mean to be a Canadian citizen as opposed to a permanent resident?" In 3–4 sentences, identify at least two rights or capacities citizens have that permanent residents do not.
Question 5 Skills · Source Analysis
[4 marks]
"It is the working class which alone can be the bearer of the new society of equality, harmony, and abundance." — Eugene Debs, American socialist, 1908
In 4 sentences: (a) identify the ideological tradition Debs is drawing on; (b) identify one philosophical assumption embedded in the statement; (c) identify one critique that classical or social liberals might offer; (d) identify one critique that traditional conservatives might offer.
Question 6 Skills · Critical Thinking
[3 marks]
A common claim is: "All populists are dangerous." In 3-4 sentences, identify one merit of this concern AND one important nuance or qualification. Refer to the Mudde/Rovira Kaltwasser definition of populism if possible.
Question 7 Reflection
[ungraded]
Which of these Unit 2 topics do you feel least confident about: (a) the conceptual map of ideologies; (b) liberalism / conservatism / socialism; (c) fascism, populism, Indigenous political thought; (d) Canadian citizenship; (e) the Canadian party system; (f) civil society and social movements? Explain what specifically is unclear.
📖 Sample Answers and Marking Rubric (Instructor View)

Q1 (3 marks, 1 each): Liberalism — political tradition emphasising individual rights, limited government, and rational individualism (Locke, Smith, Mill, Rawls). Conservatism — political tradition emphasising tradition, gradual change, organic society (Burke). Socialism — political tradition emphasising collective ownership/redistribution, critique of capitalism (Marx; democratic socialism: Bernstein, Douglas).

Q2 (3 marks): Award 1 for example, 1 for left positioning, 1 for right positioning. Examples might include: carbon pricing (left/centre = supports as climate measure; right = opposes as economic burden); minimum wage; supply management; gun control; immigration; LGBTQ rights. The goal is informal mapping skill, not precision.

Q3 (3 marks): 1 per party. Liberals (centrist, Charter, mixed economy); Conservatives (low taxes, post-2022 populist tropes, smaller government); NDP (social democracy, labour, pharmacare/dental); Bloc Québécois (Quebec sovereignty, social-democratic); Greens (environment, electoral reform); People's Party (right-populist, restrictionist).

Q4 (3 marks): Key citizen-only rights: federal vote (s.3 Charter); eligibility for elected office; passport; can leave/return freely (s.6); some sectors of employment restricted to citizens (RCMP, Canadian Forces officer ranks, certain security positions). Permanent residents have most rights except these and can be deported in some circumstances. Award 1 per right + 1 for clarity.

Q5 (4 marks, 1 each): (a) Tradition: democratic socialism / Marxism; emphasis on working class as agent of change. (b) Assumption: capitalism produces inequality that the working class can overcome; collective ownership/agency. (c) Liberal critique: rights are individual, not class-based; voluntary cooperation and market exchange better than collectivist alternatives. (d) Conservative critique: revolutionary change destroys accumulated social wisdom; class is one of many social bonds, not the foundational one.

Q6 (3 marks): Merit: populism's monistic frame ("the people" vs. "the elite") rejects pluralism — the legitimacy of competing political identities — which is a core democratic value. Nuance: populism can be left or right; in some cases populism has expanded political participation (early 20th-century progressives); the contemporary problem is when populism captures executive power and erodes pluralism. Award 1 merit, 1 nuance, 1 sophistication.

Q7 (ungraded): Use to inform pedagogical priorities. Common gaps include distinguishing populism from democracy; understanding Indigenous political thought as a substantive tradition; the Westminster brokerage-party concept.
