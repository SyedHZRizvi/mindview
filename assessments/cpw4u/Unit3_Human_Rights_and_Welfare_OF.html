Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Identify each: (a) the UDHR (1948); (b) the ICCPR and ICESCR (1966); (c) John Humphrey's role.
(a) UDHR — Universal Declaration of Human Rights; adopted UN General Assembly 10 December 1948 by 48-0 with 8 abstentions; 30 articles; foundational human-rights document. (b) ICCPR & ICESCR (1966) — binding follow-up treaties on civil/political and economic/social/cultural rights respectively; ratified by Canada 1976. (c) John Humphrey — Canadian; first director of UN Division of Human Rights; wrote UDHR's first draft (~400 pages); foundational Canadian contribution to international order.

[2]
The principle of non-refoulement, found in Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention, requires that:
B. Non-refoulement is the fundamental obligation of the international refugee regime. It binds Convention parties (149 states) and is also customary international law applicable to non-parties.
3
[3]
Define each: (a) the Truth and Reconciliation Commission; (b) UNDRIP; (c) free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC).
(a) TRC — established 2008 as part of the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement; chaired by Justice Murray Sinclair; operated 2009-15; gathered 6,750+ survivor statements; concluded the residential-school system constituted "cultural genocide"; issued 94 Calls to Action. (b) UNDRIP — UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; adopted UNGA 2007; endorsed by Canada 2010 and given federal legislative status by the UNDRIP Act (2021); 46 articles. (c) FPIC — free, prior, and informed consent; UNDRIP standard for Indigenous consent to projects affecting lands and resources; consent must be free, prior, and informed.

Examples (any 4): Unemployment Insurance / EI (1940, federal; first major Canadian social-insurance program; rebuilt 2020-22 as CERB); Family Allowance (1944, federal; first universal cash transfer; predecessor of CCB); OAS (1951, federal; universal flat-rate seniors income); CPP/QPP (1965/66, federal-provincial; contributory pension; expanded 2019-25); Medicare (1966 federal Act, provincial delivery; universal physician/hospital coverage); Canada Health Act (1984, federal; five principles framework); CCB (2016, federal; income-tested monthly family transfer); National Dental Care Plan (2023-25, federal; aged-65+ and child-12-and-under streams); Pharmacare Bill C-64 (2024-25, federal-provincial implementation). Marks: 1 per program (name + date + level + significance).
Part B: Thinking & Investigation [15 marks]
6
[6]
"The honest judgment: recognise the achievement without erasing the harm; the bronze statues require contextual interpretation, not simple celebration." — adapted from contemporary commentary on reconciliation, 2025
Analyse the Canadian Truth and Reconciliation Commission and the federal response. Apply the concept of objectives and results: in 6-8 sentences, identify the TRC's stated objectives, evaluate substantive progress on the 94 Calls to Action (as tracked by the Yellowhead Institute), and assess the gap between commitment and implementation.
Stated objectives: document residential-schools history; gather survivor testimony; produce recommendations for federal, provincial, and church institutions; advance reconciliation. Results: 6,750+ statements gathered; Volume 1-6 of Final Report published 2015; National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation established at University of Manitoba; "cultural genocide" framing entered public discourse. Substantive progress: Yellowhead Institute's annual TRC report tracks implementation — as of 2024 only ~13 of 94 Calls to Action fully implemented; progress patchy. Specific actions: TRC 80 — National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (Bill C-5, 2021, federally regulated; provincial implementation uneven); MMIWG Inquiry 231 Calls for Justice (2019); the 2021 unmarked-graves discoveries; UNDRIP Act 2021 + Action Plan 2023; the 2024 $40-billion First Nations Child Welfare settlement. Gap: commemorative recognition has outpaced substantive policy change; some critics see this as displacement, others as building political will for substantive action. Marks: 2 objectives, 2 progress evaluation, 2 gap analysis.
7
[5]
Apply the concept of political perspective: in 5 sentences, evaluate the 2024 federal pharmacare expansion (Bill C-64). Identify two legitimate political perspectives on universal pharmacare and the constitutional principles each prioritises.
Bill C-64 (2024) creates single-payer national pharmacare initially covering diabetes medications and contraceptives. Two perspectives: (1) Liberal-NDP-social-democratic perspective — pharmacare completes Canadian universal healthcare; ICESCR Article 12 (right to health) aligns; consolidates UDHR Article 25 obligations. Prioritises substantive equality and universalism. (2) Conservative-federalist perspective — pharmacare expansion is federal encroachment on provincial healthcare jurisdiction; displaces private-employer plans (~$15B+ of existing coverage); creates fiscal sustainability concerns. Prioritises federalism balance and fiscal responsibility. Implementation depends on federal-provincial agreements; Alberta and Quebec have signalled non-participation; PEI signed first deal October 2024. Marks: 1 description, 2 first perspective, 2 second perspective.
8
[4]
Apply the concept of stability and change: in 4 sentences, evaluate the Canadian human-rights framework's evolution from the 1960 Bill of Rights to the 1982 Charter. What did the Charter change? What remained stable?
The 1960 Canadian Bill of Rights (federal statute, applies only to federal law) was rarely successfully invoked — only R. v. Drybones 1970 struck down a statute. The 1982 Charter (constitutional) binds all government action (federal, provincial, municipal) and is enforceable through section 52 strike-down or section 24 remedies. Stability: human-rights protection has remained statutorily and constitutionally grounded; provincial human-rights codes (Ontario 1962, all provinces) operate alongside both. Change: judicial review of rights became central to Canadian law; Charter jurisprudence has substantially reshaped equality, expression, religion, and criminal law. Section 33 preserves a residual parliamentary check on Charter sections 2 and 7-15. Marks: 2 evolution, 1 stability, 1 change.

Write a coherent analytical paragraph (7-9 sentences) on the claim: "Canadian Indigenous-rights jurisprudence has evolved from negation through tolerance to recognition through the late 20th and 21st centuries." Use specific cases, statutes, and dates.
Look for: (1) clear thesis (agreement or qualification); (2) negation period — pre-1960s residential schools, 1876 Indian Act, voluntary enfranchisement, 1969 White Paper proposed termination; (3) tolerance period — 1973 Calder SCC recognition of pre-existing title; 1982 section 35 entrenchment; 1990 Sparrow SCC; 1997 Delgamuukw oral history admissible; (4) recognition period — 2004 Haida consultation/accommodation; 2014 Tsilhqot'in declaration of Aboriginal title; 2015 TRC; 2019 MMIWG Inquiry; 2021 UNDRIP Act; 2023 Action Plan; 2024 $40B First Nations Child Welfare settlement; (5) qualification — recognition is not equivalence; implementation gaps remain; section 35 jurisprudence is contested; (6) analytical close — recognition is a real shift but politically conditional. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence (cases/statutes), 2 analysis, 1 qualification, 1 communication.
10
[8]
Write a structured 9-11 sentence response comparing three approaches to refugee protection: the 1951 Convention model, the Canadian Privately Sponsored Refugees (PSR) model, and the post-2017 EU-Turkey arrangement / 2024 Rwanda transfer scheme. Identify one similarity and three substantive differences. Take a position on which approach best serves Convention obligations.
Similarity: all three operate within the 1951 Refugee Convention framework as background; all three address the gap between protection obligation and political capacity. Differences: (1) Selection model — 1951 Convention model is asylum-claimant-centred (the protected enter and claim); Canadian PSR is selection-abroad with sponsorship; EU-Turkey / Rwanda is externalisation (process or relocate before EU asylum). (2) Locus of decision — Convention emphasises individual case-by-case assessment; PSR involves community sponsorship; externalisation locates processing outside the receiving state. (3) Compliance with non-refoulement — Convention model directly applies; PSR pre-screens but operates within Convention; externalisation regimes have been challenged (UK Supreme Court 2023 ruled Rwanda scheme breached non-refoulement; appeal politics ongoing). Position: defensible answer either direction; argument for Canadian PSR model emphasises sustained community integration and Convention compliance; argument against emphasises that resettlement should not displace inland-asylum obligations. Marks: 1 similarity, 1 per difference, 2 evidence, 2 communication.
Part D: Application [15 marks]
11
[7]
In November 2024, the Federal Court approved the $40 billion First Nations Child Welfare settlement, completing a 17-year legal struggle by Cindy Blackstock and the First Nations Child and Family Caring Society against the Canadian government, which the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal had found discriminatory in 2016.
Apply your understanding of human-rights mechanisms and reconciliation. In 7-8 sentences: (a) summarise the constitutional and human-rights issues; (b) apply the concept of objectives and results; (c) compare to the 2008 federal Indian Residential Schools Settlement; (d) take a position on whether this case represents fulfilment or insufficient remedy.
(a) Issues: federal underfunding of on-reserve child welfare and Jordan's Principle services constituted discrimination under section 15 of the Charter and the Canadian Human Rights Act; the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal found discrimination in 2016. (b) Objectives and results: stated federal objective during litigation was to limit liability; observed result is settlement totalling ~$40B for ~300,000+ class members affected since 1991, covering compensation and infrastructure reform. (c) Comparison to IRSSA 2008: the 2008 IRSSA settled litigation over residential-school harms (~$5.5B in compensation plus TRC; ~80,000 Common Experience Payment recipients); the 2024 settlement extends similar logic to ongoing child-welfare jurisdiction. (d) Position: defensible either direction. For fulfilment: the largest federal-Indigenous settlement; institutional acknowledgment of structural discrimination; infrastructure investment for reform. For insufficient: compensation cannot replace generation lost to system; ongoing federal child-welfare jurisdiction debates remain; UNDRIP implementation has been slow. Marks: 1 constitutional issue, 2 objectives-and-results, 2 comparison, 2 position-with-evidence.
12
[5]
Application: Apply the concept of political significance: in 5 sentences, evaluate Canada's 2015-16 Syrian resettlement as a model for refugee protection. What did it accomplish? What were its limits? Could it scale?
The 2015-16 Syrian resettlement admitted ~40,000 refugees in 14 months — Canada's largest single-country resettlement since the 1979-80 Indochinese effort. Accomplishments: demonstrated PSR capacity; mobilised hundreds of sponsorship groups; produced substantial integration outcomes (employment, language, citizenship); generated international interest in the Canadian model. Limits: ad hoc framework; partial provincial capacity strains in housing and education; inland-asylum protections were not strengthened in parallel; "double standard" critique noted later when Ukrainians (210,000+ CUAET) and Afghans (50,000+) admitted more rapidly than other refugee populations. Scaling potential: model has been used (CUAET 2022; Afghan resettlement 2021-25); but the 2023-25 Levels Plan reduction shows political fragility; the underlying need (120+ million displaced globally) vastly exceeds any one country's capacity. Marks: 1 accomplishment, 2 limits, 1 scaling potential, 1 synthesis.

[3]
Cross-strand application: Compare Canada's welfare-state spending (~26% GDP on social spending; OECD 2023) to one other country (e.g., US, Sweden, Australia, Germany). In 3-4 sentences, identify one similarity and one difference; assess one consequence.
Examples: (1) Canada (26%) vs. Sweden (~30%); both have universal healthcare and substantial transfers; Sweden has higher-replacement unemployment, longer parental leave, child-care subsidisation; consequence — Swedish poverty rates and inequality lower than Canada's. (2) Canada vs. US (~23% but with different mix); both have universal birthright citizenship; US has much weaker universal coverage (no universal healthcare; varied family supports); consequence — US life expectancy 76 vs. Canada 83; higher US healthcare spending despite worse outcomes. (3) Canada vs. Australia (~17%); both English-speaking liberal-Westminster; both manage universal healthcare; Australia has supplementary private system covering ~50% of population. Marks: 1 similarity, 1 difference, 1 consequence.
