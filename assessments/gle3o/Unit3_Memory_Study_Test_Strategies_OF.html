Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[2]
Working memory capacity is best estimated by Cowan (2001) as approximately:
Answer Key:

A. Cowan (2001) revised Miller's earlier 7±2 estimate to roughly 4 chunks. The exact figure is contested; the point is that working memory is small and a bottleneck.

2
[2]
The "testing effect" (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006) found that:
Answer Key:

B. Retrieval acts strengthen memory traces more than equivalent rereading. Make It Stick (Brown, Roediger, McDaniel 2014) calls retrieval practice "the single highest-leverage learning strategy in the cognitive-science literature."

3
[3]
Define each: (a) the forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus); (b) the spacing effect; (c) the fluency illusion.
Answer Key:

(a) Forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus, 1885) — rapid memory loss after learning (~50% in an hour, ~70% in a day, ~90% in a week) unless reviews intervene. (b) Spacing effect — reviews spread across days at expanding intervals produce dramatically better long-term retention than the same total time in a single block. (c) Fluency illusion — the smooth feeling of recognition during rereading produces unwarranted confidence about retrieval; "easy now, hard on the test."

4
[2]
The rule of thumb "mnemonics for items, concept maps for relationships" means:
Answer Key:

A. Mnemonics (SMART, MAIN, BEDMAS) are right for list recall; concept maps (Novak) are right for showing relationships. Each tool has its job; using one for the other's task is a common mistake.

5
[3]
Identify and briefly describe three format-specific test-taking strategies: (a) for multiple-choice; (b) for short answer; (c) for essay.
Answer Key:

(a) MC: read the stem twice; eliminate clearly-wrong options; look for distractor patterns (opposites often signal one is right; "all of the above" needs each verified); don't change answers on doubt alone. (b) Short answer: precise vocabulary, one specific example per requested sub-point, one sentence per mark as a rough rule; answer the actual question asked. (c) Essay: spend first 5–10 minutes planning (identify ask, write thesis, outline three body paragraphs); apply frame-evidence-analyse pattern to every quotation; save last 5 minutes for read-through.

6
[3]
In 3 sentences each, describe two evidence-based interventions for test anxiety and explain the mechanism for each.
Answer Key:

Reappraisal: re-reading bodily arousal of anxiety as "preparation" rather than as "threat." Mechanism: same physiological arousal, different cognitive interpretation; brain-imaging research shows measurably better performance under the reappraisal interpretation. Expressive writing (Park, Ramirez & Beilock 2014): 5–10 minutes of pre-test journaling about test-related worries. Mechanism: offloads cognitive interference from working memory, freeing capacity for the test itself; closes test-anxiety performance gap by ~50%. Also accept breathing, retrieval practice, and familiarity-through-practice. Marks: 1.5 per intervention (name + mechanism).

Part B — Thinking [15 marks]
7
[5]
Lucas's Grade 11 French course introduces ~40 new vocabulary items per week. He currently studies them all the night before each Friday quiz; he retains for one week, forgets most before the exam.
Apply spaced repetition. In 5–6 sentences, design a study plan using Anki (or equivalent), specify the daily time commitment, and predict the impact on his cumulative-exam retention. Justify with reference to the spacing effect.
Answer Key:

Plan: add ~20 new cards on each of Mon and Wed (40/week); open Anki for 10–15 min daily on whatever is "due"; well-known cards expand intervals (30+ days), poorly-known cards return sooner. Daily load stays ~15 min even as the deck grows to 600+. Prediction: cumulative-exam retention rises from ~40% (cramming pattern) to ~90% (spaced retrieval). Justification: spacing engages re-learning at moments of partial forgetting, which is when the long-term system strengthens most (Bjork's "desirable difficulties"). Marks: 1 daily cadence, 1 schedule, 1 retention prediction, 1 spacing justification, 1 internal coherence.

8
[5]
Marcus is studying the French Revolution. The causes interlock: financial crisis from American Revolution support, Enlightenment ideas, 1788 famine, rigidities of the Estates system, personal weakness of Louis XVI, British/American examples.
Apply the mnemonics-vs-concept-maps rule. In 5 sentences, decide which tool fits and design a specific concept map (text-form: central node + 3–4 branches + at least one labelled relationship) that would help Marcus prepare for the essay question "Explain the causes of the French Revolution."
Answer Key:

Concept map is the right tool — the causes interlock (relational content). Central node: "French Revolution 1789." Branches: (1) Economic — financial crisis → tax reform blocked by Estates → bankruptcy. (2) Intellectual — Enlightenment (Rousseau, Voltaire) → ideas of popular sovereignty → political clubs. (3) Immediate — 1788 famine → bread prices → urban unrest. (4) Institutional — Estates General May 1789 → Third Estate breaks away → Tennis Court Oath. Labelled relationships across branches (e.g., bankruptcy → caused → Estates General; Enlightenment → shaped → Tennis Court Oath). On the essay, Marcus can speak about relationships, not just list items. Marks: 1 correct tool choice, 2 branches with substance, 1 cross-branch relationship, 1 essay-impact reasoning.

9
[5]
Avery's returned Functions test: 64%. Of 12 lost marks — 3 careless arithmetic slips, 4 concept errors (rational vs radical domain restrictions), 3 gap errors (transformations not studied), 2 format errors (didn't show work).
Apply error analysis. In 5 sentences, name a specific next-action for each error category and predict the impact on Avery's next test mark.
Answer Key:

Next-actions: (1) Careless — add estimate-and-check-signs habit; expect to recover 2–3 marks. (2) Concept — re-watch domain-restrictions lesson, do 5 retrieval-practice problems; expect to recover 3 marks. (3) Gap — add transformations to Anki rotation, 5 practice problems this week; expect to recover 2–3 marks. (4) Format — read every "show all reasoning" instruction twice; expect to recover 2 marks. Combined: 64% → ~80%+ on next test if all four interventions are implemented. The diagnosis-and-action protocol is what makes the improvement possible — not "study more." Marks: 1 per action linked to category (4), 1 prediction with reasoning.

Part C — Communication [15 marks]
10
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) defending or contesting the claim: "Cramming the night before a test is an effective study strategy." Use research evidence (spacing effect, forgetting curve, fluency illusion).
Answer Key:

Strong contest: thesis ("cramming produces short-term recall sufficient for next-morning tests but fails for cumulative exams and durable retention"); evidence (Cepeda et al. 2006 meta-analysis: same total time spaced beats cramming for retention at any test > 24 hours; Ebbinghaus forgetting curve drops most retention within a week without reviews); cramming's failure mode (fluency illusion produces overconfidence the morning of); nuance — cramming can be defensible as a one-off when you've already spaced; the test-result that matters in Grade 12 (exams, finals) defeats cramming. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence with research citation, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

11
[5]
Write a 4–6 sentence reflection (audience: yourself) on a recent test in any course. Apply the error-analysis categories (careless / concept / gap / format) and identify one specific change you will make in your next study cycle.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) name a specific real test; (2) categorise errors using all four labels (with concrete examples); (3) propose at least one specific change linked to a dominant error category; (4) use the appropriate self-reflective register. Marks: 1 specificity, 2 category use, 1 specific change, 1 conventions.

12
[4]
In 3–4 sentences, explain to a Grade 9 student (audience-aware register) why "re-reading" is one of the least effective study techniques and what they should do instead.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) name the fluency illusion in Grade-9-accessible language ("rereading feels like you're learning because it feels familiar, but your brain isn't doing the lifting"); (2) propose closed-book retrieval as the alternative ("close the book, try to explain it out loud, then check"); (3) connect to the concrete benefit ("you'll actually remember it next week"). Marks: 1 illusion described, 1 alternative proposed, 1 audience-appropriate, 1 accuracy.

Part D — Application [15 marks]
13
[5]
Apply spacing + retrieval to a real upcoming test in another course. In 5 sentences, design a study schedule with: (i) total hours allocated; (ii) the days of week each session will happen; (iii) what each session will actually do (retrieval, not rereading).
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) name a real test; (2) total hours roughly appropriate (e.g., 4–6 hours for a unit test); (3) days specified with expanding intervals; (4) sessions explicitly retrieval-based (closed-book problems, brain dumps, flashcards in produce-not-look mode), not rereading. Marks: 1 test specificity, 1 hours, 2 schedule with retrieval, 1 coherence.

14
[5]
Apply the format-specific test preparation protocol. Pick one upcoming test and describe in 5 sentences: (i) the format(s) it will use; (ii) how your preparation differs from how you would prepare for a different format; (iii) one specific in-test strategy you will use.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) identify the format(s) — typically a mix; (2) make explicit the preparation difference (e.g., "for the essay portion I'll practise writing introductions and topic sentences in advance; for the MC portion I'll run flashcards and review past MC questions"); (3) specify in-test strategy (e.g., "I'll spend the first 7 minutes planning the essay before writing"). Marks: 1 format, 2 preparation difference, 1 in-test strategy, 1 specificity.

15
[5]
Cross-strand application: Integrate Strand A (Self-Knowledge) with Strand B (Memory & Test Prep). In 6–8 sentences, describe how the Unit 1 metacognitive cycle (plan-monitor-evaluate) supports the Unit 3 study strategies (spacing, retrieval, error analysis), and explain why students who use both together outperform students who use only one.
Answer Key:

Strong answers integrate: Planning (Strand A) sets up the spaced schedule and chooses retrieval as the technique (Strand B); Monitoring mid-week catches drift (am I actually doing the retrieval sessions?) and lets you adjust before drift becomes failure; Evaluating after the test is essentially the error-analysis protocol (3.6). A student running both cycles simultaneously is using metacognition to deploy the right strategies and adjust based on evidence — the cycle compounds. Without monitoring, students who set up spaced schedules drift back to cramming; without evaluating, they don't learn from returned tests. Marks: 2 plan-monitor-evaluate mapping onto spacing/retrieval/error-analysis; 2 explanation of compounding; 1 synthesis statement.

📖 Complete Answer Key

This unit test covers Strand B's memory and test-taking expectations (B4.1–B4.3, B5.1–B5.3) at all four Achievement Chart levels. Level 4 answers integrate concepts across topics and apply them to real upcoming tests in the student's actual coursework. Marks rewarded for specificity, evidence, and research citations.

Common misconceptions to watch for: (1) Equating recognition (rereading) with learning; (2) treating cramming as defensible for cumulative exams; (3) confusing mnemonic and concept-map use cases; (4) seeing test anxiety as a personality trait; (5) filing returned tests without analysis.
