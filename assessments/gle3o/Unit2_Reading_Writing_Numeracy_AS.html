Topics 2.1–2.2 — Active Reading & Vocabulary
Question 1
The five steps of SQ3R (Robinson, 1946) are:
Solution: SQ3R = Survey (flip through, read headings/summary), Question (turn headings into questions), Read (one section at a time), Recite (close the book and produce the answer), Review (verify at the end). The Recite step is where most learning happens.
Question 2
Which is the most-validated finding about highlighting as a study technique?
Solution: Over-highlighting is a common student trap. Effective annotation uses a small vocabulary of marks (margin lines, stars, question marks, brief notes) — annotation that forces a decision about what the text is doing, not just colours on a page.
Question 3
"The conscription crisis exposed deep cleavages in Canadian society — Anglophone vs Francophone, urban vs rural, English-born vs Canadian-born."
A student doesn't know the word "cleavages." Which is the strongest vocabulary strategy in this case?
Solution: Context inference is the right first move — the listed pairs implicitly define "cleavages" as splits/divisions. Verifying with a glossary or dictionary is the second step. Writing the word and a self-composed sentence in a vocabulary log makes it productive vocabulary, not just passive recognition.
Topics 2.3–2.4 — Writing & Revision
Question 4
Which is a debatable, three-part thesis statement?
Solution: Option C is debatable (someone could argue otherwise), has a "because" structure (moral disengagement), and forecasts three pieces of evidence (hallucinations, violence, isolation) — the three parts of a strong thesis. A is a topic. B is a fact. D is a question.
Question 5
What distinguishes expository from persuasive writing?
Solution: Expository writing's job is to explain — to synthesise a clear account. Persuasive writing's job is to argue a debatable thesis. Misreading which form a prompt calls for is the most common Grade 11 essay-writing mistake.
Question 6
The recommended order of revision passes is:
Solution: Content (does the argument work?) → structural (is the order strongest?) → sentence-level (tight, varied) → proofread (spelling, punctuation, format). Doing them in this order avoids polishing sentences that later get cut, and avoids dividing attention across levels simultaneously.
Topics 2.5–2.6 — Numeracy & Graph Literacy
Question 7
A student calculates \(347 \times 28\) and gets 97,160 on their calculator. What estimation step should they have done?
Solution: Estimating first costs 5 seconds and catches decimal/order-of-magnitude errors that would otherwise lose marks. \(350 \times 30 = 10\,500\); the true answer is 9,716, so 97,160 is an order-of-magnitude error.
Question 8
The structured approach to multi-step problems has the phases:
Solution: Read (twice, circling given, underlining asked), Plan (substeps written out), Solve (carry units), Check (against estimate, units, reasonableness). The Plan step is where "show all reasoning" lives.
Question 9
Which intervention is most strongly supported by research on managing math anxiety?
Solution: Sian Beilock's research (Park, Ramirez & Beilock 2014) supports reappraisal and expressive writing; retrieval practice reduces the unfamiliarity component of anxiety. Math anxiety is a learnable response, not a fixed trait.
Question 10
A Level-4 graph analysis includes which of the following steps?
Solution: Frame-Read-Analyse-Critique is the four-step protocol. The critique step is where Level-4 marks live — naming what the graph isn't showing (selective time-windows, third variables, misleading scales) shows the marker that you understood the evidence's limits.
Question 11
Which is true about assistive technology in Ontario senior-grade education?
Solution: Universal Design for Learning principles support universal availability. Most boards license Read&Write or Kurzweil for all students; Immersive Reader is built into Microsoft Edge and Word. Strong students often use these tools strategically.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain why the "Recite" step in SQ3R is the part where most learning happens, even though many students skip it.
Sample: The Recite step requires you to close the book and produce the answer from your own memory, which engages retrieval — the act that actually drives durable learning (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Skipping straight from Read to Review keeps you in recognition mode, where the page is doing the work for you. The Recite step feels harder because retrieval is harder than recognition, but that "desirable difficulty" is exactly what produces the gain.
