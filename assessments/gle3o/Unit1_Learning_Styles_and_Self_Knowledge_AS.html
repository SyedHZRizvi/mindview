Topics 1.1–1.2 — Self-Knowledge & Learning-Style Inventories
Question 1
According to the Ontario Ministry's Creating Pathways to Success, career and life planning begins with which question?
Solution: The Ministry framework begins with "Who am I?" before "What are my opportunities?" and "What is my plan?" Self-knowledge is the foundation; opportunities and plans are built on it.
Question 2
VARK is a learning-style inventory that sorts learners by:
Solution: VARK (Fleming, 1987) sorts learners by their preferred mode of taking in new information: Visual, Aural, Read/write, Kinesthetic. Most learners are multimodal.
Question 3
"The matching of instruction to a learner's claimed style has not been shown to measurably improve outcomes." — paraphrased finding, Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008)
The most accurate conclusion about VARK based on current research is:
Solution: The learning-styles "matching myth" is not supported. But VARK still has value as a self-awareness tool — knowing your preference lets you design a translation strategy when lessons come in a non-preferred mode (e.g., redrawing aural-lecture notes as visual concept maps after class).
Topics 1.3–1.4 — Multiple Intelligences & Mindset
Question 4
Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences framework proposes that intelligence is best understood as:
Solution: Gardner (1983, Frames of Mind) proposed at least eight intelligences, later considering a possible ninth (existential). The framework gives students a vocabulary for strengths beyond just verbal and mathematical.
Question 5
According to Carol Dweck, a student with a fixed mindset is most likely to:
Solution: Fixed-mindset students treat ability as fixed, so any failure is read as evidence about their identity. They avoid challenge, give up sooner, hide mistakes, and feel threatened by others' success. Growth-mindset students do the opposite.
Question 6
The most powerful single language move to shift from fixed-mindset to growth-mindset framing is to:
Solution: The "power of yet" reframes a fixed claim ("I'm bad at this") as a current state ("I can't do this yet"). Dweck has emphasised that "yet" only helps if it is coupled with effective strategy change — words alone don't do the work.
Topics 1.5–1.6 — Metacognition & Self-Determination Theory
Question 7
The three phases of metacognition are:
Solution: Metacognition's three phases — planning ("what's the task and which strategy fits?"), monitoring ("is my strategy working?"), and evaluating ("what would I change?") — map onto Zimmerman's three phases of self-regulated learning: forethought, performance, self-reflection.
Question 8
In Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan), the three basic psychological needs for healthy motivation are:
Solution: SDT (Deci & Ryan, University of Rochester, 1970s-present) identifies autonomy (meaningful choice), competence (experience of growing skill), and relatedness (connection to others) as the three needs whose satisfaction supports intrinsic motivation.
Question 9
Which of the following is the most useful metacognitive technique?
Solution: The learning journal is the most-validated low-cost metacognitive tool. 3–4 sentences after each session catches what worked and what didn't, and over weeks builds an evidence base for strategy decisions. Re-reading is a recognition activity, not a metacognitive one.
Question 10
Sara has stopped doing her English homework. She used to enjoy English. Her assigned essay topics are narrow; she doesn't understand the recent "more analysis" feedback; her best English classmate transferred.
Using SDT, Sara's motivation crash is best diagnosed as:
Solution: Each of the three SDT needs is partially unmet. Diagnosis points to concrete actions: negotiate a personal angle on the essay topic (autonomy), request a brief teacher conversation about the feedback (competence), form a study pair (relatedness). Motivation crashes are usually need crashes, not character crashes.
Question 11
Why does Dweck's research distinguish "process praise" from "trait praise"?
Solution: Dweck's experimental studies showed that children praised for trait ("you're so smart") subsequently chose easier tasks to protect the trait label, while children praised for process ("you worked hard, you tried three approaches") chose harder tasks. Praise shapes mindset.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain why triangulation across multiple sources (inventories, performance data, self-observation, others' feedback) produces a more accurate learner profile than relying on a single online quiz.
Sample: Any single inventory captures only one slice of self-knowledge (a momentary self-report) and is subject to mood and to wording effects. Performance data shows what is actually working in real coursework; self-observation surfaces patterns the inventory misses; others' feedback catches blind spots you cannot see yourself. Triangulating across the four sources produces a profile detailed enough to guide actual study decisions, while a single quiz produces a thin sketch that can mislead more than help.
