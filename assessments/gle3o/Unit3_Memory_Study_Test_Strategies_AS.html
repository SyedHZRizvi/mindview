Topics 3.1–3.2 — Memory Systems & Spacing
Question 1
Working memory and long-term memory differ in that:
Solution: Cowan (2001) estimates working-memory capacity at ~4 chunks; Miller's earlier estimate was 7±2. Long-term memory is essentially unlimited; the work of studying is transferring information from one to the other.
Question 2
"Chunking" reduces cognitive load by:
Solution: A student who chunks "the four causes of WWI" into MAIN (Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism) uses one working-memory slot instead of four. Chunking is the cheapest cognitive-load reduction.
Question 3
According to Cepeda et al. (2006) and Dunlosky et al. (2013), which study schedule produces the best long-term retention?
Solution: The spacing effect is one of the most robust findings in cognitive science. Same total time, but spread across days at expanding intervals (e.g., Day 1, Day 2, Day 4, Day 7), produces dramatically better retention than the same time crammed into one session.
Topics 3.3–3.4 — Retrieval Practice, Mnemonics & Concept Maps
Question 4
The "testing effect" (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006) shows that:
Solution: The act of retrieving (without the safety net of the page) strengthens the memory trace more than equivalent rereading. The "fluency illusion" of rereading produces unwarranted confidence — easy now, hard on the test.
Question 5
Mnemonics (like SMART, MAIN, BEDMAS) are the right tool for:
Solution: The rule of thumb: mnemonics for items, concept maps for relationships. Mnemonics help recall structured lists. Understanding the underlying mechanism requires a different approach.
Question 6
Concept maps (Novak & Cañas) are best used to:
Solution: Concept maps are the right tool when material is fundamentally relational — like causal chains, biological systems, or historical interrelationships. Drawing a concept map closed-book is itself a retrieval-practice activity.
Topics 3.5–3.6 — Test Strategies & Test Anxiety
Question 7
When taking a multiple-choice test, the best practice for changing answers is:
Solution: Research is clear that first instincts are usually right; changing on doubt alone tends to make answers worse. However, if you have a specific reason (you re-read the stem and notice you misread it; you remembered a relevant fact) — change.
Question 8
The most useful first step on an essay test is:
Solution: Essay-test planning is not wasted time — it is the highest-leverage time. Students who start writing without a plan often discover mid-essay that they have no structure, and the result is rambling. 5–10 minutes of planning produces dramatically better essays.
Question 9
Park, Ramirez & Beilock (2014) showed that 5–10 minutes of journaling about test-related worries shortly before a test can close the test-anxiety performance gap by ~50%.
Why does expressive writing help with test anxiety?
Solution: Test anxiety occupies working-memory capacity that should be doing the test. Putting the worry on paper offloads it, freeing the working-memory slots. The effect is mechanical, not magic.
Question 10
Avery gets back a Functions test with 64%. Of 12 lost marks: 3 careless arithmetic, 4 concept errors (rational vs radical function domains), 3 gap errors (transformations not studied), 2 format errors (didn't show work).
Applying error analysis, what is the correct next-action for the "gap errors"?
Solution: Each error category points to a different next-action. Gap errors → add to spaced rotation + practice. Careless → add estimation/check-signs habit. Concept → re-watch lesson + retrieval practice. Format → in-test strategy. The right diagnosis enables the right intervention.
Question 11
What is the "fluency illusion" in study technique?
Solution: The fluency illusion is the central reason students re-read instead of retrieve. The page is doing the recognition work; closing the book reveals what you actually retained. The discomfort of retrieval-practice is the "desirable difficulty" — exactly the work that produces lasting memory.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain why pairing spaced repetition with retrieval practice is more powerful than either technique alone.
Sample: Spaced repetition without retrieval is just spaced rereading — you get the spacing schedule but not the retrieval strengthening, and the gain is smaller. Retrieval without spacing produces some learning but doesn't combat the forgetting curve. Together, spacing puts the retrieval acts at moments when partial forgetting has begun (so the retrieval is effortful) — which Bjork's "desirable difficulties" framework predicts produces the strongest, longest-lasting memory.
