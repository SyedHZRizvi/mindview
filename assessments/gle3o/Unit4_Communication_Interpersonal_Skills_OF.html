[2]
The four communication styles in Alberti & Emmons' framework are:
Answer Key:

B. The classic four styles. Assertive communication is associated with the best long-run outcomes and is learnable for those whose default is one of the other three.

2
[2]
The "I-statement" structure is:
Answer Key:

B. The three-part assertiveness structure: name your feeling, name the specific behaviour, name the impact.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) paraphrasing as an active-listening technique; (b) Mehrabian's "7/38/55" finding (with the correct caveat about scope); (c) the "fluency illusion" — does not apply here, replace with: cultural sensitivity.
Answer Key:

(a) Paraphrasing — restating the other person's point in your own words to verify understanding ("So what I'm hearing is..."). Forces the listener to understand before responding and signals to the speaker that they've been heard. (b) Mehrabian (1971) — found that in emotional communication where verbal and non-verbal messages conflict, listeners weight non-verbal cues heavily (the often-misquoted 7% verbal, 38% tone, 55% body language figures apply to that specific case, not to all communication). (c) Cultural sensitivity — the ongoing habit of treating cultural difference as informational, including awareness of name pronunciation, religious observance, language norms, direct vs indirect communication norms, and Indigenous protocols. Marks: 1 each.

4
[2]
The three essential components of effective group work are:
Answer Key:

A. Clear roles (final-authority assignments), shared work surface (Google Docs/Notion), regular check-ins (Tuesday/Thursday 15-minute stand-ups, done/doing/blocked).

5
[3]
Identify the five Thomas-Kilmann conflict styles and describe when each is most appropriate.
Answer Key:

(1) Avoiding (low assertiveness, low cooperativeness) — appropriate for trivial issues or when emotions are too high; destructive as a default. (2) Accommodating (low assertiveness, high cooperativeness) — appropriate when issue matters much more to the other; destructive when used to suppress own needs over time. (3) Competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness) — appropriate in emergencies or hard-line ethical questions; destructive in collaborative work. (4) Compromising (medium on both) — fast, fair-seeming, often leaves both sides half-satisfied. (5) Collaborating (high on both) — best for high-stakes ongoing relationships; takes the most time. Marks: 0.5 per style named correctly with brief situational appropriateness.

6
[3]
List three digital-etiquette principles and explain why each matters in a Grade 11 context.
Answer Key:

(1) Match channel to message — text for quick logistics; email for formal/on-record; video for substantive/emotional; forum for shared ideas. Channel mismatch escalates. (2) Account for missing context — text strips non-verbal cues; cold-sounding messages are often unintended; mitigations include over-explaining when emotions are involved, deliberate use of emoji, channel-switch for hard conversations, read-aloud before sending. (3) Assume audience permanence — anything written digitally can be screenshotted, forwarded, archived; the "private chat" assumption doesn't hold; rule of thumb: would I be comfortable seeing this under my name in a school hallway? Marks: 1 per principle with reasoning.

Part B — Thinking [15 marks]
7
[5]
Priya is part of a four-person group project. Two teammates have missed two meetings in a row and aren't responding in the shared doc. Priya is angry. She drafts an aggressive message ("You guys are ruining this project, this is so unfair, I'm going to tell the teacher").
Apply the I-statement structure. In 4–5 sentences, rewrite Priya's message as a structured I-statement that addresses the situation without producing defensiveness. Then explain why this version is more likely to produce action.
Answer Key:

Sample rewrite: "I feel worried when the meetings are missed and there's no doc activity, because the deadline is Friday and I can't finish the project alone. Can we agree on one definite time this week to check in?" Why it works: (1) names a feeling without blame (worried, not "angry"); (2) names specific behaviour (missed meetings, no doc activity); (3) names impact (deadline, can't finish); (4) invites concrete next step (agreed time). The aggressive version produces defensiveness ("you're overreacting"); the I-statement produces action. Marks: 1 per I-statement element (3), 2 for the explanation.

8
[5]
Two students sharing a study space at home: one wants total quiet; the other listens to music while studying. Stated positions: "no music" vs "music on."
Apply Fisher & Ury's interest-vs-position distinction. In 5–6 sentences, articulate the likely underlying interests of each side and propose two options that could serve both interests.
Answer Key:

Underlying interests — Quiet-wanter: focus / retention ("lyrics break my concentration; I need to retain this material"); Music-listener: reduced anxiety / familiar background ("silence makes me anxious; music helps me concentrate"). Options: (a) headphones for the music listener (preserves both); (b) lyric-free background music both can agree on (instrumental playlists); (c) time-share the workspace (mornings quiet, afternoons music). The interests reconcile where the positions don't. Marks: 1 per interest excavated (2), 2 for plausible options, 1 synthesis showing the position-vs-interest move.

9
[5]
Apply the "two stars and a wish" feedback structure. Imagine a peer has written a draft essay with a clear introduction, strong quotation analysis in body paragraph 2, but a paragraph 3 that feels disconnected from the thesis. Write the feedback you would deliver — actual sentences — in 5–6 sentences using the structure.
Answer Key:

Star 1 (specific positive): "Your introduction is really clear — the thesis at the end gave me a precise sense of where the essay was going." Star 2 (specific positive): "Your quotation analysis in paragraph 2 is strong — the way you frame the quote and then interpret it for the thesis is exactly what a body paragraph should do." Wish (specific improvement): "I'd love to see paragraph 3 connect back to the thesis more explicitly — at the moment it feels like a separate observation without showing how it supports the argument. Could you add a transition sentence at the start that names the connection?" Marks: 2 per star with specificity, 2 for wish specificity, 1 for actionability.

Part C — Communication [15 marks]
10
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) defending or contesting the claim: "Assertiveness and aggression are basically the same thing." Use specific evidence and contrast assertive with the other three styles.
Answer Key:

Strong contest: thesis ("assertiveness and aggression are opposite, not similar"); definitions (assertive = clear expression of own needs while respecting others'; aggressive = override others' needs); contrast with passive (suppresses own needs) and passive-aggressive (indirect); evidence that assertive communication produces best long-run outcomes (Alberti & Emmons); concrete example showing the same situation handled assertively vs aggressively. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence/definitions, 2 contrast, 1 conventions.

11
[5]
Write a 4–6 sentence email to a hypothetical teacher requesting a 48-hour extension on a major assignment because you have been sick. Use appropriate channel-and-register conventions (greeting, polite opening, specific request, parent-note offer, professional close).
Answer Key:

Look for: (1) appropriate salutation (e.g., "Hi Ms. ___" with the actual name); (2) brief polite opening; (3) specific, concrete request with names and dates (e.g., "I am requesting a 48-hour extension on the lab report currently due Friday"); (4) brief context with offer to provide documentation; (5) professional close with thanks. Marks: 1 each: salutation, opening, request specificity, evidence offered, close.

12
[4]
In 3–4 sentences, explain to a Grade 9 student (audience-aware register) why "paraphrasing" what someone said is more useful than rushing to reply, in a disagreement.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) use Grade-9-accessible language; (2) explain that paraphrasing forces you to understand before judging; (3) explain that it signals to the other person they've been heard; (4) connect to the de-escalation benefit ("it lowers the temperature so you can actually solve the problem"). Marks: 1 understand-before-judge, 1 signal-heard, 1 de-escalation, 1 register.

Apply the three components of effective group work (clear roles, shared work surface, regular check-ins). Pick a specific group project you are currently in (or imagine one). In 5 sentences, design: (i) the role assignments; (ii) the specific shared work surface (which tool); (iii) the check-in cadence and format (done/doing/blocked or similar).
Answer Key:

Marks: 1 specificity, 2 role detail, 1 shared surface, 1 check-in detail.

14
[5]
Scenario: in a real group conversation last week, you defaulted to "avoiding" (Thomas-Kilmann) when a teammate consistently arrived late. The issue is now bigger.
Apply the collaborative-resolution process (cool-off, state interests, brainstorm, evaluate, action plan with check-back). In 5–6 sentences, describe what you would actually do this week. Use I-statements where appropriate.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) acknowledge the cool-off step (if needed); (2) state own interests as I-statements (e.g., "When meetings start late, I feel rushed and stressed because we have limited time"); (3) invite the other to state their interests; (4) brainstorm at least one mutually-acceptable option (adjusted meeting time, structured start, asynchronous status update); (5) propose a check-back date. Marks: 1 each step of the process.

[5]
Cross-strand application: Integrate Strand A (Self-Knowledge) with Strand C (Communication). In 6–8 sentences, describe how knowing your communication-style default (from your learner profile) helps you (a) anticipate conflicts before they happen, (b) prepare for high-stakes conversations, and (c) choose group-work partners and structure check-ins.
Answer Key:

Strong answers integrate: (a) anticipating conflicts — knowing you default to passive lets you pre-plan I-statements; knowing you default to competing lets you pre-plan listening pauses. (b) Preparing high-stakes conversations — drafting an I-statement in advance, paraphrasing the likely response. (c) Group partners and check-ins — pairing with communication-style complements; structuring check-ins to surface concerns that your default would otherwise suppress. The cross-strand insight: self-knowledge from Unit 1 is the diagnostic; the communication skills from Unit 4 are the interventions; the cycle compounds. Marks: 2 per element (a/b/c) with specificity; 2 for synthesis statement.

📖 Complete Answer Key

This unit test covers Strand C's communication expectations (C1.1–C1.2, C2.1–C2.2, C3.1–C3.4, C4.1–C4.2) at all four Achievement Chart levels. Level 4 answers integrate concepts across topics — e.g., using an I-statement inside a collaborative-resolution process applied to a specific group conflict.

Common misconceptions to watch for: (1) Conflating assertive with aggressive; (2) treating communication style as personality, not habit; (3) believing private digital messages stay private; (4) reading own cultural norms as universal; (5) defaulting to one Thomas-Kilmann style across all situations.

Per Ontario's Growing Success (2010) Achievement Chart, Communication is one of the four equally-weighted categories (25% of the course mark). This rubric is applied to every student-written response (short-answer, extended-response, essay, lab report, deputation, etc.) on this assessment.
