Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[2]
Which of the following best describes Fleming's VARK framework?
Answer Key:

C. VARK (Fleming, 1987) sorts learners by their preferred mode of taking in new information. Many learners are multimodal.

2
[2]
In Self-Determination Theory, the three psychological needs that support healthy motivation are:
Answer Key:

B. Deci & Ryan's SDT (1970s-present) identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) metacognition; (b) self-regulated learning (Zimmerman's three phases); (c) growth mindset (Dweck).
Answer Key:

(a) Metacognition — "thinking about thinking"; the practice of deliberately planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own learning. (b) Self-regulated learning (Zimmerman) — three-phase model: forethought (task analysis, goal-setting, strategy choice), performance (self-control, attention, help-seeking), self-reflection (self-judgement, attribution, adaptation). (c) Growth mindset (Dweck) — the belief that ability is developed through effort, strategy, and feedback; contrasted with fixed mindset, which sees ability as essentially fixed.

4
[2]
Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences include linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, musical, and which of the following?
Answer Key:

A. Gardner's framework includes linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalistic (with existential proposed later).

5
[3]
Distinguish: (a) intrinsic motivation vs extrinsic motivation; (b) mastery goal vs performance goal.
Answer Key:

(a) Intrinsic motivation is engagement driven by interest, meaning, or alignment with values; the activity itself is rewarding. Extrinsic motivation is engagement driven by external reward (grades, prizes) or by avoidance of punishment. (b) Mastery goal targets improvement and learning ("I want to understand this better"); performance goal targets being judged favourably relative to others ("I want to score higher than the class"). Mastery orientation predicts persistence and deeper learning; performance orientation predicts short-term effort but more avoidance under difficulty.

6
[3]
Why is triangulation across multiple sources (inventories, performance data, self-observation, others' feedback) more accurate than relying on a single online quiz to build a learner profile?
Answer Key:

Any single inventory captures only one slice (a self-report from a single moment, vulnerable to wording effects and mood). Triangulation uses (1) inventories for structured snapshots, (2) performance data for what is actually working, (3) self-observation for lived patterns inventories miss, and (4) others' feedback for blind spots invisible to the self. Together these produce a richer, more actionable profile. Marks: 1 for limitation-of-single-inventory, 1 for naming multiple sources, 1 for the actionability argument.

Part B — Thinking [15 marks]
7
[5]
Ella gets back a math test with 58%. Her first thought: "I'm just not a math person." She catches the thought and re-writes: "I scored 58%. I haven't yet learned how to do quadratic word problems reliably." She does an error analysis: 4 careless arithmetic slips, 5 word-problem set-up errors, 3 conceptual factoring gaps.
Analyse Ella's response. In 5–6 sentences, identify: (i) which mindset her first thought reflects and which her re-write reflects; (ii) what the error categories tell her about her next study actions; (iii) why this is a stronger response than "just trying harder."
Answer Key:

(i) First thought reflects fixed mindset (ability framed as trait); re-write reflects growth mindset (ability framed as current state, signalled by "yet"). (ii) Each error category points to a different action: careless slips → estimate-and-check habit; word-problem set-up → 10 retrieval-practice problems over two weeks; conceptual gaps → re-watch factoring lesson, work 3 problems. (iii) "Just trying harder" applies undifferentiated effort without diagnosis; Ella's approach diagnoses the specific weakness and applies the matching strategy, which is what Dweck has emphasised — growth mindset is effort + strategy, not effort alone. Marks: 2 mindset diagnosis, 2 action analysis, 1 for the strategy-not-just-effort point.

8
[5]
Priya scores Visual=8, Read/write=6, Kinesthetic=4, Aural=2 on VARK. Her biology teacher delivers most content in lecture format.
Apply the concept of a "translation strategy." In 5 sentences, design a study plan for Priya that does not ask the teacher to change but does serve Priya's preferences. Include specific weekly actions.
Answer Key:

Translation strategy: take Cornell-style notes during the aural lecture (leveraging her read/write strength); within 24 hours, redraw each topic as a concept map or labelled diagram (leveraging her visual strength); for hands-on lab content, write a one-paragraph summary linking observation to concept (read/write + kinesthetic). Weekly: 30 minutes of post-class translation work, 15 minutes of spaced retrieval on concept maps, one Friday review of the week's diagrams. The strategy never asks the teacher to change — it changes Priya's post-class processing to match her preferences. Marks: 1 for the no-teacher-change point, 2 for specific translation actions, 1 for weekly cadence, 1 for connection back to her actual VARK profile.

9
[5]
Apply Self-Determination Theory: a Grade 11 student says, "I just can't make myself work on history." Diagnose what could be going on using the three SDT needs and propose one concrete intervention for each need. (5–6 sentences)
Answer Key:

Diagnose autonomy (do they have choice in topic? are there negotiable angles?), competence (are they getting feedback they understand? is the work too hard / too easy?), relatedness (do they have a study partner? a teacher relationship that supports the work?). Interventions: autonomy — negotiate a personal angle on the next essay topic; competence — request a brief teacher conversation about which specific skill to develop next; relatedness — form a study pair with one classmate to share the work. A strong answer treats "I can't make myself work" not as a character flaw but as evidence of unmet psychological needs and shows how each diagnosis points to a different action. Marks: 1 per need correctly diagnosed (3), 2 for the specific interventions linked to each need.

Part C — Communication [15 marks]
10
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) defending or contesting the following claim: "Learning-styles inventories like VARK are useless because the matching hypothesis has been refuted." Use specific evidence from research.
Answer Key:

Look for: (1) Clear thesis taking a position (most defensible: contest the claim, while acknowledging the empirical limit). (2) Acknowledge the Pashler et al. (2008) finding that matching does not improve outcomes. (3) Distinguish "useless" from "best used for self-awareness, not for matching." (4) Concrete use case for translation strategy. (5) Conclusion that VARK is a tool whose value depends on how it is used, not on the matching hypothesis. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence (including research citation), 2 interpretation/distinction, 1 conventions.

11
[5]
Write a 4–6 sentence one-paragraph "learner profile" of yourself, using vocabulary from Strand A: at least one learning-style observation, one mindset observation, one motivation observation (using SDT terms), and one metacognitive observation about your current planning-monitoring-evaluating habits.
Answer Key:

This is an applied-communication task — students articulate self-knowledge using Strand A vocabulary. Strong answers include: at least one learning-style observation tied to evidence; at least one growth-vs-fixed observation in a specific domain; explicit naming of which SDT needs are typically met for them and which are sometimes unmet; honest assessment of current planning-monitoring-evaluating habits. The strongest answers acknowledge a growth area and propose a concrete next action. Marks: 1 learning-style, 1 mindset, 1 motivation (SDT), 1 metacognition, 1 conventions and overall integration.

12
[4]
In 3–4 sentences, explain to a Grade 9 student (audience-aware register) what "growth mindset" means and one concrete thing they can do this week to apply it.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) use a Grade-9-accessible definition without jargon; (2) name the "yet" reframe; (3) provide a concrete weekly action (e.g., after every quiz, write one sentence about what you would do differently — strategy talk, not trait talk). Marks: 1 definition, 1 specific concept (e.g., "yet"), 1 concrete action, 1 audience-appropriate register.

Part D — Application [15 marks]
13
[5]
Apply the metacognitive cycle. Pick one upcoming task in your other coursework (an essay, a math test, a science lab, a presentation). In 5 sentences, write out: (i) your planning step (what's the task, what strategy fits), (ii) one mid-task monitoring check you will do, (iii) one evaluative reflection you will write at the end.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) name a specific real upcoming task; (2) plan: explicit strategy choice with reasoning; (3) monitoring check at a specific moment ("after problem 5 I'll check whether my approach is working"); (4) evaluation: a specific question the student will ask themselves at the end. The most common weakness: vague "I will work hard" answers that do not specify the metacognitive moves. Marks: 1 specificity of task, 1 plan, 1 monitoring, 1 evaluation, 1 internal coherence.

14
[5]
Apply the SDT framework. Pick one class you are currently taking. For each of the three SDT needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness), rate how well it is being met (low / medium / high) and propose one concrete action you could take in the next two weeks to raise the lowest one.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) name the class; (2) rate each need explicitly; (3) identify the lowest need; (4) propose a specific action — not vague "try harder" but, e.g., "request a 5-minute check-in with the teacher to clarify the rubric" or "form a study pair with one classmate by Friday." Marks: 1 specificity of class, 3 for the three need ratings, 1 for the concrete action linked to the lowest need.

15
[5]
Cross-strand application: The Ministry's Creating Pathways to Success document starts career planning with "Who am I?" In 6–8 sentences, explain how the self-knowledge tools in Strand A (learning styles, multiple intelligences, mindset, metacognition, SDT) should feed into the choices a Grade 11 student makes about (a) Grade 12 course selection, (b) postsecondary pathway, (c) communication and group-work choices in school.
Answer Key:

Look for: (1) Course selection — student-knowledge of their stamina, strengths, and which courses serve their target pathway should inform load decisions; e.g., if math is a competence-strain, sequencing matters. (2) Postsecondary pathway — Multiple Intelligences and RIASEC profiles inform whether apprenticeship, college, university, or workplace fits; e.g., strong bodily-kinesthetic + interpersonal might suggest paramedic college over university computer science. (3) Communication/group-work — knowing your default style and where your relatedness needs are met helps you choose group partners, advocate for needed support, and structure feedback. A Level-4 response treats Strand A as practical — feeding decisions, not just for "knowing yourself" abstractly. Marks: 2 each domain (6), 2 synthesis showing how the self-knowledge is the input across all three.

📖 Complete Answer Key

This unit test covers all of Strand A's expectations (A1.1, A1.2, A1.3, A2.1, A2.2, A2.3, A2.4, A3.1, A3.2, A3.3) at all four Achievement Chart levels.

Marking notes for short answer / application: award marks for specificity, evidence, and accurate use of Strand A vocabulary. Level 4 answers integrate concepts across topics (e.g., apply metacognitive monitoring while diagnosing an SDT need). Level 3 answers apply concepts correctly within a single topic. Level 2 answers show partial understanding with vocabulary errors. Level 1 answers restate the question without applying it.

Common misconceptions to watch for: (1) Treating learning styles as fixed identities; (2) confusing growth mindset with "just positive thinking"; (3) treating motivation as a character trait rather than as evidence of need satisfaction; (4) reducing metacognition to vague "reflection."

Rubric for the 5-mark applied questions (Q13–Q15): Level 4 (5/5) — specific, evidenced, integrative; Level 3 (4/5) — correct application within a single concept; Level 2 (3/5) — partial application with vocabulary errors; Level 1 (1–2/5) — restates without applying.
