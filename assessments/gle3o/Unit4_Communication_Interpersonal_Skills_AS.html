Topics 4.1–4.2 — Communication Styles & Listening
Question 1
The four communication styles are passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, and:
Solution: Assertive communication — clear expression of one's own needs while respecting others' — is the style associated with the best long-run outcomes. It is learnable, even for people whose default is one of the other three.
Question 2
The "I-statement" structure has three parts:
Solution: "I feel... when... because..." names your feeling without blaming, the specific behaviour without generalising, and the impact in a way the other person can address. The "you" structure invites defensiveness; the I-statement invites a change.
Question 3
Daniel and his friend Alex are arguing. Daniel feels himself getting defensive — his old pattern is to formulate counter-arguments while Alex is still talking.
What active-listening technique would most defuse this situation?
Solution: Paraphrasing forces you to understand before you judge. It also signals to the other person that you actually heard them, which lowers the emotional temperature and creates space for a productive exchange.
Topics 4.3–4.4 — Feedback & Group Work
Question 4
The SBI feedback structure stands for:
Solution: SBI (Center for Creative Leadership) focuses on Situation–Behaviour–Impact, keeping feedback specific and observable. It avoids character claims and invites change rather than defence.
Question 5
Which is the best structure for giving feedback?
Solution: Constructive feedback is specific, timely, balanced, and actionable. Structures like "two stars and a wish" (two specific positives, one specific improvement) and WWW/EBI (What Worked Well / Even Better If) make feedback land.
Question 6
The three components of effective group work are:
Solution: Strong group work is structural, not lucky. Clear roles prevent re-litigated decisions; shared surfaces produce visibility; regular check-ins surface blocks early. Combine all three and most group-project failures don't happen.
Topics 4.5–4.6 — Conflict, Digital Etiquette & Culture
Question 7
The Thomas-Kilmann framework identifies which five conflict styles?
Solution: Thomas-Kilmann (1974) maps conflict styles on assertiveness × cooperativeness. No single style is "best" — each fits different situations. The skill is style flexibility, choosing the style that fits, rather than defaulting to one.
Question 8
Fisher & Ury's Getting to Yes introduced which key distinction in negotiation?
Solution: Fisher & Ury (1981) showed that disputes stuck on incompatible positions often dissolve when each side names their underlying interests. "No music" vs "music on" — incompatible. Focus vs reduced-anxiety — reconcilable (headphones, instrumental playlists).
Question 9
The "match the channel to the message" principle in digital etiquette means:
Solution: Channel mismatch produces real friction — text for hard conversations strips out the non-verbal cues needed to convey nuance; long essay-style emails when a 20-second text would do waste time. The right channel depends on the message.
Question 10
A student sends a text in a "private" group chat that, three months later, is screenshotted and shared with their entire school.
What digital-etiquette principle is most relevant here?
Solution: The rule of thumb: write as if a future version of yourself, employer, or admissions officer might see it under your name. The "private chat" assumption doesn't hold. This is one of the most common Grade 11 sources of friendship breakups and reputation damage.
Question 11
A common pattern of cultural-sensitivity error is:
Solution: Cultural sensitivity is not a finished checklist; it is the ongoing habit of treating difference as informational. The most-common mistake is assuming your own norms are universal — leading to misreadings of eye contact, directness, personal space, gesture meanings, and conversational silence.
Question 12
Short answer: In 2–3 sentences, explain why "two stars and a wish" is a more effective feedback structure than feedback that only names what didn't work.
Sample: The "two stars" build trust — they signal that the giver has actually paid attention and recognises the strengths. Against that backdrop, the single "wish" lands cleaner: the receiver hears it as targeted improvement rather than as an attack on the whole effort. Without the stars, even accurate critical feedback tends to trigger defensiveness, which short-circuits the learning the feedback was supposed to enable.
