Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[2]
Which best describes the SQ3R reading method?
Answer Key:

B. SQ3R = Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review. The Recite step is the close-book retrieval that drives the learning.

2
[2]
Cornell notes have three zones:
Answer Key:

B. The Cornell system (Pauk) uses a narrow left "cues" column, a wide right "notes" column, and a bottom "summary" strip. Filling the cues column after class converts notes into retrieval-practice prompts.

3
[3]
Define each: (a) expository writing; (b) thesis statement; (c) three-part thesis structure.
Answer Key:

(a) Expository writing — writing whose job is to explain or synthesise an account; controlled by a "controlling idea" rather than a debatable thesis. (b) Thesis statement — one debatable sentence that the rest of the essay defends; not a topic, not a fact, not a question. (c) Three-part thesis — claim + "because" + forecast of evidence; e.g., "Macbeth's downfall is driven by moral disengagement (claim) because his hallucinations, his accelerating violence, and his isolation reveal a man losing his conscience (forecast)."

4
[2]
The recommended order of revision passes is:
Answer Key:

B. Content → structural → sentence-level → proofread. Doing proofreading first means polishing sentences that may later be cut at the content stage.

5
[3]
Identify and briefly describe: (a) estimation; (b) problem decomposition (Read-Plan-Solve-Check); (c) two evidence-based interventions for math anxiety.
Answer Key:

(a) Estimation — finding the order-of-magnitude answer before computing exactly, to catch decimal/order errors. (b) Problem decomposition — Read (twice, circle given, underline asked); Plan (substeps written before calculation); Solve (carry units); Check (against estimate, units, reasonableness). (c) Two interventions: reappraisal (re-reading bodily arousal as "preparation"); expressive writing (5–10 min pre-test journaling); also retrieval-practice and breathing. Marks: 1 each.

6
[3]
Describe the four-step graph-analysis protocol (Frame-Read-Analyse-Critique) and explain why the Critique step is where Level-4 marks live.
Answer Key:

Frame: title, axes (units, scale, log/linear), legend, source. Read: specific data points with numbers. Analyse: pattern, causal implications, third variables. Critique: what the graph isn't showing — axis truncation, selective time-windows, third variables, percent-of-base normalisation, deceptive visual design. Critique earns Level 4 because it shows the marker that you understood the evidence's limits — sound interpretation requires sound source criticism. Marks: 2 for the four steps, 1 for the Level-4 critique argument.

Part B — Thinking [15 marks]
7
[5]
Maya has 90 minutes to read a 20-page history chapter on the Treaty of Versailles. She currently reads straight through, highlights, and finishes. She retains ~30% by the next class.
Apply SQ3R. In 5 sentences, design a 90-minute study plan that uses SQ3R explicitly, and predict whether retention will improve. Justify your prediction with reference to retrieval vs recognition.
Answer Key:

Plan: Survey (5 min — flip through, note sub-headings, summary box). Question (3 min — turn each heading into a question on a sheet). Read + Recite (60 min — one section at a time, then close the book and write 2–3 sentences answering the question without peeking). Review (10 min — verify each answer against the chapter). Prediction: retention will improve substantially because the Recite step engages retrieval (Roediger & Karpicke 2006), which strengthens long-term memory more than rereading. The student is moving from recognition-mode (the page does the work) to retrieval-mode (memory does the work). Marks: 1 each step (4) + 1 justification linking to retrieval research.

8
[5]
Prompt: "Was the Treaty of Versailles too harsh on Germany?"
Write a three-part thesis statement (claim + because + evidence forecast). Then write the first sentence of three body paragraphs that would follow from the thesis. (5 sentences total — 1 thesis + 3 topic sentences + 1 conclusion-style sentence.)
Answer Key:

Sample: Thesis: "The Treaty of Versailles was too harsh on Germany because its war-guilt clause, its punitive reparations, and its territorial losses combined to destabilise the Weimar Republic — evident in the 1923 hyperinflation, the 1929 collapse, and the 1933 NSDAP rise." Topic sentence 1: "Article 231's war-guilt assignment immediately delegitimised the new German government." Topic sentence 2: "The reparations schedule, 132 billion gold marks, exceeded Germany's payment capacity and triggered the 1923 hyperinflation." Topic sentence 3: "Territorial losses (Alsace-Lorraine, the Polish Corridor, all overseas colonies) deprived Germany of resources and population, deepening economic vulnerability." Conclusion-style: "Together these provisions made the Treaty not a peace settlement but a slow-burning trigger for the catastrophe of 1933." Marks: 2 thesis specificity, 3 topic sentences each connect to thesis, plus internal coherence.

9
[5]
A Grade 11 chemistry student computes a stoichiometry answer of 659.7 g and does not estimate. Apply the Read-Plan-Solve-Check protocol. In 5 sentences, identify where the protocol would have caught a likely decimal-point error and what the consequence is of skipping it.
Answer Key:

Check step would compare 659.7 g against an estimation done at the Read/Plan step. If a 45.0 g glucose burn produces ~66 g CO₂ in estimation (\(\frac{45}{180} \times 6 \times 44 \approx 66\)), then a computed answer of 659.7 g is 10× too large — a decimal/order-of-magnitude error. The Check step would flag this in 5 seconds; the consequence of skipping it is loss of 3–4 marks on a single test question for an entirely correctable error. The estimation habit is among the highest-leverage 5-second investments in senior-grade quantitative work. Marks: 1 each step (4) + 1 quantitative reasoning.

Part C — Communication [15 marks]
10
[6]
Write one analytical paragraph (6–8 sentences) defending or contesting the claim: "Highlighting is a useful study technique." Use specific research evidence about retention, the role of retrieval, and the failure mode of over-highlighting.
Answer Key:

Strong contest of the claim: thesis ("highlighting is useful only as a minimal annotation tool — its high-volume use is a recognition activity that gives the illusion of learning"); evidence (Dunlosky et al. 2013 ranks highlighting as low-utility; over 10% highlighted yields no measurable gain); contrast with retrieval-practice (Roediger & Karpicke); recommended alternative (annotation with a small mark vocabulary, plus closed-book retrieval). Some strong defenders might argue minimal use is okay — but the strongest answers acknowledge that even useful highlighting is dwarfed by retrieval-based strategies. Marks: 1 thesis, 2 evidence, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

11
[5]
Write a 4–6 sentence sample short-answer response that demonstrates the principle "one sentence per mark, precise vocabulary, one specific example per requested sub-point." The prompt: "In 4 sentences, identify two strategies for managing test anxiety [4 marks]." Your answer should model what a Level-4 student would write.
Answer Key:

Sample: "Two evidence-based interventions for test anxiety are reappraisal and expressive writing. Reappraisal involves re-reading the bodily arousal of anxiety as 'preparation' rather than 'threat'; brain-imaging research shows this produces measurably better performance because the same arousal is interpreted as readiness instead of danger. Expressive writing involves 5–10 minutes of journaling about test-related worries shortly before the test; Park, Ramirez & Beilock (2014) showed this offloads working-memory interference and can close the test-anxiety performance gap by ~50%. Both interventions treat anxiety as a learnable cognitive-load problem rather than as a personality trait." Marks: 1 per identified strategy (2), 1 per evidence/example (2), 1 conventions/coherence.

12
[4]
Write 3–4 sentences explaining to a Grade 9 student (audience-aware register) why the "Recite" step in SQ3R is the part where most of the learning happens.
Answer Key:

Strong answers use Grade-9-accessible language, the recognition-vs-retrieval distinction without jargon, and a concrete example ("close the book and try to explain that page to a friend; the bit where you have to dig the answer out of your memory is where your brain actually builds the lasting memory — re-reading just shows you the page again, but doesn't make your brain do the lifting"). Marks: 1 distinction, 1 example, 1 audience-appropriate register, 1 accuracy.

Part D — Application [15 marks]
13
[5]
Apply the multi-stage revision protocol. Pick a real essay or written assignment you have produced (or one you are working on now). For each of the four stages (content, structural, sentence-level, proofread), name one specific thing you would (or did) change.
Answer Key:

Strong answers (1) name a specific real piece of writing, (2) identify one content-level change (e.g., "delete paragraph 3, rewrite to defend the actual thesis"), (3) one structural change (e.g., "move paragraph 2 to the position after the introduction"), (4) one sentence-level change (e.g., "cut 8 forms of 'to be,' replace with active verbs"), (5) one proofread (e.g., "fix three apostrophe errors, correct citation format"). Marks: 1 per stage with specific example.

14
[5]
A 2024 graph titled "Mean Global Temperature Anomaly, 1880–2020" shows a y-axis from \(-0.3\)°C to \(+1.1\)°C, source NASA GISS, with the line crossing 0 around 1920 and reaching about \(+1.0\)°C by 2020.
Apply the Frame-Read-Analyse-Critique protocol to this graph in 5 sentences. Include at least one specific data point in the Read step and at least one specific critique in the Critique step.
Answer Key:

Sample: Frame: title clear; x-axis 1880–2020 (linear); y-axis temperature anomaly relative to a baseline (graphs of this type typically use the 1951–1980 reference), in °C; source NASA GISS. Read: anomaly ~\(-0.2\)°C in 1880, ~0 around 1920, ~\(+1.0\)°C by 2020, with steeper slope post-1970. Analyse: pattern shows accelerating warming, particularly post-1970, consistent with anthropogenic-cause hypotheses. Critique: graph shows global mean only — does not show regional variation (the Arctic warms ~4× faster); does not on its own demonstrate cause (additional graphs of CO₂, solar irradiance needed). Marks: 1 frame, 1 read with specific data, 2 analyse, 1 critique with specific limitation.

15
[5]
Cross-strand application: Apply Unit 1's metacognitive cycle (plan-monitor-evaluate) to a specific upcoming essay-writing task. In 6 sentences, write out: (i) your planning step (which Unit 2 strategies — SQ3R for research? thesis structure? revision protocol? — fit this task and why); (ii) what you will monitor mid-essay; (iii) what you will evaluate at the end.
Answer Key:

Strong answers integrate Strand A's metacognitive cycle with Strand B's specific tools. Plan: explicit choice of SQ3R for research reading, three-part thesis for argument, multi-stage revision for editing — with reasoning. Monitor: a specific mid-task check (e.g., "after writing body paragraph 2, check whether each topic sentence flows from the thesis"). Evaluate: a specific reflection (e.g., "after submission, write 3 sentences identifying what worked, what didn't, and which strategy to keep next time"). Marks: 2 planning with strategy specificity, 2 monitoring, 1 evaluating; full marks require integration across the two strands.

📖 Complete Answer Key

This unit test covers Strand B's reading, writing, and numeracy expectations (B1.1–B1.4, B2.1–B2.3, B3.1–B3.4) across all four Achievement Chart levels. Marks rewarded for specificity and evidence; Level 4 answers integrate across topics (e.g., metacognitive planning applied to a multi-stage revision task).

Common misconceptions to watch for: (1) Treating revision as proofreading only; (2) confusing topic with thesis; (3) skipping the estimation step in numeracy; (4) skipping framing/critique in graph analysis; (5) believing assistive tech is only for IEP students.
