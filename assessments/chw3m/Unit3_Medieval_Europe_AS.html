Topic 3.1 — Byzantium
Question 1
The people we call "Byzantines" called themselves:
Solution: The term "Byzantine" is a later European invention (16th c.) designed partly to deny the eastern empire its Roman identity. Inhabitants always called themselves Romaioi — Romans.
Question 2
Justinian I's most enduring institutional legacy was the:
Solution: Justinian's law-code, compiled 529–34 CE, became the basis of all civil-law jurisdictions in modern Europe (and Quebec). Roman law's transmission through Justinian is one of the great legacies of the period.
Topic 3.2 — Feudalism & Society
Question 3
Modern medievalists like Susan Reynolds argue that the "textbook feudal pyramid" was:
Solution: Susan Reynolds's Fiefs and Vassals (1994) showed the neat pyramid was a later construction. Real medieval landholding was a patchwork — allodial property, military tenures, church benefices, royal grants in many combinations.
Question 4
"In Witelei there are 5 hides. There is land for 8 ploughs. There is in the demesne one plough; and 5 villeins and 4 bordars with 4 ploughs… It was worth 40 shillings; now 60 shillings." — Domesday Book, 1086
The Domesday Book is significant as a source because it:
Solution: Domesday is exceptional because it lets us answer quantitative questions about land use, peasant categories, and economic value at a precise moment — rare for medieval Europe.
Topic 3.3 — Church & Crusades
Question 5
The Investiture Controversy (c. 1075–1122) was significant because it:
Solution: Gregory VII vs Henry IV's clash over bishop appointments forced both sides to articulate ecclesiastical and secular authority as distinct. The Concordat of Worms (1122) made that distinction institutional — a medieval innovation with enormous modern legacy.
Question 6
A historically rigorous account of the First Crusade (1096–99) requires identifying at least:
Solution: Big historical events almost always need multi-causal explanations. Urban II's call was the immediate trigger; the response of 60,000+ people required preconditions in theology, politics, society, economy, and geopolitics.
Question 7
The Fourth Crusade (1202–04) is notable because it:
Solution: The Fourth Crusade was diverted to Constantinople and sacked the Byzantine capital in 1204 — Christian on Christian. The damage to Byzantium was severe and the Orthodox-Catholic estrangement permanent.
Topic 3.4 — Black Death & Universities
Question 8
The Black Death (1346–53) killed approximately:
Solution: 30–50% in Europe; comparable in Egypt and the Levant; total Eurasian deaths estimated 75–100 million. The deadliest pandemic in human history.
Question 9
A direct economic consequence of the Black Death in Western Europe was:
Solution: English agricultural day-wages roughly doubled (from ~3d to ~5d), real wages rose ~25–30% even adjusted for food prices. Eastern Europe imposed "second serfdom" instead; the two regions diverged.
Question 10
A Gothic cathedral such as Notre-Dame de Paris demonstrates:
Solution: Gothic cathedrals are the technical pinnacle of medieval architecture — the pointed arch, ribbed vault, and flying buttress enabled massive stained-glass windows. They were collaboratively funded by Church, kings, towns, guilds, and the faithful.
