Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [14 marks]
1
[3]
Identify three architects of Athenian democracy and their key reforms: Solon (594 BCE), Cleisthenes (508/7 BCE), Pericles (mid-fifth century).
Answer Key

Solon — cancelled debts and abolished debt-slavery for Athenians (594 BCE). Cleisthenes — reorganised citizenship into ten tribes across class lines, established the Assembly (Ekklesia) and Council of 500 (Boule) (508/7 BCE). Pericles — extended payment to jurors and the propertyless, opened most offices by lot to all male citizens, passed the 451 BCE citizenship law restricting citizenship to those with two Athenian-born parents. Marks: 1 each.

2
[2]
The Shakespearean / English-sonnet structure is to English poetry as polis is to classical Greek politics. Define polis.
Answer Key

B. Polis (plural poleis) = the politically sovereign Greek city-state, militarily self-defended by its citizens; over 1000 existed in the classical Greek world.

3
[3]
Identify three key Greek thinkers and one major idea associated with each: Socrates, Plato, Aristotle.
Answer Key

Socrates (469–399 BCE) — Socratic method of relentless questioning; "the unexamined life is not worth living." Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) — theory of Forms (timeless abstract patterns); the philosopher-king; Allegory of the Cave. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) — empirical observation; zōon politikon (human as political animal); virtue ethics centred on eudaimonia. Marks: 1 each thinker with idea.

The Roman Republic became the Roman Empire under Augustus in:
Answer Key

B. Octavian won Actium in 31 BCE but did not formally take the new title Augustus and assume permanent constitutional power until 27 BCE — under the fiction of "restoring the Republic."

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
"Our constitution does not copy the laws of neighbouring states… its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy." — Pericles, Funeral Oration (Thucydides, c. 430 BCE)
Apply SOURCE-CRITICISM to this passage. (a) Who composed the surviving text, when, and how? (b) What does "the many" mean here, and who is excluded? (c) What does the oration achieve as wartime propaganda?
Answer Key

(a) The speech survives only as Thucydides paraphrased it about 30 years later in his History of the Peloponnesian War; Thucydides admits he composed speeches expressing what he thought speakers should have said. So we have genuine fifth-century Athenian rhetorical ideology, not a transcript. (b) "The many" = the adult male citizen body, perhaps 30,000–40,000 men out of c. 300,000 inhabitants of Attica; women, slaves (one-third of the population), and metics are entirely excluded. (c) As wartime propaganda, the oration recasts the war as defence of a uniquely valuable political form, motivating continued sacrifice. Marks: 1 sourcing, 2 limits / exclusions, 1 propaganda function.

8
[4]
Why did the Roman Republic fall? Identify and briefly explain THREE structural causes — not personal-ambition explanations.
Answer Key

(1) Military reform — Marius's reforms (107 BCE) opened legions to the propertyless, who served for pay and looked to their general (not the Senate) for land on discharge. (2) Economic dislocation — slave-labour plantations (latifundia) were displacing small farmers, swelling the urban poor. (3) Political gridlock — the Senate could not accommodate reform; the Gracchi (133, 121 BCE) were killed for proposing land redistribution. (4) Provincial governance — became a private fief of senators extracting wealth. Caesar, Pompey, Crassus inherited this gridlock. The civil wars were the consequence of structural failure, not individual character. Marks: 1 each cause, 1 communication / synthesis.

9
[4]
"Why did Christianity become the dominant religion of the Roman Empire?" Critique any single-cause answer. Identify TWO or MORE distinct mechanisms (theological, social, demographic, political, cultural).
Answer Key

No single explanation suffices. Possible mechanisms: (1) THEOLOGICAL — personal salvation through faith was different from civic-transactional pagan cult. (2) SOCIAL — Christian communities provided welfare (widows, the poor, plague care) no Roman institution offered. (3) DEMOGRAPHIC — prohibition of infanticide / polygyny, remarrying widows produced higher net reproduction than pagan urban populations. (4) POLITICAL — Constantine's patronage (after 312 CE) gave imperial endorsement. (5) CULTURAL — Christianity was portable, not ethnic (unlike Judaism), and absorbed Hellenistic philosophical vocabulary. Mechanisms varied in importance by stage: pre-313 CE growth was bottom-up; post-313 CE was reinforced by state endorsement. Marks: 1 critique of single-cause, 3 mechanisms.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
In one well-developed paragraph (5–7 sentences), explain Plato's Allegory of the Cave and its political implications.
Answer Key

In Book VII of the Republic, Plato describes prisoners chained in a cave who can see only shadows cast by a fire behind them. The shadows are the only world they know. One prisoner escapes, sees the sun, returns to teach the others — and is killed. The shadows represent ordinary sense-experience, which Plato treats as imperfect copies of the true Forms — timeless abstract patterns. The political implication is that most people live in cognitive captivity; the philosopher who escapes to the sunlight has a duty (and a danger) of returning to teach. This grounds the philosopher-king doctrine of the Republic: ultimate authority belongs to the few capable of seeing the Forms. The doctrine is anti-democratic — it elevates intellectual elites above popular deliberation. Marks: 1 setup, 2 interpretation, 1 conventions.

11
[4]
Compare Athenian democracy with Spartan oligarchy on at least three dimensions (e.g., political institutions, citizen training, women's roles, treatment of unfree population).
Answer Key

Athens — direct democracy of adult male citizens via Assembly and Boule; education private/sophistic; women secluded with no political rights; slaves c. 1/3 of population, urban / household. Sparta — mixed constitution (two kings + Gerousia + Apella); state-controlled agōgē military training from age 7; women had more freedom (exercised in public, owned property — to be mothers of warriors); helots an enslaved Messenian agricultural population, controlled by terror. Both excluded most of their populations from political participation; both were militarily formidable; their contrast was central to ancient political philosophy. Marks: 1 each dimension; 1 communication.

12
[4]
"The Pax Romana (27 BCE–c. 180 CE) was a golden age." Write a 5–7 sentence paragraph qualifying this claim — what was true about it, and whose perspective is centred or marginalised?
Answer Key

The Pax Romana was real in many measurable senses: trade flowed from Roman Britain to Indian ports; piracy was suppressed; 80,000 km of roads connected the empire; coined money circulated everywhere. Elite mortality, civil-war frequency, and trade volume all favoured the period. But Edward Gibbon's famous celebration ("most happy and prosperous period in human history") was a slaveholder's view. From the perspective of slaves (perhaps a third of the empire), provincial peasants, women, conquered Britons, or executed Christians, "happy and prosperous" misses most of the story. Modern historians qualify the celebration without rejecting it — elite stability was unusual; mass experience was less unequivocal. Marks: 1 evidence of "golden age" claim, 2 critique/perspective, 1 conventions.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[4]
Apply historical-thinking concepts to the question "When did the Roman Empire end?" A strong response identifies a criterion of "ending" and defends it. Sketch THREE defensible answers, each with its criterion.
Answer Key

(1) 476 CE — if "political authority in the West" (deposition of Romulus Augustulus). (2) 565 CE — if "the last emperor to rule a re-united Mediterranean" (Justinian). (3) c. 650 CE — Pirenne's date, when Arab naval power closed the Mediterranean. (4) 1453 CE — if "the city that was the Roman capital" (Constantinople falls). (5) Never — if "the languages, laws, institutions of Rome" (Latin still alive as Romance; Roman law still alive in civil codes). Each answer is defensible; what matters is naming and applying the criterion consistently. Marks: 3 dates / criteria; 1 communication.

Answer Key

Possible examples: Greek — democracy as political ideal; deductive proof in mathematics (Euclid); the tragic-drama tradition; Hippocratic medical ethics. Roman — civil-law systems in modern Europe and Quebec; Roman roads / civic infrastructure; Latin-derived European languages; Roman / Christian Church organisation. Either — Stoicism and modern cognitive-behavioural therapy; classical architecture (Pantheon → US Capitol); citizenship as a portable status. Marks: 1 each, with brief explanation.

15
[4]
STSE Application: Eratosthenes (c. 240 BCE) measured the Earth's circumference using sticks, shadows, and arithmetic to within ~1% of the modern value. Why does this matter for understanding ancient science — and for understanding the popular myth that "ancient people thought the Earth was flat"?
Answer Key

Eratosthenes used the difference in noon shadow angle (~7.2°) between Syene and Alexandria, the known distance between them (~800 km), and elementary geometry to deduce Earth's circumference = ~40,000 km — within 1% of the modern 40,075 km. The achievement shows: (1) Hellenistic science was genuinely mathematical and quantitative — not pre-scientific. (2) Educated Greeks from at least Pythagoras onwards knew Earth was spherical; the popular myth of medieval flat-earthism is largely wrong (medieval scholars knew Earth was round). (3) The Library and Museum of Alexandria gave Eratosthenes access to measurements no individual could collect alone — INSTITUTIONS matter for science. Marks: 1 method, 1 result vs modern, 1 myth correction, 1 institutional point.
