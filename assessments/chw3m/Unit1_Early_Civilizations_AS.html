Topic 1.1 — Neolithic & Mesopotamia
Question 1
The Neolithic Revolution is best described as:
Solution: The term "revolution" (V. Gordon Childe) is metaphorical — the transition took thousands of years and emerged independently in at least seven regions (Fertile Crescent, Nile, Yangtze/Yellow rivers, Indus, Mesoamerica, Andes, sub-Saharan Africa).
Question 2
"If a man destroy the eye of another man, they shall destroy his eye. If one destroy the eye of a freeman… he shall pay one gold mina." — Code of Hammurabi (1754 BCE), clauses 196 & 198
The juxtaposition of these clauses tells us that lex talionis ("eye for eye") in Hammurabi's Code applied:
Solution: The two clauses together reveal Hammurabi's three-tier hierarchy: awīlum (aristocrats), muškēnum (commoners), wardum (slaves). "Eye for eye" applied only within the same class — between classes, fines substituted.
Question 3
Cuneiform writing was invented primarily to:
Solution: The earliest cuneiform tablets from Uruk (c. 3200 BCE) are receipts, livestock tallies, and ration records. Writing's purpose at origin was administrative; literature followed centuries later.
Topic 1.2 — Egypt
Question 4
Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the Nile."
The most important reason geography unified Egypt over 3,000 years was:
Solution: Unlike Mesopotamia's chaotic Tigris-Euphrates, the Nile rose gently and predictably; deserts east and west and cataracts to the south made Egypt geographically defensible and culturally continuous.
Question 5
The Egyptian concept of ma'at refers to:
Solution: Ma'at — cosmic order, truth, justice — was personally guaranteed by the pharaoh. When ma'at failed (drought, defeat), the pharaoh's legitimacy was in question.
Topic 1.3 — Indus & Shang
Question 6
The Indus Valley city of Mohenjo-Daro is distinctive among Bronze Age civilizations for:
Solution: The Indus shows extraordinary uniformity (brick sizes, weights, city plans) without identifiable palaces or royal tombs — a profile so unusual that it complicates the assumption that early civilization requires monarchy.
Question 7
"Crack-making on Yi-si day, Que divined: This evening it will not rain. King [Wu Ding] read the cracks and said: It will rain. On the night of Yi-si day, it really did rain." — Shang oracle bone, c. 1200 BCE
Shang oracle bones tell us that royal authority in Shang China rested on:
Solution: The Shang king (wang) alone could divine the will of royal ancestors. Religion and politics were one institution — ancestor worship legitimised political authority.
Question 8
The Mandate of Heaven (Tianming) was a Zhou-era doctrine that:
Solution: The Mandate of Heaven justified the Zhou overthrow of the Shang (c. 1046 BCE) and became the standard theory of dynastic legitimacy in China for 3,000 years — implying that rebellion against a corrupt ruler is Heaven's agency, not treason.
Topic 1.4 — Historical Thinking
Question 9
Historians compare the four river-valley writing systems on three axes: medium, content, decipherment. Which statement is accurate?
Solution: Cuneiform was deciphered in the mid-1800s via the Behistun inscription; hieroglyphics by Champollion in 1822 via the Rosetta Stone; Shang script is continuous with later Chinese characters. The Indus script remains undeciphered — no bilingual key has been found.
Question 10
A common student mistake in writing about early civilizations is to:
Solution: Anachronism — projecting later concepts (the modern state, universal human rights, democratic equality) onto pre-modern societies — is a basic methodological error. Bronze Age politics must be understood on its own terms before it is judged.
