Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [14 marks]
1
[3]
What were the Silk Roads? Identify three goods that travelled along them and one religion that spread.
Answer Key

A long-evolving network of overland trade routes (c. 200 BCE–c. 1500 CE) linking China through Central Asia, Persia, and the Mediterranean. Goods: silk, tea, porcelain, lacquerware (Chinese); spices, gems, cotton (Indian); horses, glass (Persian / Byzantine). Religions: Buddhism (India to China by 1st c. CE), Manichaeism, Nestorian Christianity, Islam. Marks: 1 description, 1 goods, 1 religion.

2
[2]
Zheng He's seven Ming-era Indian Ocean voyages took place in:
Answer Key

B. The seven voyages (1405–33) reached Calicut, Hormuz, Aden, Mogadishu, and Kilwa. The expeditions ended in 1433 — sixty years before Vasco da Gama reached Calicut.

3
[3]
Identify the FIVE STAGES of the Ontario Strand A historical-inquiry process.
Answer Key

(1) Formulating questions. (2) Gathering and organising evidence (primary and secondary sources). (3) Interpreting and analysing evidence (sourcing, contextualising, corroborating). (4) Evaluating and drawing conclusions (framing a defensible argument). (5) Communicating findings in an appropriate format. Marks: 0.5 each + 0.5 framing.

4
[2]
A primary source is:
Answer Key

B. Primary sources are period material; secondary sources are later analytical works. Both are essential to historical research.

5
[2]
Define historiography.
Answer Key

Historiography is the history of historical writing — how interpretations of the past have changed across generations of historians. It is the discipline's awareness of its own evolution. Marks: 2 (definition).

6
[2]
The "Columbian Exchange" refers to:
Answer Key

B. The Columbian Exchange transformed agriculture on both sides and brought catastrophic disease (smallpox, measles, influenza) to the Americas, killing perhaps 50–90% of indigenous populations within a century.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
"From Nanai-vandak to Varzakk, in Samarkand. I write to inform you that we have just had bad news from inside [China]. The Huns have invaded; the emperor has fled; Luoyang and Yecheng have been burnt; we three brothers and our families are still at Jincheng. From here to Dunhuang is dangerous. We do not know if we will be able to make it home. May the gods preserve you." — Sogdian Ancient Letter II, c. 313 CE
Apply SOURCING and CRITICAL INFERENCE. (a) What kind of source is this? (b) Why is its survival accidental? (c) What does it tell us that Chinese imperial chronicles cannot? (d) What is one LIMIT of using such letters as historical evidence?
Answer Key

(a) A private mercantile letter, in Sogdian (an Iranian language), from c. 313 CE; undelivered. (b) It survived because it never reached its destination — instead lay in dry sand at the abandoned Dunhuang watchtower for 16 centuries until found by Aurel Stein in 1907. (c) It gives a first-person Sogdian merchant's view of the chaos of fourth-century North China — confirming that Sogdian commercial networks reached deep into China and that a single Hun raid could disrupt trade for years. Chinese imperial sources record the events (fall of Luoyang to Xiongnu raiders in 311 CE) but from the centre, not from a merchant's perspective. (d) Limits: only a tiny number of Sogdian letters survive; the corpus is biased toward merchants writing to commercial partners; women's voices, peasants' voices, and slaves' voices are entirely absent. Marks: 1 each.

8
[4]
Why did the Ming abandon Zheng He's voyages after 1433, despite their evident success? Identify THREE distinct factors.
Answer Key

(1) Bureaucratic-Confucian opposition — court Confucians treated mercantile activity as morally low; the eunuch faction that championed Zheng He lost the factional war after 1433. (2) Fiscal cost — each fleet cost millions of taels; the treasury was under strain from rebuilding the Grand Canal and defending the Mongol frontier. (3) Mongol threat — the Northern Yuan and Oirat khanates pressed the Ming frontier; the 1449 Tumu Crisis (Oirat capture of the Ming emperor) confirmed that land defence had to come first. (4) Diminishing returns — tribute missions produced no net revenue. (5) Strategic conception — Ming China saw itself as the centre of a world order in which others should come; outward fleets were the opposite of the proper relationship. Marks: 1 each (any 3) + 1 synthesis.

9
[4]
Take any topic from CHW3M and identify whose voices are PRESENT in the available source record and whose voices are ABSENT or MUTED. Then propose two strategies for partially recovering muted voices.
Answer Key

Example — the Crusades: Present voices = Latin Christian chroniclers (Fulcher of Chartres, Robert the Monk), papal letters, Arabic chronicles (Ibn al-Athir), Hebrew memorial books (Sefer Zekhirah). Muted = peasant participants, women, the conquered city populations, enslaved war captives. Strategies: (1) Triangulate across the available sources — Hebrew, Arabic, and Latin together give a richer picture than any one alone. (2) Use archaeology and material culture — what people built and consumed often tells us what their words don't preserve. (3) Read elite sources for what they unintentionally reveal about the silenced (e.g., chronicler complaints about peasant motives). (4) Acknowledge silences as historical facts requiring naming, not flaws to hide. Marks: 2 present / absent, 2 strategies.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
Construct a STRONG research question for the culminating-inquiry project. Then justify why it meets the three criteria: researchable, debatable, significant.
Answer Key

Sample: "How do Marco Polo, Rashid al-Din, and the Mongol Secret History diverge on whether Mongol governance under Pax Mongolica produced cultural enrichment or only commercial advantage?" Justification: Researchable — three named contemporary primary sources exist in modern translation; a substantial secondary literature exists. Debatable — modern historians divide; multiple defensible interpretations of these sources are possible. Significant — the question bears on a major historiographical question (the value of Pax Mongolica) and on the broader methodological question of how cross-cultural comparison works. Marks: 1 question, 3 justifications.

11
[4]
In one paragraph (5–7 sentences), distinguish a thesis from a topic. Provide one example of each on a CHW3M-relevant subject.
Answer Key

A topic is a subject area you intend to research — broad, often a noun phrase. A thesis is a debatable, specific, supportable claim about a topic that organises your inquiry. The thesis answers a question; the topic is what the question is about. Example topic: "The Mongol Empire." Example thesis: "Comparing Marco Polo, Rashid al-Din, and the Mongol Secret History reveals that contemporaries' assessment of Pax Mongolica depended on their relationship to Mongol commerce — those who profited praised it; those who farmed under Mongol taxation did not." A strong inquiry essay is organised around its thesis, not around the chronology of its topic. Marks: 1 topic definition, 1 thesis definition, 1 each example.

12
[4]
"Historiography matters." Explain why understanding how historical interpretations have changed across the centuries is part of understanding history. Use the Crusades as your worked example. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

The same events get re-narrated by different generations of historians, often with strikingly different judgements about cause, significance, and consequence. For the Crusades: in Gibbon (1776–88) they appeared as bizarre medieval superstition; in 19th-century imperial Britain they appeared as heroic chivalric adventure; in Runciman (1951–54) they appeared as Christian-on-Christian and Christian-on-Jewish brutality; in Riley-Smith (1980s onward) they appeared as a serious religious project that historians must take on its own terms; post-2001 they reappeared as politically charged terms in 21st-century geopolitical conflict. None of these is "the right answer." Each illuminates what its era found significant. To do history well requires reading not just primary sources but also the historiographic conversation — what counts as evidence, what counts as significance, has itself a history. Marks: 1 thesis, 3 stages of Crusades historiography, 1 conventions.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[4]
Apply Seixas's SIX HISTORICAL-THINKING CONCEPTS to the date 1492. Identify what each concept invites you to ask.
Answer Key

(1) HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE — significant in retrospect because of Atlantic transformations; not significant by Ming Chinese or Songhai standards. (2) CAUSE / CONSEQUENCE — caused by Portuguese maritime experiments + Castilian Reconquista; consequences include Columbian Exchange, Atlantic empires, Industrial Revolution. (3) CONTINUITY / CHANGE — for Mediterranean and Asian networks, 1492 changed almost nothing immediately; for Americas, catastrophic population collapse began. (4) HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE — Columbus thought he had reached Asia; Native Americans had no way to know consequences; Ming Chinese had no awareness of 1492 for a century. (5) ETHICAL DIMENSION — the encounter began three centuries of conquest, slavery, and disease recognised today as genocide; modern Columbus Day vs Indigenous Peoples' Day disputes inherit this question. (6) EVIDENCE — primary sources include Columbus's journal (curated by Las Casas), Taíno oral memory (suppressed but partly recoverable), Spanish royal correspondence. Marks: 0.5 per concept + 1 synthesis.

14
[4]
Apply source-criticism to a hypothetical Marco Polo passage in which he describes Khubilai Khan's palace. Identify FOUR questions a careful historian should ask before treating the passage as straightforward eyewitness reporting.
Answer Key

(1) SOURCING — Who wrote it, when? (Polo dictated Il Milione in Genoese prison c. 1298 to fellow prisoner Rustichello da Pisa, a romance writer.) (2) CONTEXTUALISING — Polo's audience was European; the text would impress them, not Chinese readers. (3) CORROBORATING — Do Yuan-era Chinese sources confirm specific claims? (Some yes; some no.) (4) AUTHORIAL AGENDA — Polo and Rustichello may have inflated details to make the book sell. (5) LITERARY FORM — Il Milione mixes travel report with romance conventions, including some episodes Polo could not have witnessed. (6) ABSENCE — Polo mentions neither the Great Wall, foot-binding, nor tea — strange omissions for a 24-year resident. Marks: 1 each (any 4).

15
[4]
STSE Application: Consider how the historical-inquiry skills developed in CHW3M apply OUTSIDE history class. In 5–7 sentences, choose ONE 2026-era information context (news headlines, social-media posts, political rhetoric, scientific controversy) and explain how source-criticism, multi-source corroboration, and ethical-dimension thinking help you evaluate it.
Answer Key

Sample (news headlines about a contested event): Source-criticism asks who produced the headline, for whom, with what editorial pressures (a state-owned outlet has different incentives than an independent one). Multi-source corroboration asks whether the same claim appears in outlets with different political stances (cross-spectrum agreement is stronger evidence than within-bubble agreement). Ethical-dimension thinking asks whose perspective is centred and whose is marginalised in the framing (e.g., "civilian casualties" vs "collateral damage" — the choice of phrase is itself an ethical and political act). These are the same disciplines a historian applies to a Crusader chronicle or a Mongol-era account. The lesson: doing history is doing critical citizenship; the skills transfer to any moment in which evidence must be weighed in the face of competing claims. Marks: 1 context choice, 3 skill applications, 0–1 communication.
