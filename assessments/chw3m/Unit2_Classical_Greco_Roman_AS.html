Topic 2.1 — Greek Polis & Democracy
Question 1
Athenian democracy in the age of Pericles is best described as:
Solution: Roughly 10–15% of the population of Attica — adult male citizens with two Athenian-born parents (after Pericles's 451 BCE citizenship law) — could vote. Women, slaves (about a third of the population), and metics had no political rights.
Question 2
"Our constitution does not copy the laws of neighbouring states… its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy." — Pericles, Funeral Oration (Thucydides, c. 430 BCE)
A historical-thinking reading notes that Thucydides:
Solution: Thucydides explicitly says in his methodological preface that he composed speeches expressing what he thought speakers should have said. The oration is therefore genuine fifth-century rhetorical ideology, but not a transcript.
Topic 2.2 — Greek Thought
Question 3
In Plato's Allegory of the Cave (Republic, Book VII), the prisoners' shadows on the wall represent:
Solution: The shadows = sense-experience; the prisoners = ordinary humans; the escapee who sees the sun = the philosopher who perceives the Forms. Plato uses the allegory to argue for philosopher-kings.
Question 4
Eratosthenes, working in Alexandria around 240 BCE, used sticks, shadows, and arithmetic to:
Solution: Eratosthenes used the difference in noon shadow angle between Syene and Alexandria (~7.2°) and the distance between them (~800 km) to calculate Earth's circumference as ~40,000 km — within 1% of the modern value of 40,075 km.
Topic 2.3 — Roman Republic & Empire
Question 5
The Roman Republic's two consuls were:
Solution: Two consuls elected annually; mutual veto; both commanded armies. The institutional design was specifically intended to block any one man's accumulation of monarchic power.
Question 6
Octavian became the first Roman emperor under the new title Augustus in:
Solution: Octavian won at Actium in 31 BCE but claimed to "restore the Republic" in 27 BCE, taking the new title Augustus while in fact establishing the Principate. He carefully maintained constitutional fiction.
Question 7
The Edict of Milan (313 CE) and the Edict of Thessalonica (380 CE) marked two distinct steps:
Solution: Constantine and Licinius legalised Christianity in 313 CE; Theodosius I made it the official state religion in 380 CE. The 67-year gap matters for understanding how state-religion took shape gradually, not at one moment.
Topic 2.4 — Fall of Rome
Question 8
The "Fall of Rome" in 476 CE refers most precisely to:
Solution: 476 CE marks only the deposition of the last titular Western emperor. The Eastern Roman Empire continued to 1453 CE; Roman law, Latin, the Church, and Roman roads continued indefinitely. Periodisation is itself a historical argument.
Question 9
Henri Pirenne, working in the early twentieth century, argued the "fall" of Roman civilisation should be dated to:
Solution: Pirenne's Mohammed and Charlemagne argued that Mediterranean Roman civilisation continued until the Arab conquests divided the Mediterranean in the seventh century. This is one of many defensible periodisations of "the fall."
Question 10
A historical-thinking essay on Rome's fall should:
Solution: Periodisation is a historical argument, not a fact to memorise. A Level 4 essay names its criterion, defends it against alternatives, and acknowledges the historiographic conversation (Gibbon → Pirenne → Brown → Ward-Perkins).
