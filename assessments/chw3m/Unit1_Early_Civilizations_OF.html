Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [15 marks]
1
[3]
Define the term Neolithic Revolution, identify the approximate dates, and name three regions where it occurred independently.
Answer Key

The Neolithic Revolution is the slow transition (c. 10,000–4,000 BCE) from hunting-and-gathering to settled agriculture and animal domestication. The term "revolution" is metaphorical — the change took millennia. Independent regions include: the Fertile Crescent, the Nile Valley, the Yangtze and Yellow river basins of China, the Indus Valley, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and parts of sub-Saharan Africa (any three). Marks: 1 definition, 1 dates, 1 regions.

2
[2]
Cuneiform was invented primarily to:
Answer Key

B. The earliest cuneiform tablets from Uruk (c. 3200 BCE) are accounting records. Writing's purpose at origin was administrative.

3
[3]
Define the Egyptian concept of ma'at and explain how it shaped pharaonic responsibility.
Answer Key

Ma'at = cosmic order, truth, and justice. The pharaoh was personally responsible for maintaining ma'at; when it failed (drought, defeat, invasion), pharaonic legitimacy was in question. Ma'at made the pharaoh more than a king — he was the cosmic intermediary keeping the universe in order. Marks: 1 definition, 1 role of pharaoh, 1 connection to failure / legitimacy.

4
[3]
Identify each: (a) Hammurabi, (b) Narmer, (c) Shang king Wu Ding.
Answer Key

(a) Babylonian king (r. 1792–1750 BCE), author of the famous law code; (b) c. 3100 BCE pharaoh traditionally credited with unifying Upper and Lower Egypt (subject of the Narmer Palette); (c) Shang king (r. c. 1250–1192 BCE) whose oracle-bone divinations survive in large quantity. Marks: 1 each.

5
[2]
Which of the following Bronze-Age writing systems remains UNDECIPHERED?
Answer Key

C. The Indus script remains undeciphered — no bilingual "Rosetta Stone" has been found and no language can be definitively associated with it. Cuneiform was deciphered in the mid-1800s via Behistun; hieroglyphics by Champollion in 1822; Shang script is continuous with later Chinese characters.

6
[2]
Identify the Mandate of Heaven (Tianming) and explain its political significance.
Answer Key

Zhou-era doctrine (post-1046 BCE): Heaven grants the right to rule, conditional on just government; rebellion against a corrupt dynasty is Heaven's agency, not treason. The Mandate became the standard theory of Chinese dynastic legitimacy for 3,000 years. Marks: 1 definition, 1 political consequence (rebellion legitimised).

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
"If a man destroy the eye of another man, they shall destroy his eye. If one destroy the eye of a freeman or break the bone of a freeman, he shall pay one gold mina." — Code of Hammurabi, clauses 196 & 198
Use SOURCING and CONTEXTUALISING to analyse these two clauses. What do they reveal about Mesopotamian society? Identify what the famous principle "an eye for an eye" actually meant in practice.
Answer Key

Sourcing: inscribed c. 1754 BCE on a 2.25-m basalt stele at Babylon (now Louvre). Contextualising: Hammurabi's expanding empire needed uniform administration. Analysis: the juxtaposition reveals a three-tier hierarchy — awīlum (aristocrats), muškēnum (commoners), wardum (slaves). Lex talionis ("eye for eye") applied ONLY between members of the same class; between classes, fines substituted. The Code therefore reveals not equality before the law but a graded class system. Marks: 1 sourcing, 1 context, 2 analysis of class hierarchy.

8
[4]
Compare Egyptian and Mesopotamian religious worldviews. Why might the optimistic Egyptian afterlife and the pessimistic Mesopotamian underworld be linked to their respective river environments?
Answer Key

Egypt: optimistic religion, elaborate afterlife (Book of the Dead, mummification), pharaoh as living god — geography of predictable annual Nile flood, defended by deserts. Mesopotamia: pessimistic theology (capricious gods, dusty underworld), humans created to serve gods — geography of unpredictable violent rivers, exposed plain, repeated invasion. Environmental determinism in pure form is reductive, but the broad correspondence (predictable river → optimistic worldview; violent river → pessimistic) is striking and defensible as a contributing factor. Marks: 1 Egyptian worldview, 1 Mesopotamian, 2 connection to environment.

9
[4]
No palace, no royal tomb, no monumental king-portrait has been securely identified at any Indus Valley site. A careless reader concludes "the Indus was egalitarian." Apply HISTORICAL THINKING to assess this conclusion.
Answer Key

(1) Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — organic materials (wood, cloth) didn't survive; royal regalia may have decayed. (2) Yet the POSITIVE archaeology is striking: standardised brick sizes, uniform weights, grid-planned cities across 1000+ km imply SOME coordinating authority. (3) The mismatch — strong coordination, weak monumental display — is itself the historical puzzle. (4) Provisional reading: Indus political organisation may have been collective, ritual, or commercial rather than monarchic — but until the script is read, certainty is impossible. Honest acknowledgement of uncertainty is itself a historical-thinking skill. Marks: 1 each step.

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
In 4–6 sentences, define civilisation and explain why scholars now use the term cautiously.
Answer Key

"Civilisation" (from Latin civitas, "city") conventionally names complex urban societies with writing, hierarchies, monumental architecture, and surplus economy. Scholars now use it cautiously because: (a) the term builds in a value judgement (urban = civilised; nomadic / non-urban = not), which marginalises complex non-urban societies (Plains peoples, steppe nomads); (b) the conventional check-list of features (e.g., writing) excludes societies like the Inca that lacked writing but were politically complex; (c) the term's historical use was bound up with colonialism's hierarchy of "civilised" and "primitive" peoples. Marks: 1 definition, 2 limits / critique, 1 conventions / clarity.

11
[4]
In one well-developed paragraph (5–7 sentences), explain why writing was invented and how that origin complicates the romantic story that writing is a triumph of the human spirit.
Answer Key

Writing was invented to count things, not to express human feeling. The earliest cuneiform tablets from Uruk (c. 3200 BCE), Egyptian hieroglyphic inscriptions, Indus seal labels, and Shang oracle-bone records are all administrative or ritual. Writing emerged because complex bureaucracies needed to track grain, livestock, tax, and divinatory replies; literature followed centuries later. This origin complicates the romantic story (writing as triumph of spirit) because writing was, just as much, a tool of state surveillance and tax collection. The same technology that gave us Gilgamesh gave us the means by which Babylonian kings recorded who owed them how much wheat. Marks: 1 thesis, 1 evidence (specific dated examples), 1 complication, 1 conventions.

12
[4]
Define anachronism and explain why it is one of the most common errors students make in writing about early civilisations. Give two concrete examples of anachronistic reasoning.
Answer Key

Anachronism = projecting later concepts backward inappropriately into earlier eras. Examples: (1) calling Hammurabi's law a "constitution" — there were no citizens with rights; (2) treating "the state" in Bronze-Age societies as a modern nation-state with maps and borders — it was a king's tax-enforcement reach; (3) reading "equality before the law" into ancient codes — none of them imagined this; (4) calling Egyptian women "feminists" because they had legal personhood. Bronze-Age societies must be understood on their own terms before they are judged. Marks: 1 definition, 2 examples, 1 conventions.

Part D: Application [11 marks]
13
[4]
Apply historical-thinking concepts to the Shang oracle-bone tradition. (a) Why are oracle bones significant historical evidence? (b) Identify one SELECTION BIAS that complicates using them as evidence.
Answer Key

(a) Oracle bones are the world's largest body of contemporary religious-administrative text from the third millennium BCE — c. 150,000 fragments. They record real-time royal concerns (rain, harvest, hunting, military). They confirm Shang dynasty existence (denied as legendary until 1899 finds). (b) Selection bias: only "successful" predictions may have been recorded — failed prophecies thrown away. The surviving corpus is biased toward kings who appear divinely correct. Marks: 2 significance, 2 bias.

14
[3]
Apply historical perspective: in 3–5 sentences, explain why a modern reader should NOT call Bronze-Age societies "primitive" — and what term might be more accurate.
Answer Key

"Primitive" carries a 19th-century evolutionary hierarchy (savage → barbarian → civilised). Bronze Age societies were technically sophisticated (pyramid engineering, complex casting, mathematics) and socially complex (state structures, law, religion). They differ from modern societies in scale and technology, not in human capacity or intelligence. Better terms: "early," "ancient," "pre-modern," "Bronze Age" — descriptive rather than evaluative. Marks: 1 critique of "primitive," 1 evidence of sophistication, 1 alternative term, 0–1 communication.

15
[4]
STSE / Real-World Application: What enduring legacies of the Bronze-Age civilisations still shape life in 2026 CE? Identify FOUR concrete examples and briefly explain each.
Answer Key

Possible examples (any 4): (1) decimal place-value numerals via India → Arabia → Europe (used worldwide for modern arithmetic); (2) sexagesimal time (Mesopotamian: 60 minutes, 360°, still used in clocks and maps); (3) written law / codification of legal norms (Hammurabi → Justinian → modern civil codes); (4) the seven-day week (Babylonian astronomy → Jewish Sabbath → modern calendar); (5) monumental religious architecture (pyramids → cathedrals → modern temples and mosques); (6) state-administered taxation; (7) bureaucratic writing; (8) ancestor commemoration. Marks: 1 each example with brief explanation.
