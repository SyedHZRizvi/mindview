Part A — Knowledge & Understanding [25 marks]
1
[2]B · Early
The Neolithic Revolution occurred independently in at least how many world regions?
Answer Key

C. Domestication of plants and animals emerged independently in many regions; it is not a single-origin event.

2
[3]B · Mesopotamia
Define cuneiform, ziggurat, and lex talionis.
Answer Key

Cuneiform = wedge-shaped writing pressed into wet clay, c. 3200 BCE onward (originally Sumerian). Ziggurat = stepped temple-tower of Mesopotamian city-states, residence of the city's patron god. Lex talionis = retaliatory equivalence ("eye for eye"); in Hammurabi's Code, applied only between members of the same class.

3
[2]B · Egypt
The Old Kingdom is associated with which Egyptian achievement?
Answer Key

B. The Giza pyramids (Khufu, Khafre, Menkaure) are Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BCE). The Valley of the Kings is New Kingdom.

4
[2]B · Asia
The Mandate of Heaven is associated with which Chinese dynasty?
Answer Key

B. The Zhou justified their conquest of the Shang with the doctrine, which then became the standard Chinese theory of dynastic legitimacy.

5
[3]C · Greece
In one paragraph (3–5 sentences), explain why Athenian democracy was BOTH more inclusive than its contemporaries AND restrictive by modern standards.
Answer Key

Athenian democracy granted political voice to all adult male citizens regardless of wealth, paying jurors so the poor could serve, choosing many offices by lot — unprecedented inclusion of the propertyless in classical Mediterranean politics. But women, slaves (one-third of the population), and metics (resident foreigners, including those born in Athens) had no political rights. Roughly 10–15% of Attica could vote. Both descriptions are true at once.

6
[3]C · Rome
Identify each: (a) Augustus, (b) Constantine, (c) the Edict of Thessalonica (380 CE).
Answer Key

(a) Augustus — Octavian; first Roman emperor (formally from 27 BCE); founded the Principate. (b) Constantine (r. 306–337 CE) — emperor who legalised Christianity (Edict of Milan 313), called the Council of Nicaea (325), and founded Constantinople (330). (c) Edict of Thessalonica (380 CE) — Theodosius I's edict making Christianity the official Roman state religion and outlawing polytheism.

7
[2]D · Byzantium
Justinian I's Corpus Juris Civilis is the foundation of:
Answer Key

B. Justinian's 6th-century codification became the basis of civil-law (Romano-Germanic) systems used across most of Europe and in Quebec; common law is the English / Commonwealth tradition.

8
[3]D · Islam
Identify the FIVE PILLARS of Islam and briefly describe each.
Answer Key

(1) Shahada — declaration of faith. (2) Salat — five daily prayers. (3) Zakat — almsgiving (c. 2.5%). (4) Sawm — daytime fasting during Ramadan. (5) Hajj — pilgrimage to Mecca once in life if able.

9
[2]D · China
Bi Sheng's movable-type printing dates from approximately:
Answer Key

B. Bi Sheng used ceramic individual characters c. 1040 — long before Gutenberg's c. 1450 metal type. Block printing in China dates back further (Diamond Sutra, 868 CE).

10
[3]E · Networks
Briefly describe the Silk Roads and the Indian Ocean trade. For each, identify one good and one cultural product transmitted.
Answer Key

Silk Roads = overland caravan network (c. 200 BCE–c. 1500 CE) linking China through Central Asia, Persia, and the Mediterranean; carried silk, tea, paper, Buddhism, Manichaeism, the plague. Indian Ocean = monsoon-driven maritime network connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, China; carried gold, ivory, spices, Indian cotton, Islam, Buddhism, smallpox.

Part B — Thinking & Investigation [25 marks]
11
[6]B · Comparative Source Analysis
Source A — Code of Hammurabi: "If a man destroy the eye of another man, they shall destroy his eye. If one destroy the eye of a freeman or break the bone of a freeman, he shall pay one gold mina."
Source B — Twelve Tables (Roman, c. 450 BCE): "If a person breaks another's limb, there shall be retaliation in kind, unless he makes settlement with him."
Compare and contrast these two legal traditions. Address: (i) shared assumptions, (ii) one notable difference, (iii) what each tells us about the social structure of its civilisation.
Answer Key

Shared: both invoke lex talionis (retaliatory equivalence); both treat law as public (carved or published); both implicitly distinguish classes. Differences: Hammurabi specifies three named tiers (awīlum/muškēnum/wardum) with gold-fine substitutes between classes; the Twelve Tables allow private settlement as an alternative — anticipating the more flexible Roman jurisprudence. Social structure: Babylon = rigid graded hierarchy fixed in law; early Rome = patrician/plebeian conflict already prompting codification (the Twelve Tables were the plebeian victory of c. 450 BCE). Marks: 2 shared, 2 difference, 2 social structure.

12
[6]C · Causation
"The Roman Republic fell because of Julius Caesar's ambition." Critique this claim and offer a multi-causal alternative in 6–8 sentences.
Answer Key

Critique: single-cause explanations fail to account for why a Republic that had survived centuries of ambitious individuals (Coriolanus, Sulla, Marius before Caesar) collapsed in this particular generation. Structural causes include: (1) Marius's military reforms (107 BCE) made legions loyal to generals rather than the Senate; (2) latifundia displaced small farmers, swelling Rome's urban poor; (3) the Senate could not pass reform (the Gracchi were murdered in 133 and 121 BCE for trying); (4) provincial governance had become a private fief of senators extracting wealth. Caesar inherited a Republic that was institutionally incapable of peaceful reform. The civil wars of 49–31 BCE were the consequence of structural failure, not individual character — though Caesar's character was certainly a triggering element. Marks: 1 critique, 4 structural causes, 1 communication.

13
[6]D · Source Analysis
In the 1900s, European archaeologists at Great Zimbabwe invented elaborate theories that the site had been built by Phoenicians, Sabaeans, or the Queen of Sheba — anyone except indigenous southern Africans. Twentieth-century work (Gertrude Caton-Thompson, 1929) corrected the record.
Apply HISTORICAL THINKING to this episode. What does it teach about (a) the political functions of archaeology, (b) how researcher assumptions can override evidence, (c) what students should do when reading older histories?
Answer Key

(a) Archaeology was bound up with the political project of denying indigenous African capacity, which mattered for justifying colonial rule (Rhodesia, modern Zimbabwe). Stratigraphic evidence was overridden by ideology. (b) Researcher assumptions about race, civilisation, and indigenous capacity drove conclusions; the rejection of African construction was not an evidential conclusion but an ideological prior. (c) Students should read older histories — including textbook descriptions — as themselves PRIMARY sources about the era in which they were written, not as transparent windows on the past. The 1900s-1920s scholarship on Great Zimbabwe is now evidence about colonial-era racism rather than about Zimbabwe. Marks: 2 each.

14
[7]E · Source Triangulation
Source A — Ibn Battuta on Mali (c. 1352): "The Negroes of Mali are seldom unjust, and have a greater abhorrence of injustice than any other people."
Source B — Catalan Atlas (1375): "This Negro lord [Mansa Musa] is the richest and most noble king in all the land."
Source C — Tarikh al-Sudan (Sahelian chronicle, 17th c., reflecting earlier tradition): Describes Sankoré Madrasa in Timbuktu housing 25,000 students.
Use these three sources together to construct an argument about medieval West African civilisation. Identify what each contributes, what they corroborate, and what should be questioned.
Answer Key

Argument: Medieval West African states (Mali, Songhai) were politically sophisticated, economically wealthy, and intellectually serious — integrated into both Islamic and trans-Mediterranean networks. Source A (Ibn Battuta, Moroccan jurist-traveller, in person 1352) testifies to Mali's reputation for legal justice; Source B (Catalan Atlas, European cartographer 1375 working from Genoese-Maghrebi reports) testifies to European awareness of Mali's wealth and Mansa Musa's stature; Source C (Tarikh al-Sudan, internal Sahelian historical chronicle) testifies to Timbuktu's intellectual scale. Corroboration: all three independent sources agree on Mali's wealth, sophistication, and Islamic intellectual integration. Caution: Ibn Battuta and the Tarikh writers had reasons to favour Islamic Mali; the 25,000 student figure may be inflated by retrospective pride. Triangulation does not eliminate bias; it allows working past single-source distortions. Marks: 2 each source contribution + 1 synthesis.

Part C — Communication [25 marks]
15
[8]D · Extended Paragraph
In one well-developed paragraph of 8–12 sentences, explain the long-term consequences of the Black Death (1346–53). Address economic, demographic, religious, and social dimensions.
Answer Key

Strong response: The Black Death killed 30–50% of European, Egyptian, and Levantine populations within five years — perhaps 75–100 million Eurasian deaths in total. Economically, surviving labour scarcity roughly doubled wages within a generation; English day-wages rose from c. 3d to c. 5d, with real wages up 25–30% even after food-price inflation. Demographically, Western European serfdom collapsed under bargaining power that lords could not contain (the Statute of Labourers of 1351 failed precisely because neighbours wouldn't enforce wage caps). Religiously, the Church's monopoly on consolation was strained — clergy and monasteries died in higher than average proportions (since they tended the dying), and survivors lost confidence in clerical efficacy. Socially, the Jewish communities of the Rhineland were devastated by 1348–49 pogroms blaming them for well-poisoning; survivors moved east to Poland, where they were granted legal protection, founding the centre of Ashkenazi Jewish life until the Holocaust. Cultural responses included the danse macabre as a recurrent visual motif and Boccaccio's Decameron (1353), framed as ten Florentines telling stories outside the city. Eastern Europe took the opposite economic path — lords imposed "second serfdom" because the labour shortage made them desperate. The plague catalysed divergence between Western and Eastern European trajectories that lasted for centuries. Marks: thesis identification (1), four dimensions covered (4), specific evidence (2), conventions (1).

16
[8]A · Argumentative Essay
"When did the Roman Empire end?" Write a one-paragraph (8–10 sentences) defence of a single defensible answer. Name your criterion; consider one alternative; reach a defended conclusion.
Answer Key

Sample defence (any of multiple defensible answers): "The Roman Empire ended in 1453 CE, when Constantinople — the city Constantine had refounded as the New Rome in 330 CE — fell to the Ottoman cannon of Mehmed II. By this criterion — continuous self-identification of a state as Roman, ruling from the city the Romans called Rome — the empire that began with Augustus in 27 BCE persisted for 1,480 years. The conventional Western date of 476 CE marks only the deposition of Romulus Augustulus, a teenage figure who ruled almost nothing for almost no time; the Eastern Empire continued in unbroken political and institutional continuity for nearly a millennium more. Henri Pirenne's alternative 7th-c. date based on Mediterranean fragmentation captures something real but understates Constantinople's persistence. A historical-thinking essay must name its criterion (here: continuity of state in the city of capital), apply it consistently, and acknowledge what it foregrounds and what it backgrounds. The 1453 answer foregrounds institutional continuity and political identity; it backgrounds the cultural transformation from Latin to Greek and from pagan to Christian. Each defensible answer is a different question wearing the same words." Marks: 1 criterion, 4 defence, 2 alternative engaged, 1 conclusion/conventions.

17
[9]D · Synthesis
Compare and contrast TWO of the following medieval civilizational orders in 10–14 sentences: Latin Christian Europe; Byzantine Empire; Islamic caliphates; Tang/Song China. Address political organisation, religious institutions, and intellectual culture.
Answer Key

Sample (Islamic caliphates vs Tang/Song China): Politically, the Caliphate evolved from the Rashidun's umma-based leadership to the Umayyad/Abbasid hereditary monarchies; Tang/Song China inherited two millennia of imperial-bureaucratic governance and added the keju civil-service exam system. Religious institutions: the Caliphate institutionalised the umma through the ulama (Islamic legal scholars) and shari'a courts, with no centralised papal-style hierarchy; Tang/Song China integrated Confucian ethics, Buddhist temples (Buddhism reached its East Asian peak in the Tang), and Daoist traditions under imperial sponsorship — a syncretic rather than monotheistic landscape. Intellectual culture: Abbasid Baghdad's House of Wisdom systematically translated Greek, Persian, and Indian works, producing al-Khwarizmi's algebra, al-Razi's medicine, Ibn al-Haytham's optics, Ibn Sina's medical encyclopaedia; Song China invented movable type, paper money, the magnetic compass, and a Neo-Confucian synthesis (Zhu Xi). Both produced world-historic intellectual achievements that medieval Europe inherited only via translation — Aristotle reached the Latin West via Arabic; printing reached Europe via Mongol-mediated routes. Both civilisations far exceeded contemporary medieval European achievement. Marks: 3 political, 3 religious, 3 intellectual.

Part D — Application [25 marks]
18
[6]E · Periodisation
Apply historical-thinking concepts to "1492." (a) Why is 1492 conventionally treated as the end of the medieval world? (b) From the perspective of Ming Chinese, Songhai Africans, or pre-Mughal Indians, was 1492 significant? (c) Argue for or against periodising "the medieval world" ending in 1492.
Answer Key

(a) Western retrospective view: Columbus's voyage, Reconquista, expulsion of Jews from Spain, and Nebrija's Castilian grammar all in one year created a symbolic threshold; the Columbian Exchange and Atlantic empires that followed transformed the world. (b) From Ming Chinese (Hongzhi emperor), Songhai (Askia Muhammad), Vijayanagara, or Lodi-era Delhi perspectives, 1492 was unremarkable — no one outside Iberia knew about it for decades or centuries. The Atlantic-transformation framing is Eurocentric. (c) Defensible answers run both ways. For: the post-1492 world is genuinely structurally different (Atlantic empires, Columbian Exchange, eventual industrialisation). Against: the change took 200+ years to register globally; using 1492 obscures non-Atlantic continuities. A defensible compromise: 1492 marks the start of a long Atlantic transformation, not the end of a single medieval world. Marks: 2 each.

19
[6]A · Inquiry Design
Apply the historical-inquiry process. Sketch a culminating-inquiry project on a CHW3M topic of your choice: (a) the research question, (b) two primary sources you would consult, (c) two secondary sources you would consult, (d) a draft thesis, (e) one ethical-dimension consideration.
Answer Key

Sample (the Black Death and women's labour): (a) "Did the post-Black-Death labour shortage produce lasting changes in women's economic agency in fourteenth-century England?" (b) Primary sources: manorial court rolls (records of villein services); guild records from York or London showing women's craft enrollment. (c) Secondary sources: Judith Bennett's Ale, Beer and Brewsters in England (1996); Caroline Barron's "The 'Golden Age' of Women in Medieval London" (1989). (d) Draft thesis: "Post-plague labour scarcity opened economic opportunities for English women in brewing, dairying, and small textiles, but the gains were partial, short-lived, and concentrated in unmarried women — by the early fifteenth century, recovering male-guild dominance had largely re-closed access." (e) Ethical dimension: historical women have left fewer sources than men; reading the gendered archive requires acknowledging what the surviving record can and cannot tell us, and treating its silences as historical evidence about gendered power. Marks: 1 question, 1 primary, 1 secondary, 1 thesis, 2 ethical / synthesis.

20
[7]E · Modern Application
STSE Application: In 2026 CE, after the COVID-19 pandemic, what does the Black Death teach us? In 7–10 sentences, identify FOUR transferable lessons (epidemiological, economic, social, institutional) and reflect on what is and is not directly comparable.
Answer Key

(1) Epidemiology — pandemics spread along the same networks that carry prosperity (Silk Roads then; aviation now); rapid global integration accelerates both. (2) Economy — labour scarcity has lasting consequences (post-Black-Death wages doubled; post-COVID-19 labour markets show measurable wage pressure and shifting bargaining patterns). (3) Society — scapegoating follows pandemics; anti-Jewish pogroms then; anti-Asian harassment during COVID-19. (4) Institutions — public health requires institutional capacity; medieval Italian cities invented quarantine (quaranta giorni, Ragusa 1377); modern responses build on six centuries of public-health learning. NOT directly comparable: Yersinia pestis and SARS-CoV-2 have very different fatality rates; the Black Death lacked any biomedical theory of disease, while COVID-19 had vaccine development within a year; modern social safety nets did not exist in medieval Europe. The lesson: pandemics are recurring features of urbanised connected societies, and historical thinking offers humility about our own moment without imposing false equivalence. Marks: 4 lessons, 2 non-comparability, 1 communication.

21
[6]D · Multiple Perspectives
Apply the Mongol Empire as a case study in MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES. In one paragraph (7–10 sentences), construct how the same century of Mongol expansion was experienced by FOUR different actors of the period — a Venetian merchant, a Khwarezmian peasant, a Mongol khan, and a Yuan-era Chinese scholar.
Answer Key

To the Venetian merchant (c. 1280): an extraordinary commercial opportunity — overland routes were safer than ever before; goods, currency, and protection were available across the empire from China to the Mediterranean; Marco Polo's account is the canonical witness. To the Khwarezmian peasant (c. 1230): catastrophe — cities destroyed, agricultural infrastructure devastated, taxation harsh; entire urban centres of the Khwarezmid Empire never recovered. To the Mongol khan (c. 1260): the natural order — steppe peoples successfully exporting their political form, with capable administrators of many religions serving the empire (Christian, Buddhist, Muslim Mongol nobles co-existed). To the Yuan-era Chinese scholar (c. 1300): an awkward accommodation — Mongol political dominance but Chinese cultural and bureaucratic continuity; the Yuan was Sinicising fast; the keju was suspended then partially restored. Strong historical writing holds these perspectives in view simultaneously, refusing the binary "were the Mongols good or bad?" The Pax Mongolica was many things at once. Marks: 4 perspectives, 2 synthesis.
