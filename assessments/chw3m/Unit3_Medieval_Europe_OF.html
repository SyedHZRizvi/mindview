Part A: Knowledge & Understanding [14 marks]
1
[3]
Identify three reasons the Eastern (Byzantine) Roman Empire survived the Western collapse: geographic, economic, and military.
Answer Key

Geographic: Constantinople sat on a defensible peninsula commanding the Bosphorus, with massive Theodosian Walls. Economic: the East was wealthier and more urbanised, with Egyptian grain, Syrian textiles, and Indian Ocean trade. Military: the "themes" system gave defensive depth, with land grants to soldiers for service. Marks: 1 each.

2
[2]
Justinian's most enduring legacy is:
Answer Key

B. Justinian's law codification (529–34 CE) systematised Roman law into the form that has shaped civil-law jurisdictions in Europe and Quebec.

3
[3]
Define the following medieval terms: (a) demesne, (b) villein, (c) three-field rotation.
Answer Key

(a) Demesne — the land of the manor reserved for the lord's direct use, on which villein peasants owed labour services. (b) Villein — a peasant bound to the manor, owing labour to the lord, requiring permission to marry off the manor; not a slave (had customary rights) but not free. (c) Three-field rotation — winter wheat / spring oats and barley / fallow, c. 9th c. onward; increased yield by ~1/3 over earlier two-field rotation. Marks: 1 each.

4
[2]
The Investiture Controversy (c. 1075–1122) was resolved by the:
Answer Key

B. The Concordat of Worms (1122) compromised: kings approved bishops' temporal possessions; only the Church granted spiritual authority.

5
[2]
The Fourth Crusade (1202–04) is notable because it:
Answer Key

B. The Fourth Crusade was diverted to Constantinople; the 1204 sack permanently damaged Byzantium and embittered Orthodox-Catholic relations.

6
[2]
The Black Death (1346–53) killed approximately:
Answer Key

B. 30–50% — the deadliest pandemic in human history. Total deaths c. 75–100 million.

Part B: Thinking & Investigation [12 marks]
7
[4]
"In Witelei there are 5 hides. There is land for 8 ploughs. There is in the demesne one plough; and 5 villeins and 4 bordars with 4 ploughs. There are 6 acres of meadow… It was worth 40 shillings; now 60 shillings." — Domesday Book, 1086
Apply SOURCING and CRITICAL INFERENCE. (a) What sort of source is this and how/why was it produced? (b) What does the value-change (40 → 60 shillings) tell us about the manor? (c) Identify ONE LIMIT of Domesday as a historical source.
Answer Key

(a) A royal land survey ordered by William I in 1085, completed 1086, recording tax-eligible property as a snapshot — for fiscal/administrative purposes. (b) Value rose 50% between the Norman conquest and 1086 — suggests intensified land use (more ploughs) or harsher extraction by a new Norman lord. (c) Limits: Domesday recorded only what the king could tax, not what peasants consumed; under-reports the very rich and very poor; gives no peasant voice. Marks: 1 sourcing, 2 analysis, 1 limit.

8
[4]
Susan Reynolds argued (1994) that the "feudal pyramid" is a 16th-17th-century construction projected back onto a much more varied medieval reality. What evidence supports her position, and what does her critique mean for how Grade 11 students should write about feudalism?
Answer Key

Evidence: the tidy pyramid is documented only in late legal documents; real medieval landholding was allodial (outright), tenancies of multiple kinds, church benefices, royal grants, conditional service in many combinations. The word "feudalism" was not used by medieval people; it was coined retrospectively. For Grade 11 essays: use "feudalism" only with quotation marks or with specific qualifications; prefer more accurate terms (vassalage, lordship, manorialism, military tenure); acknowledge regional variation. Marks: 2 evidence, 2 implications for writing.

9
[4]
Why did so many people respond to Pope Urban II's call for the First Crusade in 1095? Identify FOUR distinct preconditions (theological, political, social, economic, or geopolitical).
Answer Key

(1) Theological — eleventh-century theology had developed warfare on behalf of Christendom as sin-remitting; growing emphasis on Jerusalem in popular piety. (2) Political — the Gregorian Reform papal claim to universal jurisdiction needed a worthy project; the Byzantine appeal offered one. (3) Social — many landless second sons of European knightly families needed something to do other than feud at home. (4) Economic — Italian maritime cities (Venice, Genoa, Pisa) had an interest in expanded Mediterranean commerce. (5) Geopolitical — the Seljuk advance into Anatolia after Manzikert (1071) had alarmed Byzantium enough to appeal to Rome despite the 1054 schism. Marks: 1 each (any 4).

Part C: Communication [12 marks]
10
[4]
In one well-developed paragraph (5–7 sentences), explain the doctrine and significance of the Mandate of Heaven OR the Investiture Controversy — your choice. (Both have lasting modern legacy.)
Answer Key

Investiture Controversy (sample): Gregory VII forbade kings/emperors from "investing" bishops with the symbols of their office, on the grounds that ecclesiastical authority must flow only from the Church. Emperor Henry IV resisted, was excommunicated, did public penance at Canossa (1077), was excommunicated again. The conflict festered until the Concordat of Worms (1122) compromised: kings approved temporal possessions; only the Church granted spiritual authority. The lasting significance is that for the first time, "Church" and "State" were structurally separated as distinct institutions — a medieval innovation with enormous modern legacy. Marks: 1 setup, 2 significance, 1 conventions.

11
[4]
Why did wages roughly double in post-plague Western Europe, and what were the social-political consequences? Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Black Death mortality of 30–50% produced a labour shortage that gave surviving peasants and urban workers enormous bargaining power. English wages roughly doubled (3d → 5d per day); real wages rose ~25–30% even adjusted for food inflation. Serfdom collapsed in Western Europe; the Statute of Labourers (1351) tried to freeze wages at pre-plague levels but failed because neighbours wouldn't enforce. Peasant revolts followed (Jacquerie 1358, Wat Tyler's 1381). Eastern Europe took the opposite path: lords imposed "second serfdom" — they were desperate, and weak central authority let them. Marks: 1 demographic cause, 2 economic / social consequences, 1 East/West divergence.

12
[4]
Describe the engineering and theological logic of a Gothic cathedral. Use Notre-Dame de Paris OR Chartres as your specific example. Aim for 5–7 sentences.
Answer Key

Gothic engineering used the pointed arch (distributing weight downward and outward), the ribbed vault (lightening the ceiling), and the flying buttress (external arches transferring lateral thrust from the roof to outer piers). Together these innovations allowed walls to be opened with massive stained-glass windows. Theologically, light flooding the interior was understood as the diffusion of divine truth (Pseudo-Dionysius's neo-Platonic Christian theology). Notre-Dame de Paris (begun 1163, substantially complete c. 1345) is divided into three vertical sections and three horizontal levels — mirroring the Trinity twice over. The cathedral was simultaneously a parish church, the bishop's seat, the coronation site, and a centre of pilgrimage — engineering and theology made architectural. Marks: 1 engineering, 1 theology, 1 specific example, 1 conventions.

Part D: Application [12 marks]
13
[4]
Apply historical-thinking concepts to the Crusades. Identify the ETHICAL DIMENSION — what are modern readers owed by, and what do we owe to, the various communities affected by these events?
Answer Key

The Crusades produced massive death and suffering, including: the Rhineland Jewish pogroms of 1096 (devastating Ashkenazi communities); the 1099 massacre at Jerusalem (Christian on Muslim and Jewish populations); the 1204 sack of Constantinople (Christian on Christian); centuries of Muslim memory of European invasion. Modern readers owe: (1) recognition of the violence on all sides; (2) honest engagement with both the religious motivation of participants and its catastrophic costs; (3) acknowledgement that medieval anti-Jewish pogroms are continuous with modern antisemitism; (4) caution about modern political invocations of "Crusade" that re-weaponise the medieval term. The ethical dimension does not require moral condemnation across all 900 years — it requires honest accounting of who suffered what at whose hands. Marks: 1 violence on multiple sides, 1 communities affected, 2 ethical responsibilities.

14
[4]
Apply medieval legacies to the modern world. Identify FOUR institutions or technologies still visible in 2026 CE that have medieval European origins.
Answer Key

Possible examples: (1) Universities — medieval inventions; Bologna (1088), Paris (c. 1150), Oxford (c. 1167), Cambridge (c. 1209). (2) Civil-law systems (continuing through Justinian's Roman law). (3) The conceptual distinction between Church and State (Investiture Controversy legacy). (4) Gothic architecture (still used in modern church / university design). (5) Canon law structures (still operative in the Catholic Church). (6) Mechanical clocks; water mills; later, gunpowder (from China via Mongols). (7) Trial by jury (12th-century Norman English innovation). Marks: 1 each with explanation.

15
[4]
STSE Application: The Black Death (1346–53) was a pandemic that killed perhaps 30% of Eurasia. In 2026 CE — after COVID-19 — what historical-thinking lessons does the medieval pandemic offer modern public health and history education?
Answer Key

(1) Pandemics are demographic events with very long economic / social consequences (post-Black-Death labour scarcity ended Western serfdom; post-COVID-19 labour markets show parallels). (2) Disease can spread along trade networks that also carry prosperity (Silk Roads carried plague AND goods; modern aviation carries COVID AND tourism). (3) Misinformation and scapegoating follow pandemics (anti-Jewish pogroms during the Black Death; anti-Asian harassment during COVID-19). (4) Public-health responses required institutional capacity — medieval Italian city-states invented quarantine (Ragusa 1377: forty-day isolation, quaranta giorni); modern responses build on centuries of public-health learning. The historical-thinking lesson: pandemics are recurring features of urbanised inter-connected societies, and humility about our own moment is a useful disposition. Marks: 1 economic continuity, 1 epidemiology / transmission, 1 scapegoating, 1 institutional response.
