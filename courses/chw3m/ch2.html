🏛️ 2.1 The Greek Polis & Athenian Democracy
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Greek polis (plural poleis) — usually translated "city-state" — was the dominant political unit of the classical Greek world from roughly the eighth century BCE to the conquests of Alexander. There were over a thousand of them, scattered across mainland Greece, the Aegean islands, the western coast of Asia Minor, southern Italy ("Magna Graecia"), and the colonised shores of the Black Sea. Each polis was independent, jealously sovereign, militarily organised around its citizens (not paid soldiers), and proudly distinctive in its laws, dialect, and patron deity. Athens worshipped Athena; Sparta, Apollo; Corinth, the merchant goddess Aphrodite. The polis was therefore not just a place. It was the place where a particular community made itself by its own laws — what the Greeks called autonomia, "self-legislation," the source of our English word "autonomy."

Of these poleis, Athens produced the single most influential political experiment in human history: democracy. The word itself is Greek — demos (the common people) plus kratia (power). Solon's reforms of 594 BCE cancelled debts and abolished debt-slavery for Athenians. Cleisthenes's reforms of 508/7 BCE reorganised Athenian citizenship into ten tribes drawn from across class lines, broke the old aristocratic faction-structure, and created the Assembly (Ekklesia) and the Council of 500 (Boule). Under Pericles in the mid-fifth century, all male citizens over 18 — regardless of property — could vote in the Assembly, hold most offices by lot, and serve in the courts as paid jurors. Roughly 6,000 men gathered every nine days on the Pnyx hill, voted directly on every public question by show of hands, and went home to their farms. This was direct, participatory democracy — not the modern representative system.

Athenian democracy was also fiercely exclusionary by modern standards. Women, even citizen women, had no political rights and could not own property in their own names. Slaves — about one-third of the population — had no rights at all. Resident foreigners (metics), even those born and raised in Athens, were also excluded. Pericles's citizenship law of 451 BCE tightened the definition still further: both parents had to be Athenian-born. So when we praise Athenian democracy as "rule by the people," we should specify which people — roughly 10–15% of the population of Attica. This is a recurring lesson in historical perspective: a society's self-description is not a neutral description.

Sparta, Athens's great rival, was almost everything Athens was not. It was a mixed constitution with two hereditary kings, a council of elders, and an assembly that could only approve or reject proposals. Its citizens (Spartiates, perhaps 8,000 strong at peak) were a tiny minority of permanent warriors, supported by the vastly more numerous helots, an enslaved labouring class held in permanent terror. Spartan boys entered the agōgē — a brutal state education — at age seven. Women in Sparta had more freedom than Athenian women: they exercised in public, owned property, and were trained to be mothers of warriors. The Athens-Sparta contrast is one of the foundational comparisons of Western political thought: the open society versus the closed, the deliberative versus the disciplinary, the wealthy commercial republic versus the agrarian military commune.

  • Polis — Greek city-state; politically sovereign, militarily self-defended.
  • Demos / kratia / demokratia — people / power / "people-power."
  • Solon, Cleisthenes, Pericles — three architects of Athenian democracy.
  • Ekklesia (Assembly) — direct legislative body of all male citizens.
  • Boule (Council of 500) — agenda-setting body chosen by lot from the ten tribes.
  • Sortition — selecting officers by random lot; a hallmark of Athenian democracy.
  • Ostracism — public vote to exile a leader for ten years; used to defuse tyranny.
  • Helots — Spartan state-owned agricultural slaves, mostly Messenian.
  • Agōgē — Spartan public education and military training system.

Worked Example — Reading Pericles's Funeral Oration

Thucydides records Pericles's funeral oration of 430 BCE in honour of the fallen of the first year of the Peloponnesian War. The speech famously declares: "Our constitution does not copy the laws of neighbouring states; we are rather a pattern to others than imitators ourselves. Its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy." A historical-thinking reading proceeds: Sourcing — the speech survives only as Thucydides paraphrased it about thirty years later in his History of the Peloponnesian War; Thucydides admits in his methodological preface that he composed speeches to express what he thought speakers would have said. Significance — the speech is the foundational Western articulation of democratic ideology. Limits — Pericles defines "the many" without mentioning women, slaves, or metics; "the many" means the male citizen body. Question — when Pericles says Athens "favours the many," who exactly counts? Answer: about 30,000–40,000 adult male citizens out of perhaps 300,000 inhabitants of Attica. The oration is both genuine political philosophy and a piece of wartime morale-building from a slaveholding empire.

  • Equating Athenian democracy with modern democracy — it was direct, not representative; restricted, not universal.
  • Forgetting that Athens was simultaneously a democracy and a slaveholding empire.
  • Treating Sparta as authoritarian and Athens as free — both excluded most of their populations.
  • Reading the Funeral Oration as journalism — Thucydides reconstructed speeches he thought speakers should have given.
  • Imagining "the West" began with Athens — most Greek poleis were not democratic.

🎭 2.2 Greek Philosophy, Drama, & Science
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

For roughly two centuries — from the late sixth century BCE through the fourth — Greek-speaking thinkers in city-states like Miletus, Ephesus, Athens, and Syracuse invented an astonishing range of intellectual practices that the modern world still uses: philosophical argument, geometric proof, deductive logic, historical inquiry, medical case-study, scientific cosmology, tragic and comic drama. Greek thought did not appear out of nothing — it inherited from Egyptian mathematics, Mesopotamian astronomy, and Persian administration — but its distinctive move was to treat the world as explicable in principle, separable from divine caprice. Thales of Miletus (c. 624–545 BCE) is conventionally cited as the first philosopher because he tried to identify a single material principle (water) from which everything is made. His Ionian successors offered competing principles (Anaximander's "boundless," Heraclitus's fire, Empedocles's four elements). What made these philosophy rather than mere speculation was their willingness to be argued with.

Socrates (469–399 BCE) wrote nothing. We know him through the dialogues of his student Plato and through the Athenian comic poet Aristophanes (whose Clouds mocks him). Socrates's contribution was a method: relentless questioning of any claim to certain knowledge, with the goal of exposing how little we actually understand. The "Socratic method" remains the basis of seminar teaching in modern universities. He was executed by an Athenian jury in 399 BCE — ostensibly for "impiety and corrupting the youth," but more deeply for the political crime of having been associated with the disgraced oligarch Critias and with the rejection of Athenian democratic certainties.

Plato (c. 428–348 BCE), Socrates's student, founded the Academy and produced a body of philosophical dialogues — the Republic, Symposium, Phaedo, Timaeus — that have remained central to Western thought. Plato argued that ultimate reality consists not of the changing material world but of timeless "Forms" — perfect, abstract patterns of which physical things are imperfect copies. The Republic sketches an ideal city ruled by philosopher-kings, makes a famous case against poetry as morally corrupting, and contains the allegory of the cave, in which prisoners chained underground mistake shadows for reality.

Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Plato's student and Alexander the Great's tutor, broke with the Platonic emphasis on transcendent Forms. He insisted that the form of a thing is in the thing — knowable through systematic empirical observation. He wrote on logic, biology, physics, ethics, politics, rhetoric, and poetics, founding a research tradition that dominated Western and Islamic intellectual life until the seventeenth century. His Nicomachean Ethics grounded virtue in habit and moral excellence in the rational pursuit of eudaimonia (often translated "flourishing" or "the good life"). His Politics famously claimed that "the human being is by nature a political animal" (zōon politikon), meaning that humans realise their nature only inside a polis. Greek drama (Aeschylus's Oresteia, Sophocles's Antigone and Oedipus Rex, Euripides's Medea, Aristophanes's Lysistrata) explored similar questions of justice, family, and the limits of human knowledge on the public stage of the Theatre of Dionysus. Greek science — Hippocratic medicine, Euclidean geometry, Archimedean physics, Ptolemaic astronomy — extended these methods into the natural world.

  • Philosophy — Greek "love of wisdom"; rational inquiry into knowledge, ethics, and reality.
  • Pre-Socratics — Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Empedocles, Democritus.
  • Socratic method — dialectical questioning aimed at exposing assumptions.
  • Plato's Forms — timeless, perfect abstract patterns of which material things are copies.
  • Eudaimonia — Aristotelian "flourishing"; the good human life.
  • Zōon politikon — Aristotle's claim that humans are by nature creatures of the polis.
  • Greek tragedy — Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides; staged at the festival of Dionysus.
  • Euclid (c. 300 BCE) — Hellenistic mathematician of Alexandria; axiomatic geometry.
  • Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BCE) — Coan physician; medical case-study, the Hippocratic Oath.

Worked Example — Reading Plato's Allegory of the Cave

In Book VII of the Republic, Plato describes prisoners chained in a cave who can see only shadows cast on the wall by a fire behind them. The shadows are the only world they know. One prisoner escapes, sees the sun, returns to tell the others — and is killed. A historical-thinking reading proceeds: What does the allegory claim? That ordinary sense-experience is a shadow of true reality (the world of Forms), and that the philosopher who escapes to the sunlight has a duty (and a danger) of returning to teach the others. What does it imply for politics? That most people live in cognitive captivity and that the genuine philosopher is owed political authority — the "philosopher-king" doctrine of the Republic. What might we critique? The doctrine elevates a tiny intellectual class above democratic deliberation; it inverts the Socratic emphasis on universal questioning. What is the historical legacy? The Allegory of the Cave shaped Christian Neoplatonism, Augustinian theology, Renaissance Platonism, and Enlightenment "Republic of Letters" — for better and for worse. Grade 11 students should be able to summarise the allegory's claim, identify its political implication, and locate one modern echo (e.g., The Matrix).

  • Treating Socrates as the author of dialogues he never wrote.
  • Calling Plato a democrat — he was deeply sceptical of Athenian democracy.
  • Imagining Greek philosophers worked in isolation; they were in dialogue with Persian, Egyptian, and (later) Indian thought.
  • Equating tragedy with sadness — Aristotle defined it as imitation of a serious action producing catharsis.
  • Forgetting that almost all Greek "scientific" knowledge was woven with assumptions modern science rejects (e.g., Ptolemy's geocentrism).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.5 — "describe characteristics of belief systems and intellectual traditions in classical civilizations (e.g., Greek philosophy, Confucianism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Roman religion, early Christianity)."

⚔️ 2.3 Alexander the Great & the Hellenistic World
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Alexander III of Macedon — known to history as Alexander the Great — was born in 356 BCE in the small mountainous kingdom of Macedon on the northern fringe of the Greek-speaking world. His father, Philip II, had reorganised the Macedonian army around the long-sarissa-pike phalanx, paid professional soldiers, and elite shock cavalry — and used this new instrument to subdue the disunited Greek poleis at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BCE. When Philip was assassinated in 336 BCE, Alexander, twenty years old and tutored as a boy by Aristotle, inherited a unified Greek world and a war plan against Persia. In thirteen years (334–323 BCE) he conquered the largest empire the world had ever seen — from Greece across modern Turkey, Egypt, the entire Persian Empire, parts of Central Asia, and into the Punjab — before dying of fever (or poison; historians still debate) in Babylon in 323 BCE, just under thirty-three.

Alexander's military achievements are extraordinary by any measure — he never lost a battle, his army marched some 22,000 km, he founded perhaps twenty cities (most named Alexandria, the most famous being the one in Egypt). But his deeper historical significance lies in what came after. With no clear successor (his only legitimate child was unborn at his death), his generals (the Diadochoi, or "Successors") fought a forty-year sequence of wars that eventually partitioned the empire into three large Hellenistic kingdoms: Ptolemaic Egypt (centred on Alexandria, ending with Cleopatra VII in 30 BCE), the Seleucid Empire (centred on Syria-Mesopotamia, eventually whittled down by Roman and Parthian pressure), and Antigonid Macedon. Each kingdom was Greek-ruled but governed populations dominated by Egyptian, Persian, Phoenician, Mesopotamian, and other peoples.

The Hellenistic period (323–30 BCE) is the era of intercultural exchange that Alexander's conquests made possible. Greek became the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean. Greek schools (the gymnasion) were founded from Egypt to Bactria. The library and museum at Alexandria, founded by Ptolemy I and his successors, gathered perhaps 500,000 papyrus scrolls and supported scholars including Euclid, Aristarchus (who proposed a heliocentric solar system in the third century BCE), Eratosthenes (who measured the circumference of the Earth to within a few per cent), Archimedes (a visitor; later killed at the Roman siege of Syracuse in 212 BCE), and the medical writers of the Hippocratic corpus. The synthesis of Greek and non-Greek traditions produced Greco-Egyptian gods (Serapis), Greek-Buddhist art (Gandharan sculpture), and the translation of the Hebrew Bible into Greek (the Septuagint) for the Jewish community of Alexandria.

The Hellenistic age also produced a noticeably more cosmopolitan and individualist culture than classical Greece. The polis was no longer the dominant political unit — kings governed multi-ethnic territories. New philosophies — Stoicism (Zeno of Citium, c. 300 BCE), Epicureanism (Epicurus, c. 307 BCE), Scepticism (Pyrrho of Elis) — focused on personal tranquillity in a world too large for any one citizen to control. Roman thinkers like Cicero, Seneca, Marcus Aurelius, and Lucretius later popularised these schools across the Roman world. Alexander's legacy was therefore not military but cultural and intellectual: he forced the small fractious world of the polis into a much larger conversation it could never reverse.

  • Macedon — kingdom north of Greece, militarily reorganised by Philip II.
  • Phalanx / sarissa — close-formation infantry; long Macedonian pike (5–6 m).
  • Diadochoi — "successors"; Alexander's generals who partitioned his empire after 323 BCE.
  • Ptolemaic / Seleucid / Antigonid — three principal Hellenistic kingdoms.
  • Alexandria (Egypt) — Greek-founded Egyptian capital; library and museum.
  • Hellenistic — "Greek-like"; the post-Alexander hybrid Mediterranean culture.
  • Lingua franca — a common language enabling exchange (here, koinē Greek).
  • Stoicism / Epicureanism — Hellenistic philosophies of inner tranquillity.
  • Eratosthenes — Alexandrian polymath who measured Earth's circumference c. 240 BCE.

Worked Example — Eratosthenes Measures the Earth

Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes — the chief librarian at Alexandria — measured the Earth's circumference using a remarkably elegant procedure. He knew that at noon on the summer solstice in Syene (modern Aswan), the Sun shone directly down a well, casting no shadow. On the same day in Alexandria, about 800 km due north, a vertical gnomon cast a shadow that gave an angle of about 7.2°. Assuming the Sun was effectively infinitely far away (so its rays were parallel) and that the Earth was spherical, the arc from Syene to Alexandria must be 7.2/360 of the full circumference. The full circumference therefore = 800 km × (360 / 7.2) = 40,000 km. The modern value is 40,075 km — Eratosthenes's measurement was within 1% of the truth using nothing but sticks, shadows, and arithmetic. The achievement illustrates three things: that Hellenistic science was not pre-scientific; that mathematical reasoning about the natural world predates the Scientific Revolution by 1900 years; and that scholarly institutions (the Library) matter — Eratosthenes had access to measurements no individual could collect alone.

  • Calling Alexander "Greek" without qualification — Macedonians were peripheral to classical Greek identity.
  • Treating Alexander's empire as politically stable — it fragmented within years of his death.
  • Confusing the Hellenic (classical Greek) and Hellenistic (post-Alexander) periods.
  • Forgetting that the medieval world inherited classical learning largely through Hellenistic Alexandria, Syriac translators, and the later House of Wisdom in Baghdad.
  • Imagining ancient peoples thought the Earth was flat — educated Greeks knew it was spherical from at least Pythagoras onward.

🛡️ 2.4 The Roman Republic — Senate, Citizenship, & Conquest
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Roman Republic (509–27 BCE) was, for most of its life, a small Italian city-state that became, in the course of three centuries, master of the entire Mediterranean world. The story Romans told about themselves began with the expulsion of the last Etruscan king, Tarquinius Superbus, in 509 BCE and the establishment of a "Republic" (res publica, literally "the public thing") ruled not by a king but by elected magistrates, an aristocratic Senate, and various popular assemblies. The two most important magistrates were the consuls — two elected each year, with equal authority, each holding veto over the other, who jointly commanded the army and presided over the Senate. This was a deliberate institutional check on tyranny: any one man's ambition could be blocked by a colleague.

Roman political life was always shadowed by class conflict. The patricians (a closed hereditary nobility tracing descent to the original senate of Romulus) initially monopolised political offices. The plebeians (everyone else — small farmers, urban poor, wealthy merchants, freed slaves' descendants) staged a series of "secessions" (literally, walk-outs from the city) between roughly 494 and 287 BCE, eventually winning tribunes (officials whose persons were sacrosanct and whose veto could block any magistrate's act), access to all major offices, and codification of the laws in the Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE). By the third century BCE, the patrician-plebeian distinction had largely faded; a new aristocracy of office-holders (the nobiles) ran the state.

The Republic's expansion was extraordinary and largely accidental. After unifying central Italy by the 270s BCE, Rome was drawn into the First Punic War (264–241 BCE) against Carthage over Sicily, then into the Second Punic War (218–201 BCE) — the war of Hannibal's elephants — and finally the Third Punic War (149–146 BCE), which ended with the literal salt-sowing destruction of Carthage. Simultaneously the Romans absorbed Greece (final defeat of Macedon at Pydna, 168 BCE), Asia Minor, southern Gaul, and Spain. Conquest brought enormous wealth, hundreds of thousands of slaves, and a flood of Greek culture (the Romans were the most enthusiastic intellectual borrowers in history). It also produced political crises the Republic was institutionally unequipped to handle: a class of veterans demanding land, an aristocratic faction defending traditional control, and ambitious generals (Marius, Sulla, Pompey, Crassus, Caesar) who realised that loyal armies could be turned inward as well as outward. The Republic's last century (133–27 BCE) was a slow-motion civil war that ended only when Octavian — Caesar's adopted heir — won the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE and became, four years later, the first emperor under the new title Augustus.

Roman citizenship was the key political technology that allowed a city-state to rule an empire. From an early date, Romans extended citizenship (or partial citizenship) to defeated Italian allies, then progressively to colonies, then to whole provinces. The Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE finally extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. This was a profoundly inclusive instrument compared to Athenian exclusivity — and it bound conquered peoples to Rome by giving them a stake in its laws and offices. The legacy is immense: Roman law became the foundation of every civil-law jurisdiction in the modern world (from France and Germany to Quebec and Latin America), and the concept of legal citizenship as a portable status is itself Roman in origin.

  • Res publica — "the public thing"; the Roman Republic.
  • Consul — one of two annually elected chief magistrates, each holding veto on the other.
  • Senate — advisory council of c. 300 (later 600) former magistrates; in practice the dominant body.
  • Patrician / plebeian — original aristocratic and commoner classes; later merged into the nobiles.
  • Tribune of the plebs — plebeian magistrate whose person was sacrosanct and whose veto could block legislation.
  • Twelve Tables — c. 450 BCE codification of Roman law; foundation of all later Roman jurisprudence.
  • Punic Wars — three wars (264–146 BCE) between Rome and Carthage.
  • Hannibal Barca — Carthaginian general who crossed the Alps with elephants (218 BCE).
  • Constitutio Antoniniana (212 CE) — extended Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire.

Worked Example — Why the Republic Fell

By 100 BCE, the Roman Republic faced four structural problems no consul could fix in a one-year term: (1) military — Marius's reforms (107 BCE) opened the legions to the propertyless, who served for pay and looked to their general (not the Senate) for land on discharge; (2) economic — slave-labour-driven plantation agriculture (latifundia) was displacing small farmers, swelling Rome's urban poor; (3) political — the Senate was structurally incapable of accommodating reformers (the Gracchi brothers, tribunes in 133 and 121 BCE, were killed by senatorial mobs for proposing modest land redistribution); (4) geographic — provincial governance had become a private fief of senators who treated their year-long postings as opportunities for extortion. Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus inherited this constitutional gridlock. The civil wars of 49–31 BCE were not caused by individual ambition alone; they were the consequence of a Republic that could not reform itself peacefully. The lesson — fundamental to Roman history — is that institutional sclerosis can be just as dangerous as foreign threat. Marks: 2 military, 2 economic, 2 political, 2 geographic, 2 synthesis.

  • Treating the Roman Republic as a democracy — it was a mixed constitution dominated by aristocratic interests.
  • Confusing the consuls (annual elected magistrates) with the emperors (later, permanent rulers).
  • Forgetting that Julius Caesar was never actually emperor; the title Augustus took in 27 BCE was new.
  • Imagining Rome conquered the Mediterranean by deliberate plan — most expansion was reactive.
  • Reading "republic" as our modern democratic-republican system — Roman senators were unelected lifetime members.

👑 2.5 The Roman Empire, Pax Romana, & Christianity
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

When Octavian — heir to Julius Caesar and victor at Actium in 31 BCE — settled the Roman state in 27 BCE, he claimed merely to have "restored the Republic." In fact he had inaugurated the Roman Empire. He took the new name Augustus ("revered"), assumed permanent command of the army, controlled the most lucrative provinces directly, and accumulated tribunician powers that made him simultaneously the de facto monarch and the formal protector of the people. The Senate kept its dignity but lost real power. Augustus's careful constitutional fiction — that he was merely princeps, "first among equals" — masked an absolute monarchy that lasted, with varying degrees of competence, until the Western collapse five centuries later. The system is called the Principate to distinguish it from the much more openly autocratic Dominate of Diocletian (r. 284–305 CE) and Constantine (r. 306–337 CE) onwards.

The first two centuries of the Empire (27 BCE – c. 180 CE) are conventionally called the Pax Romana, "Roman Peace." It is mostly accurate: the Mediterranean was at war less often than it had been in any era before or since; piracy was suppressed; trade flowed from Roman Britain to Indian ports; the road network connecting the empire stretched 80,000 km; coined money (the silver denarius) circulated everywhere. The empire reached its maximum geographic extent under Trajan (r. 98–117 CE), encompassing perhaps 60 million people — roughly a fifth of humanity at the time. Edward Gibbon, in his eighteenth-century Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, famously wrote that "if a man were called to fix the period in the history of the world during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous, he would, without hesitation, name that which elapsed from the death of Domitian [96 CE] to the accession of Commodus [180 CE]." Modern historians qualify Gibbon — slaves and provincial poor would not have agreed — but his point about elite stability still stands.

Beneath the imperial peace, the great cultural development of the period was the rise and slow institutionalisation of Christianity. Jesus of Nazareth was executed by the Roman governor Pontius Pilate around 30 CE on a charge of sedition. His followers, initially a Jewish sect, were spread across the empire by missionary travel (especially that of Paul of Tarsus, a Roman-citizen Jew of the diaspora, c. 5–c. 65 CE), persecuted intermittently for refusing to participate in the imperial cult (notably under Nero in 64 CE and Diocletian in 303 CE), and slowly grew. The Edict of Milan (313 CE), issued by Constantine and his co-emperor Licinius, legalised Christianity throughout the empire; the Council of Nicaea (325 CE), summoned by Constantine, produced the first universal Christian creed; the Edict of Thessalonica (380 CE) under Theodosius I made Christianity the empire's official religion and outlawed traditional polytheism. In four centuries Christianity went from a small persecuted sect to the state cult of the Mediterranean world.

The Empire faced the "Crisis of the Third Century" (235–284 CE) — twenty-six emperors in fifty years, runaway inflation, plague (the Antonine Plague c. 165–180 CE and the Plague of Cyprian c. 250–270 CE), and frontier pressure from Sassanid Persia in the east and migrating peoples (Goths, Franks, Alemanni) on the Rhine and Danube. Diocletian stabilised the empire by formal division (the Tetrarchy of two senior emperors and two junior emperors in 293 CE), administrative reorganisation, and ruthless tax-collection. Constantine moved the capital eastward in 330 CE to the new city of Constantinople — the choice would matter immensely: the eastern half of the empire would survive the crises of the next century while the western half slowly fell apart.

  • Augustus — title taken by Octavian in 27 BCE; first Roman emperor.
  • Principate / Dominate — early (constitutional-fiction) / late (openly autocratic) phases of empire.
  • Pax Romana — "Roman Peace," 27 BCE–c. 180 CE; relative stability across the Mediterranean.
  • Trajan — emperor 98–117 CE; under whom the empire reached its greatest extent.
  • Paul of Tarsus — Roman-citizen Jewish missionary who spread Christianity beyond Judea (c. 5–c. 65 CE).
  • Edict of Milan (313 CE) — legalised Christianity in the empire.
  • Council of Nicaea (325 CE) — first ecumenical council; produced the Nicene Creed.
  • Diocletian's Tetrarchy — four-emperor system (293 CE) stabilising the late empire.
  • Constantine — founded Constantinople (330 CE); patronised Christianity.

Worked Example — Why Did Christianity Win?

Historians have offered five main explanations for the Christianisation of the Roman Empire. (1) Theological — the doctrine of personal salvation through faith offered something traditional Roman religion (a transactional cult of civic gods) did not. (2) Social — early Christian communities provided welfare (feeding widows, burying the poor, caring for the sick during plagues) that no Roman institution offered. (3) Demographic — Christians prohibited infanticide and abortion, did not practise polygyny, and remarried widows, all of which gave them higher net reproductive rates than pagan urban populations. (4) Political — Constantine's patronage from 312 CE onward provided imperial endorsement and resources. (5) Cultural — Christianity was portable, did not require ethnic membership (unlike Judaism), and absorbed Hellenistic philosophical vocabulary fluidly. No single explanation is sufficient. Christianity grew slowly (perhaps 10% of the empire by 300 CE) before Constantine; then much faster after. Sociologist Rodney Stark estimated a sustainable 40%-per-decade growth — small at first, exponential later. A strong Grade 11 essay names two or more of these mechanisms and weighs which mattered most at which stage; a weak essay attributes the change to a single cause (usually Constantine, which over-credits one moment).

  • Calling Augustus the founder of "the Empire" without noting his careful constitutional fictions.
  • Treating Christianity's rise as exclusively top-down (Constantine) or exclusively bottom-up (martyrs).
  • Confusing the date Christianity became legal (313 CE) with the date it became official (380 CE).
  • Mistaking the Crisis of the Third Century for the "fall" of Rome — Diocletian and Constantine recovered.
  • Imagining all Romans worshipped one set of gods — religious life was extraordinarily diverse, with mystery cults of Isis, Mithras, and Cybele competing with Christianity.

🌫️ 2.6 The "Fall" of Rome & the End of Antiquity
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The "Fall of Rome" is one of the most over-determined and under-defined questions in the historical curriculum. The conventional Western date — 476 CE — marks the deposition of Romulus Augustulus, a teenage emperor of the western half of the empire, by the Germanic warlord Odoacer. But Romulus Augustulus had ruled almost nothing for almost no time, and the eastern Roman Empire (which contemporaries called Romania and modern historians call the Byzantine Empire) continued for another thousand years. So the question is not really "when did Rome fall?" but "what counts as a fall?" Asking that question is exactly the kind of historiographic discipline that Strand C of CHW3M is meant to teach.

If we restrict the question to the political collapse of central Roman authority in the western Mediterranean, the relevant period is roughly 376 CE (Gothic crossings of the Danube) to 480 CE (the death of Julius Nepos, the last western emperor recognised by Constantinople). Five overlapping factors are usually cited. (1) Migration / "barbarian" pressure — Goths, Vandals, Huns, Franks, Saxons, Lombards, Suevi, and Alans crossed the imperial frontier in increasing numbers, many fleeing the Huns of the steppe. (2) Military overstretch and recruitment crisis — the legions were increasingly composed of the same peoples they were supposed to be defending against. (3) Economic fragmentation — by the fifth century, large estates in Gaul and Spain operated semi-autonomously; tax revenue collapsed; cities depopulated. (4) Climate and plague — the "Late Antique Little Ice Age" (c. 536–660 CE) and the Justinianic Plague (541 CE onward) accelerated the collapse, though they came after the official 476 CE date. (5) Religious / cultural change — Edward Gibbon famously blamed Christianity for sapping the "civic spirit" of Rome; most modern historians regard this as overdrawn but agree that the relationship between the Church and the imperial state restructured what Roman identity meant.

The historiographical battle has been bitter. The older view — going back to Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire (1776–88) — emphasised catastrophe, decadence, and discontinuity. The mid-twentieth-century continuity school (associated with the Belgian medievalist Henri Pirenne) argued that Rome did not really "fall" in 476 CE but slowly transformed into a Mediterranean Christian civilisation that survived until Arab conquests cut the Mediterranean in two in the seventh century. Late twentieth-century "Late Antiquity" scholarship (Peter Brown, Averil Cameron, Garth Fowden) extended this further: there was no fall at all, only a long transformation in which Roman institutions, Christian theology, and Germanic kingship recombined into the early medieval world. Most recently, a "neo-Catastrophist" school (Bryan Ward-Perkins, Peter Heather) has pushed back, citing archaeological evidence of massive decline in living standards — fewer coins, smaller houses, simpler pottery — to insist that something terrible did happen, even if it took centuries.

The lesson for Grade 11 historical thinking is that periodisation — choosing where to draw the lines — is itself a historical argument. "The Fall of Rome" is not a date you memorise but a question you answer: what aspects of Roman civilisation ended when, for whom, and at what cost? The eastern empire continued. The western Church continued (it became the new Rome). Latin continued (as a learned language for 1500 years and as the parent of French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian). Roman law continued (in the law codes of every European state). Roman roads, aqueducts, and city plans continued (much of European urban geography was set by 200 CE). To say Rome "fell" in 476 CE is therefore to highlight one kind of change — the loss of unified central political authority — while obscuring everything that did not change.

  • Romulus Augustulus — last Western Roman emperor, deposed 476 CE.
  • Odoacer — Germanic warlord who deposed Romulus Augustulus.
  • Visigoths / Ostrogoths / Vandals / Huns / Franks — peoples involved in 4th–6th century migrations.
  • Adrianople (378 CE) — battle in which Goths killed Emperor Valens; psychological turning point.
  • Sack of Rome (410 CE) — Alaric's Visigoths sack Rome; first sack in 800 years.
  • Late Antique Little Ice Age — climatic cooling c. 536–660 CE.
  • Justinianic Plague — first pandemic of Yersinia pestis, c. 541–549 CE onward.
  • Henri Pirenne — Belgian historian; argued Rome ended with the Arab conquests, not the German migrations.
  • Late Antiquity — historiographic period c. 250–800 CE; transformation rather than catastrophe.

Worked Example — Periodising the End of Antiquity

Imagine you must answer the essay prompt: "When did the Roman Empire end?" A strong response refuses the single-date trap. Possible defensible answers and their criteria: (1) 476 CE — if the question is "political authority in the West." (2) 565 CE — if "the death of the last emperor who imagined ruling a re-united Mediterranean" (Justinian, who reconquered Italy and North Africa). (3) c. 650 CE — Pirenne's date, when Arab naval power closed the Mediterranean as a Roman lake. (4) 1453 CE — if "the city that was the Roman capital" (Constantinople falls to the Ottomans). (5) Never — if "the languages, laws, and institutions Rome left behind." Each answer is defensible; what matters is that the writer specifies the criterion of "ending" and applies it consistently. A Level 4 essay would name a criterion, defend it against alternatives, and acknowledge the historiographical conversation it joins. This is precisely the difference between memorisation and historical thinking — and precisely what Strand C is designed to develop.

  • Memorising 476 CE as the "fall" without questioning the criterion.
  • Calling the migrating peoples "barbarians" without quotation marks (the term is Roman invective).
  • Treating the fall as caused by a single factor (Christianity, lead poisoning, climate).
  • Forgetting that the Eastern Empire continued for nearly a thousand more years.
  • Equating "fall of Rome" with "end of antiquity" — they may or may not be the same event.
