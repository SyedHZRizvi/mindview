⛪ 3.1 The Byzantine Empire — Rome After Rome
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Eastern Roman Empire — what later historians, but never the people who lived in it, called the Byzantine Empire — survived the political collapse of the Western Roman Empire by nearly a thousand years. Its inhabitants called themselves Romans (Greek: Romaioi), spoke Greek as their everyday language after about 600 CE, and considered their state to be the unbroken Roman state of Augustus and Constantine. The convention of calling this empire "Byzantine" (after Byzantium, the old name of the city Constantine renamed in 330 CE) was a later European invention, partly designed to deny the eastern empire its Roman identity. Reading the period requires undoing that bias and recognising that for a Constantinopolitan in 1000 CE, "the empire" was Rome.

The Eastern Empire survived the western collapse for several structural reasons. Geographically, Constantinople sat on a defensible peninsula commanding the Bosphorus, with massive walls (the Theodosian Walls of 413 CE, repaired and extended over centuries) that no army would breach until the Ottoman cannon of 1453. Economically, the East was richer and more urbanised than the West — Egypt's grain, Syria's textiles, and the Indian Ocean trade route's spices passed through Constantinople. Politically, the empire had a more stable succession system, a professional civil service, and a unified Greek-speaking population. Militarily, the East invested in defensive depth rather than offensive expansion, and the doctrine of "themes" — military districts governed by generals who farmed land in exchange for service — produced a self-funding territorial defence.

The reign of Justinian I (r. 527–565 CE) is the high-water mark of the Eastern recovery. His generals Belisarius and Narses reconquered North Africa from the Vandals, Italy from the Ostrogoths, and southern Spain from the Visigoths. He codified Roman law in the Corpus Juris Civilis (the four-book systematisation that became the foundation of every civil-law system in modern Europe and Quebec). He built the Hagia Sophia, which on its completion in 537 CE was the largest enclosed space in the world and remained the largest Christian church until Seville Cathedral was completed in 1520. But Justinian's reign also brought the Justinianic Plague (541 CE onward), which killed perhaps 25–50% of the empire's population in successive waves over two centuries and undid most of his territorial gains.

The Byzantine state would face permanent challenges from the seventh century onwards. The rise of Islam (632 CE onwards) tore away Syria, Egypt, and North Africa within a generation. Bulgar, Slav, and later Turkic migrations reshaped the Balkan and Anatolian frontiers. The Iconoclast Controversy of the eighth and ninth centuries (over whether religious images were idolatrous) split the empire from the western Church and contributed to the Great Schism of 1054, which permanently divided Latin Catholic and Greek Orthodox Christianity. The catastrophe at the Battle of Manzikert (1071 CE) opened Anatolia to the Seljuk Turks; the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople (1204 CE) wounded the empire so badly that the recovered Palaiologos dynasty after 1261 never regained its strength. The final fall to Mehmed II's Ottoman army on 29 May 1453 was less a sudden catastrophe than the last act of a long siege.

  • Byzantium / Constantinople — original Greek colony, refounded by Constantine 330 CE.
  • Theodosian Walls — multi-ring defences of Constantinople, c. 413 CE.
  • Justinian I (r. 527–565 CE) — emperor of the recovery; lawgiver; builder of Hagia Sophia.
  • Corpus Juris Civilis — Justinian's codification of Roman law; foundation of civil-law tradition.
  • Hagia Sophia — Justinian's cathedral; later mosque; now museum/mosque again.
  • Themes — military provinces of the middle Byzantine empire (post-7th c.).
  • Iconoclasm — 8th–9th c. controversy over religious images.
  • Great Schism (1054) — formal break between Latin Catholic and Greek Orthodox Churches.
  • Manzikert (1071) — Byzantine defeat opening Anatolia to the Seljuks.
  • Fall of Constantinople (1453) — Ottoman conquest under Mehmed II.

Worked Example — Was Byzantium "Roman"?

The question is more than terminological. (1) Self-identification — Byzantines called themselves Romaioi; their state was Basileia Romaion (Empire of the Romans); their capital was the New Rome. (2) Institutional continuity — the Senate continued (though with diminished power); the legal system remained Roman; the imperial title (basileus) was inherited from the Roman augustus. (3) Religious tradition — the church traced apostolic foundation in Constantinople, Antioch, Alexandria, and Jerusalem, all Roman provinces. (4) Cultural shift — Greek replaced Latin as the working language after Heraclius (c. 620 CE); pagan Roman culture gave way to Orthodox Christian culture. (5) External denial — Charlemagne's coronation as emperor in 800 CE, the western "Holy Roman Empire" after 962 CE, and Renaissance humanism's identification of Rome with Latin antiquity all worked to disqualify the eastern empire from "Roman" status. A defensible answer: Byzantium was Roman in continuous self-identification, institution, and theology; it was not Roman in language or in everyday culture after c. 600 CE. Whether you accept it as "Roman" depends on which criteria you privilege — a textbook case of historical-thinking method.

  • Calling Byzantium "Greek" and contrasting it with "Rome" — its inhabitants called themselves Romans.
  • Treating 1453 as a sudden collapse — the empire had been declining for centuries.
  • Imagining the Crusades helped Byzantium — the Fourth Crusade (1204) was its near-fatal blow.
  • Confusing Justinian's recovery (565 CE) with permanent restoration — his gains were undone within decades.
  • Treating Orthodox and Catholic Christianity as different from the start — they did not formally split until 1054 CE.

🏰 3.2 Feudalism, Manorialism, & Medieval Society
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

"Feudalism" is one of the most heavily contested concepts in medieval history. The textbook version describes a tidy pyramid: a king grants land (a fief) to a noble (a vassal), who swears homage and fealty, owes military service when called, and in turn grants sub-fiefs to lesser knights. Below the noble pyramid lies the manor — a self-sufficient agricultural estate worked by peasants (most of them serfs bound to the land) who give the lord part of their harvest and labour in exchange for protection and the right to farm. The whole system is presented as the political order of medieval Europe between roughly 800 and 1200 CE. The pyramid is memorable and largely false — or at least, deeply incomplete.

Modern medievalists (above all Susan Reynolds in her 1994 Fiefs and Vassals) have shown that the tidy textbook diagram was constructed by sixteenth- and seventeenth-century French jurists from a small number of late documents and projected back across a thousand years of European history that was, in fact, enormously more varied. The realities were closer to a patchwork: in different parts of Europe at different times, lords held land outright (allodial property), in tenancies of unequal kinds, in church benefices, in mortgaged returns, in royal grants conditional on service, or in any combination. Vassalage existed but was not the universal organising principle of political life. The word "feudalism" itself was not used by medieval people; it was coined retrospectively. Most current historians use "feudalism" only with quotation marks and recommend more specific terms: vassalage, lordship, manorialism, military tenure.

"Manorialism" survives the historiographical critique better than "feudalism" because the economic unit it describes — the agricultural estate operated by peasant labour for a landlord — really did exist across most of medieval Europe. A typical manor of 11th-century England (we know about these from the Domesday Book of 1086) had a manorial house, a church, perhaps a mill, hundreds of acres of arable open fields divided into long strips, meadow and woodland, and a village of 20–60 peasant households. Open fields were farmed in a three-field rotation (winter wheat / spring oats and barley / fallow), an improvement on the older two-field rotation that increased yields by perhaps a third. The peasantry was stratified: free peasants paid rent but kept their personal status; villeins or serfs were bound to the manor, owed labour services on the lord's demesne, could not legally leave, and required the lord's permission to marry off the manor or to send a child into the priesthood.

Peasant life was hard but not lawless. Custom — what had been done in the village for generations — had real weight in the lord's court; the steward could not arbitrarily increase labour services beyond customary norms. Peasant households worked perhaps 200–250 days a year on average (the church calendar provided about 50 holidays); average life expectancy at birth was perhaps 30–35 years, dragged down heavily by infant mortality. Almost everyone was a farmer; perhaps 90% of medieval Europeans lived in villages of fewer than 300 people; perhaps 5% in towns of 5,000+ inhabitants. The slowness of this world — communication at the pace of a horse, trade limited by costly transport — is something modern readers struggle to imagine, and historians caution against romanticising "the medieval mind" as either uniformly devout or uniformly miserable. Like ours, it was both.

  • "Feudalism" — retrospective term for European lord-vassal relations; use with caution.
  • Fief / vassal / homage / fealty — land grant / receiver / loyalty oath / pledge of faithful service.
  • Manorialism — the agricultural economy of large estates worked by peasant labour.
  • Demesne — land of the manor reserved for the lord's direct use.
  • Open-field system — strip farming in shared fields, organised by village custom.
  • Three-field rotation — winter / spring / fallow cycle, c. 9th century onward.
  • Serf / villein — peasant bound to the land and owing labour to the lord.
  • Allodial property — land held outright, not as a fief from a superior lord.
  • Domesday Book (1086) — William the Conqueror's survey of English property; rare detailed source.

Worked Example — Reading a Domesday Entry

A characteristic entry from the Domesday Book (1086) reads (in modernised English): "In Witelei there are 5 hides. There is land for 8 ploughs. There is in the demesne one plough; and 5 villeins and 4 bordars with 4 ploughs. There are 6 acres of meadow and woodland 1 league long and 1 furlong wide. It was worth 40 shillings; now 60 shillings." A historical-thinking reading: (1) Source — a royal land survey ordered by William I in 1085, completed by 1086, recording tax-eligible property as a snapshot. (2) Decode — "hide" = unit of taxable land (c. 120 acres); "plough" = a measure of arable capacity; "villein" / "bordar" = unfree peasant of two grades; values in shillings = the manor's annual revenue. (3) Significance — the value rose from 40 to 60 shillings between the Norman conquest and 1086, suggesting either intensification (more ploughing) or harsher extraction. (4) Limits — Domesday recorded what the king could tax, not what peasants actually consumed; it under-reports the very rich and very poor. (5) Question — what changed in this village in the twenty years after the Norman conquest? Likely: more land brought under plough; a new lord extracting more rent; perhaps new mills (taxed in shillings). Domesday is exceptional because it lets us answer such questions with quantitative evidence rare elsewhere in medieval Europe.

  • Drawing the tidy "feudal pyramid" without noting that it is a 17th-century invention.
  • Imagining all medieval peasants were serfs — free peasants were a substantial minority.
  • Treating manorialism as universal — towns, marshland, mountain regions, and frontier zones often operated differently.
  • Confusing villeins (unfree but with customary rights) with slaves (without rights in person).
  • Romanticising chivalry as the everyday behaviour of knights — most was idealised after the fact.

✝️ 3.3 The Medieval Church & the Investiture Controversy
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The medieval Latin Church was the largest, longest-lasting, and most internally coherent institution in European history. From the fifth century to the Reformation, the Church regulated the rhythm of daily life (the church bell, the calendar of feast days), the structure of the lifecycle (baptism, marriage, last rites), the limits of legitimate violence (the Peace and Truce of God movements), the bounds of acceptable belief (heresy trials), the diffusion of literacy (monastic schools, parish priests), the channelling of charity (alms, hospitals), and the only mechanism most people knew for the salvation of their souls. To call the medieval Church powerful is therefore misleading; it would be more accurate to say that almost everything we would call "politics" or "society" or "culture" in medieval Europe ran through the Church.

Monasticism was the engine. The Rule of Saint Benedict (c. 530 CE), composed at Monte Cassino in central Italy, established a balanced daily routine of prayer, manual work, and study (ora et labora, "pray and work") that became the template for European monasticism. Benedictine monasteries copied texts, preserved much of what survives of classical literature, drained swamps, cleared forests, invented water-powered manufacturing, recorded annals, and educated future bishops. Reform movements — Cluny in the tenth century, the Cistercians in the twelfth — periodically purified monastic life when accumulation of property and influence corrupted it. Mendicant orders (the Franciscans and Dominicans, both founded around 1210–15) introduced a new model: friars who took vows of poverty, lived in towns, and preached to the laity, marking the Church's adaptation to the urbanising twelfth-thirteenth-century economy.

The high-medieval papacy — from Gregory VII (r. 1073–1085) through Innocent III (r. 1198–1216) to Boniface VIII (r. 1294–1303) — argued that the Church held a superior, universal jurisdiction that could and should discipline lay rulers. This came to a head in the Investiture Controversy (c. 1075–1122), in which Gregory VII forbade kings and emperors from "investing" bishops with the symbols of their office (ring and crozier), on the grounds that ecclesiastical authority must flow only from the Church. Emperor Henry IV resisted, was excommunicated, was forced to do public penance in the snow at Canossa in 1077, was excommunicated again, and the conflict festered until the Concordat of Worms in 1122 produced a compromise: kings could approve bishops' temporal possessions, but only the Church could grant spiritual authority. The Investiture Controversy permanently altered the relationship between religious and secular power in the Latin West, producing for the first time a clearly conceived "Church" as an institution structurally separate from "State."

The Church also developed canon law (the systematic law of the Church, largely consolidated by Gratian's Decretum of c. 1140), provided most universities (Paris, Oxford, Bologna) with their theological core, and increasingly insisted on doctrinal uniformity. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required annual confession and communion, defined transubstantiation, regulated marriage, mandated identifying clothing for Jews and Muslims in Christian lands, and prohibited Christians from charging interest (a prohibition partly responsible for the segregation of moneylending to Jewish communities, with terrible consequences when the protections failed). By the late medieval period, the gap between the Church's universal claims and its visible corruption — selling of indulgences, scandalous papal succession, the seven-decade Avignon Papacy and subsequent Western Schism (1378–1417) — set the conditions for the reform movements of the fifteenth century and the eventual Protestant Reformation of 1517.

  • Rule of Saint Benedict — c. 530 CE; foundational rule of Western monasticism.
  • Ora et labora — "pray and work"; Benedictine motto.
  • Cluny / Cîteaux — reform monastic orders (910 CE; 1098 CE).
  • Franciscans / Dominicans — mendicant orders, c. 1210–15.
  • Investiture Controversy — c. 1075–1122; conflict over appointment of bishops.
  • Canossa (1077) — Henry IV's public penance before Gregory VII.
  • Concordat of Worms (1122) — compromise settling the Investiture Controversy.
  • Canon law — the law of the Catholic Church, especially after Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140).
  • Fourth Lateran Council (1215) — Innocent III's reforming council; defined many later medieval norms.
  • Western Schism (1378–1417) — period of two and then three rival popes.

Worked Example — Reading the Concordat of Worms

The compromise text of 1122, in summary, said: (1) Bishops would henceforth be elected by the cathedral chapter (the clergy), not appointed directly by kings. (2) The newly elected bishop would receive his temporal possessions (lands, revenues) from the king or emperor through a touch of the royal sceptre. (3) The same bishop would then receive his spiritual authority (ring and crozier) from the metropolitan archbishop or pope. A historical-thinking reading: Significance — the Concordat conceded that ecclesiastical and secular authority were two different things, structurally separated; this idea — distinct "Church" and "State" — was a medieval innovation with vast modern legacy. Causation — the compromise resulted from a 47-year deadlock in which both pope and emperor exhausted political capital; neither "won." Limits — bishops continued in practice to be powerful political actors (English archbishops sat in Parliament; German prince-bishops ruled territories); the formal separation co-existed with practical entanglement. Consequence — the Investiture Controversy made possible the high-medieval claim of papal supremacy (Innocent III) and, in the long run, the modern secular state. A Level 4 essay would identify all four moves.

  • Treating the medieval Church as monolithically corrupt — it was reformed repeatedly from within.
  • Calling the Investiture Controversy a "minor" dispute — it restructured European political theory permanently.
  • Imagining the Reformation began in 1517 — it had medieval roots stretching back centuries.
  • Forgetting that "the Church" in medieval Europe meant the Latin Catholic Church; Orthodox, Coptic, Nestorian, and other Christian traditions existed elsewhere.
  • Treating excommunication as merely a religious sanction — it was a powerful political tool that could legitimise revolt.

⚔️ 3.4 The Crusades — Causes, Consequences, & Critiques
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Crusades are, perhaps along with the Mongol conquests, the medieval phenomenon most often distorted by popular memory. The traditional Western account — a series of holy wars to free the Christian Holy Land from Muslim oppression — is now widely recognised as a self-flattering simplification. So is the inverse modern account — that the Crusades were a unique European atrocity. Both pictures miss most of what was actually happening. The Crusades were a multi-front, two-century intermittent religious-military mobilisation that reshaped Mediterranean politics, accelerated economic exchange, and produced lasting traumatic memories on all sides — including the Eastern Orthodox Christians whose great city Constantinople was sacked by Western crusaders in 1204.

Pope Urban II's speech at Clermont in November 1095 — the trigger for the First Crusade — fused several concerns: a Byzantine plea for military help against the Seljuk Turks after the disaster at Manzikert (1071); the long-running Reconquista in Iberia (Christian kingdoms had been pushing Islamic emirates south for nearly four centuries); a desire to redirect endemic noble violence outward; the theological idea (much-developed in the eleventh century) that warfare on behalf of Christendom could remit sin. The response far exceeded expectations: perhaps 60,000 people set out, including the "People's Crusade" of peasants under Peter the Hermit. After three years of often brutal travel, the surviving force took Jerusalem on 15 July 1099, perpetrating a massacre of Jews and Muslims that contemporary Christian chroniclers themselves described as horrific. The Crusader States — the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the Principality of Antioch, the County of Tripoli, the County of Edessa — were established in the eastern Mediterranean and persisted, with shrinking borders, until the fall of Acre in 1291.

The numbered "Crusades" — eight major expeditions are conventionally listed, from 1096 to 1291, with the Children's Crusade of 1212 as a desperate footnote — are only the most visible part of the picture. Holy war was also fought in Iberia (Reconquista, completed 1492), against pagan Slavs (Northern Crusades, 12th–13th c.), against Cathar heretics in southern France (Albigensian Crusade, 1209–29), and even against Christian rivals of the papacy. The Fourth Crusade (1202–04) infamously diverted from its Egyptian destination to sack Christian Constantinople, devastating the Byzantine Empire and permanently embittering Orthodox-Catholic relations. The Crusader States imported the customs of "feudalism" wholesale into territories that had been Islamic for four centuries, and their tiny knightly elite ruled vastly larger local Christian Arab, Muslim, and Jewish populations.

What did the Crusades achieve? In military terms, almost nothing lasting: Christian rule over Jerusalem lasted 88 years (1099–1187 and a brief recovery 1229–44), and by 1291 the entire Eastern Mediterranean was again under Muslim rule. In economic terms, the Italian maritime republics — Venice, Genoa, Pisa — grew immensely wealthy supplying and shipping crusaders, and Mediterranean trade was permanently reorganised. In cultural terms, contact with Islamic learning accelerated the transmission of Aristotle, algebra, optics, and medicine into the Latin West. In religious terms, the Crusades reinforced antisemitism (Jews were attacked along the Rhine on the way east in 1096 in some of the worst medieval pogroms), normalised holy war as a category, and inscribed Jerusalem into Western Christian imagination as the central pilgrim destination it has remained. Modern Muslim historical memory remembers the Crusades selectively too — the term Salibiyyīn ("Crusaders") only became politically prominent in the late nineteenth century, when European colonial powers were once again pressing into Arab lands.

  • Urban II (r. 1088–99) — pope who called the First Crusade at Clermont, 1095.
  • Reconquista — centuries-long Christian conquest of Iberia, completed 1492.
  • Crusader States — Jerusalem, Antioch, Tripoli, Edessa; 1099–1291.
  • Saladin (Salah ad-Din) — Kurdish Muslim leader; recaptured Jerusalem 1187.
  • Fourth Crusade (1202–04) — sacked Constantinople; permanently damaged Byzantium.
  • Templars / Hospitallers / Teutonic Knights — military religious orders.
  • Albigensian Crusade (1209–29) — against Cathar heretics in southern France.
  • Acre (1291) — fall of the last Crusader stronghold in the Levant.
  • Indulgence — remission of penance / temporal punishment; offered for Crusade participation.

Worked Example — Multiple Causation in the First Crusade

Why did the First Crusade happen? A weak essay names one cause (Urban II's speech). A strong essay identifies the layered causation that historical-thinking demands. (1) Immediate trigger — Urban II's speech at Clermont in November 1095, framed as a response to a Byzantine appeal. (2) Underlying religious cause — eleventh-century theological development of holy war as sin-remitting; the rising emphasis on Jerusalem in popular piety. (3) Political cause — the papal claim of universal jurisdiction (the Gregorian Reform) needed a project worthy of universal scale; the Byzantine appeal offered one. (4) Social cause — large numbers of landless second sons of European knightly families had little to do at home except feud; an externally directed expedition relieved internal pressure. (5) Economic cause — Italian maritime cities had an interest in expanded Mediterranean commerce. (6) Geopolitical cause — the Seljuk advance into Anatolia after Manzikert (1071) had alarmed Constantinople sufficiently to make appeal to Rome thinkable for the first time since the schism of 1054. No single factor is sufficient; together they produce the explosive response of 60,000+ people leaving Europe within a year. The lesson — central to Strand A historical-inquiry skills — is that big historical events almost always require multi-causal explanations.

  • Treating the Crusades as a single event rather than a two-century mobilisation across multiple fronts.
  • Imagining Muslim and Christian sides were each politically unified — both contained warring factions throughout.
  • Calling the First Crusade "the Crusade" — most participants used the language of pilgrimage.
  • Forgetting the Crusader pogroms against Rhineland Jewish communities (1096) and the Crusades' contribution to medieval antisemitism.
  • Reading modern Western-Muslim relations directly back into the medieval period — both sides have re-edited the memory.

💀 3.5 The Black Death
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Black Death — the bubonic, septicaemic, and pneumonic plague of Yersinia pestis that swept Eurasia between 1346 and 1353 — was the deadliest pandemic in human history. Genomic and historical evidence now points to its origin somewhere in Central Asia (probably the Tian Shan mountains of modern Kyrgyzstan, where a major outbreak among nomadic populations is recorded in 1338–39). It then spread along the Silk Roads and Mongol overland routes to the Crimean port of Caffa in 1346, where Genoese ships brought it to Sicily, then to mainland Italy and the wider Mediterranean by 1347, then northwest through France, England, and Scandinavia, then back south by 1351. It killed perhaps 30–50% of the European population within five years. Egypt and the Levant lost similar fractions. North African and central Asian populations also collapsed. Total deaths are unknowable but a conservative estimate runs to 75–100 million people worldwide — perhaps a quarter of humanity. There has been no comparable mortality event since.

Medieval people did not know about bacteria. They explained the plague through several frameworks. (1) Theological — the disease was divine punishment for sin; processions of flagellants whipped themselves through cities. (2) Astrological — a particular conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars in 1345 had corrupted the air. (3) Miasma theory — bad smells carried disease through the air, which is partly why physicians wore beak-masks stuffed with herbs. (4) Conspiracy — Jewish people were accused of poisoning wells; horrifying pogroms followed across Iberia, France, and the Holy Roman Empire, particularly in 1348–49. The pope, Clement VI, issued papal bulls condemning the antisemitic attacks but could not stop them; the massacres devastated Ashkenazi Jewish communities and pushed survivors eastward into Poland, which permitted Jewish settlement and so became a centre of European Jewish life for the next six centuries — until the Holocaust.

Economically and socially the consequences were vast. Wages roughly doubled across surviving Europe within a generation as labour scarcity gave peasants and urban artisans enormous bargaining power. Serfdom in Western Europe collapsed — landlords who tried to reimpose customary services faced peasant revolts (the Jacquerie in France 1358, Wat Tyler's Rebellion in England 1381, multiple Florentine uprisings) and could not afford to enforce them anyway. Land was abundant relative to people for the first time in centuries; smaller poorer fields were abandoned and the survivors farmed more productive ones. Average diet improved; per-capita meat consumption rose. Eastern Europe took a different path: lords successfully imposed a "second serfdom" precisely because the labour shortage made them desperate, and Russian serfdom persisted until the abolition of 1861. The Black Death thus pushed Western and Eastern Europe onto diverging social-economic trajectories whose effects persisted into the modern period.

Culturally and intellectually the consequences were similarly profound. The Church's monopoly on consolation was strained: many survivors lost confidence in clerical efficacy when monasteries and parish clergy died in higher proportions than the general population (presumably because they tended to the dying). The arts turned darker — Petrarch's poems mourn lost friends; Boccaccio framed the Decameron (1353) as ten Florentine refugees telling stories outside the city; the danse macabre (the "dance of death") became a recurrent visual motif. Universities reopened their curricula to admit lower-born students; the labour shortage forced re-tooling of medical, legal, and administrative training. Some historians (David Herlihy in The Black Death and the Transformation of the West, 1997) have argued that the demographic catastrophe was the immediate precondition for the Renaissance — wealth concentrated in fewer hands, surviving households suddenly literate and ambitious, classical learning newly desirable as a system of explanation when divine providence seemed to have failed. The thesis is provocative but defensible; what is certain is that medieval Europe could not have continued unchanged after losing a third of its people.

  • Yersinia pestis — bacterial agent of plague; identified 1894 by Alexandre Yersin.
  • Bubonic / septicaemic / pneumonic plague — three forms of Y. pestis infection.
  • Caffa (1346) — Genoese port in Crimea where plague crossed to Italian ships.
  • Miasma theory — pre-germ-theory belief that "bad air" caused disease.
  • Flagellants — penitential groups who whipped themselves in public processions.
  • Pogrom — organised, often officially tolerated, violent attacks on Jewish communities.
  • Clement VI — pope (r. 1342–52) who condemned anti-Jewish pogroms during the plague.
  • Statute of Labourers (1351) — failed English law trying to freeze wages at pre-plague levels.
  • Second serfdom — re-imposition of serfdom in Eastern Europe, contrary to Western trend.
  • Decameron — Boccaccio's c. 1353 frame-narrative of plague refugees.

Worked Example — Long-Term Consequences of the Black Death

Quantify the long-run wage effect. English agricultural day-wages in shillings (a contemporary unit): c. 1340 (pre-plague), about 3 pence per day. By 1380, about 5 pence per day for the same agricultural task — roughly a 65% nominal-wage increase. Adjust for price inflation (about 30% rise in food prices over the same period), real wages still rose by perhaps 25–30%. Multiply by 10% population survival rate and the conclusion is striking: the surviving English peasantry was, in per-capita real terms, roughly 25–30% better off after the plague than before. This is the demographic substrate of the late-medieval social revolution: it is much harder to be a docile serf when wages have risen dramatically and the lord cannot find anyone else to plough. The 1351 Statute of Labourers, which tried to freeze wages at pre-plague levels under royal sanction, failed because no one would enforce it against neighbours and kin. Wat Tyler's Rebellion of 1381 — when peasants marched on London demanding the abolition of serfdom — was made possible by a generation of post-plague wage bargaining. The lesson — central to historical thinking — is that demographic shocks have material consequences far longer than the initial mortality.

  • Calling the Black Death a "European" pandemic — it killed comparable percentages in Egypt, the Levant, and Central Asia.
  • Confusing the Black Death (1346–53) with the Plague of Justinian (541 CE onward) or the Third Plague Pandemic (1855 onward) — three separate pandemics of the same pathogen.
  • Forgetting the antisemitic pogroms — they were among the worst medieval atrocities and reshaped Jewish demography for centuries.
  • Imagining peasants automatically benefited everywhere — Western Europe's gains contrasted with Eastern Europe's "second serfdom."
  • Treating the plague as a one-time event — it returned in waves every 10–20 years through the seventeenth century.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "analyse the impact of the Black Death on Eurasian societies, including economic, demographic, religious, and social consequences."

🎓 3.6 Universities, Gothic Cathedrals, & Late Medieval Culture
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The high medieval period (c. 1050–1300 CE) was, against the common stereotype of stagnation, one of the most intellectually creative eras in European history. Two of its enduring institutional inventions — the university and the Gothic cathedral — illustrate how religious institutions in this period generated learning and art at a scale and ambition not seen since classical antiquity. Both depended on three preconditions: a growing population, surplus agricultural output, and an urbanising economy that could support people whose primary work was neither farming nor fighting.

The university began as an informal gathering of scholars and students around individual teachers — usually at a cathedral school in a town that could feed and house them. Bologna (founded c. 1088 as a school of law), Paris (c. 1150, focused on theology), Oxford (c. 1167, founded by English scholars expelled from Paris), and Cambridge (c. 1209, by scholars fleeing Oxford after a townsmen-students riot) became the four foundational institutions of European higher learning. By 1500 there were perhaps 80 universities across Europe. They were chartered by popes or rulers as legal corporations (the Latin universitas means a guild or community) with the right to grant degrees recognised across Christendom. The curriculum was structured around the seven liberal arts — the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic) and the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music) — followed by advanced study in theology, law, or medicine. Teaching was conducted in Latin; students were nominally clerics (with all the legal protections that implied); women were excluded almost without exception.

Scholasticism — the dominant medieval method of philosophical-theological inquiry — emerged from this environment. Its central figure is Thomas Aquinas (1225–74), a Dominican friar who taught at Paris and Naples, and whose Summa Theologica attempted a comprehensive synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy (newly available in Latin translations from Arabic in the twelfth century) with Christian theology. Aquinas's method — posing a question, surveying authorities for and against, offering a determination, and answering objections — set the model for university disputation for centuries. Other major Scholastics included Anselm of Canterbury, Peter Abelard, Albertus Magnus, Bonaventure, Duns Scotus, and William of Ockham. Scholasticism is sometimes caricatured as fruitless ("how many angels can dance on the head of a pin?" — a question no medieval scholar actually asked). In reality the period produced sophisticated logic (Ockham's nominalism, supposition theory), early physics (the Oxford Calculators' work on uniform acceleration), and theology that integrated reason and faith more rigorously than any subsequent system.

Gothic architecture — the cathedrals of Saint-Denis (c. 1144), Chartres (c. 1194–1220), Notre-Dame de Paris (1163–1345), Reims (1211–1311), Amiens (1220–1288), Cologne (1248 onward) — was the visual counterpart to Scholastic theology. Where Romanesque churches were heavy, dim, and intimate, Gothic cathedrals were light, vertical, and cosmic. The technical innovations of the pointed arch, the ribbed vault, and especially the flying buttress allowed walls to be opened with massive stained-glass windows, flooding interiors with coloured light that was understood theologically as the diffusion of divine truth. The cathedrals were the largest and most expensive buildings of the medieval economy — Chartres cost the equivalent of about five times Chartres town's annual GDP and took 26 years to build. They were also collaborative achievements of bishops, kings, town corporations, masons' lodges, glaziers' guilds, and the faithful who funded windows and chapels. The contrast with the modern Western imagination of medieval Europe as "dark" or "primitive" could not be more complete. Gothic cathedrals are still standing, after eight centuries, as proof of the technical and aesthetic ambition of the high medieval world.

  • Universitas — Latin for "guild"; legal corporation of scholars and students.
  • Bologna / Paris / Oxford / Cambridge — the four founding European universities.
  • Trivium — grammar, rhetoric, dialectic; the first three liberal arts.
  • Quadrivium — arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music; the four mathematical arts.
  • Scholasticism — medieval academic philosophy/theology; dialectical method.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225–74) — Dominican philosopher; Summa Theologica; synthesised Aristotle with Christian theology.
  • William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347) — Franciscan logician; "Ockham's razor"; nominalism.
  • Romanesque / Gothic — earlier (heavy, round-arched) and later (light, pointed-arched) medieval architectural styles.
  • Flying buttress — exterior arch supporting tall thin walls; key Gothic innovation.
  • Stained glass — coloured glass windows; theology of light made architectural.

Worked Example — Reading a Gothic Cathedral

Notre-Dame de Paris was begun in 1163 under Bishop Maurice de Sully and was not substantially complete until c. 1345 — 182 years, or roughly six generations of masons. The west façade is divided into three vertical sections (left, centre, right) and three horizontal levels (portals, kings' gallery, rose window and towers), mirroring the Trinity twice over. The central rose window (c. 1225) is 9.6 m in diameter — a marvel of geometry and engineering — and its hundreds of stained-glass panels illustrate the Old Testament prophets, the apostles, and the labours of the months. A historical-thinking reading: Significance — the cathedral was simultaneously a working parish church, the seat of the bishop, the coronation site of French monarchs, a centre of pilgrimage, and a visible argument that Paris was a city of divine and royal authority. Causation — the construction was made possible by Paris's growing wealth as a centre of trade and university learning, by royal patronage of the Capetian dynasty, and by the institutional capacity of the Church to mobilise long-term labour and donations. Continuity / change — the cathedral has been continuously modified: medieval additions, Revolutionary desecration (1793), Viollet-le-Duc's nineteenth-century restoration, the 2019 fire and ongoing repair. The building tells the history of France in stone. Ethical dimension — much of its construction was funded by indulgences (gifts to the Church in exchange for remission of penance), which raises modern questions about the relationship between religious manipulation and artistic achievement. A Level 4 essay would address all four dimensions.

  • Treating "the Middle Ages" as intellectually dark — the high medieval period was one of the most creative in European history.
  • Imagining universities granted broadly accessible education — fewer than 1% of medieval Europeans attended.
  • Calling Gothic cathedrals "primitive" because they predate the Renaissance — they are technically more sophisticated than most Renaissance churches.
  • Repeating the discredited "angels-on-a-pin" parody of Scholasticism — no medieval text asks it.
  • Forgetting that medieval universities were exclusively male, exclusively Latin-using, and overwhelmingly clerical — they were exclusive in ways that shaped later European intellectual life.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "describe enduring cultural, intellectual, and technological legacies of medieval societies (e.g., universities, Gothic architecture, Islamic algebra, Chinese printing)."
