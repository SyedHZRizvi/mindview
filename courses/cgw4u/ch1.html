📊 1.1 Measuring Quality of Life — GDP, HDI, and Beyond
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Quality of life is a multidimensional concept that captures how well people are able to meet their needs and aspirations. Geographers and economists need ways to measure it so they can compare countries, regions, and communities. The simplest measure, Gross Domestic Product per capita, is the value of all goods and services produced in a country divided by its population. GDP per capita is useful but incomplete: it averages out enormous inequality, ignores unpaid work (much of it done by women), and is silent on environmental degradation or life expectancy. Qatar has a high GDP per capita but a low Press Freedom Index; Costa Rica has a moderate GDP per capita but a Happy Planet Index higher than the United States. Numbers tell different stories depending on what you choose to count.

The United Nations Development Programme introduced the Human Development Index (HDI) in 1990 as a composite measure that incorporates three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth), knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling), and a decent standard of living (gross national income per capita, PPP-adjusted). Each dimension is normalised on a 0–1 scale and combined using the geometric mean. HDI values place countries into four tiers — very high (≥ 0.800), high, medium, and low (< 0.550). Norway, Switzerland, and Iceland regularly top the index; Niger, South Sudan, and Chad sit near the bottom. Canada's HDI is approximately 0.936, placing it eighteenth globally as of the 2024 report.

Critics of the HDI note that it still misses inequality, gender gaps, environmental sustainability, and subjective well-being. To address these, the UNDP also publishes the Inequality-Adjusted HDI (IHDI), the Gender Inequality Index (GII), and the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI). The Happy Planet Index divides happy life-years by ecological footprint, rewarding countries that deliver well-being without overshooting planetary boundaries. No single number captures everything; geographers triangulate.

  • GDP per capita — total economic output divided by population.
  • Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) — adjustment that compares incomes using the cost of a standard basket of goods.
  • HDI — composite of life expectancy, education, and income (UNDP).
  • Composite indicator — single number aggregating several variables.
  • IHDI — HDI discounted for internal inequality.
  • Happy Planet Index — well-being per unit ecological footprint.

Worked example — Computing an HDI score

A country has life expectancy 75 years, expected schooling 14 years, mean schooling 8 years, and GNI per capita US$15 000 (PPP). Compute its HDI using the UNDP method, where each dimension index is normalised to 0–1.

Life-expectancy index: \(\frac{75 - 20}{85 - 20} = \frac{55}{65} \approx 0.846\). Expected-schooling index: \(\frac{14}{18} \approx 0.778\); mean-schooling index: \(\frac{8}{15} \approx 0.533\); education index = average ≈ 0.656. Income index: \(\frac{\ln(15000) - \ln(100)}{\ln(75000) - \ln(100)} = \frac{9.616 - 4.605}{11.225 - 4.605} \approx 0.757\). HDI = geometric mean = \(\sqrt[3]{0.846 \times 0.656 \times 0.757} \approx 0.748\) — a high-HDI country, slightly below the very-high cutoff of 0.800.

  • Treating GDP per capita as a measure of welfare — it ignores distribution and non-market goods.
  • Confusing nominal and PPP-adjusted GDP — PPP is the correct measure for cross-country comparisons of living standards.
  • Assuming HDI is "the truth" — it is one composite among several, with its own choices baked in.
  • Forgetting that the HDI uses the geometric mean, so weakness in any one dimension drags down the overall score.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "analyse the use of composite indicators (e.g., the Human Development Index, the Gender Inequality Index, the Happy Planet Index) to compare quality of life across countries."

📈 1.2 Inequality and the Gini Coefficient
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Two countries with the same average income can have very different societies depending on how that income is distributed. The most widely used measure of income inequality is the Gini coefficient, devised in 1912 by the Italian statistician Corrado Gini. The Gini coefficient is derived from the Lorenz curve, which plots the cumulative share of total income (vertical axis) against the cumulative share of the population, ranked from poorest to richest (horizontal axis). A society of perfect equality produces a 45-degree line; the further the actual curve sags below it, the more unequal the society. The Gini is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve, divided by the total area under the line of equality. It ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality, sometimes expressed as 0–100).

Countries cluster into recognisable patterns. Scandinavian countries report some of the lowest Ginis (Norway ~0.27, Denmark ~0.28), reflecting strong tax-and-transfer systems. The United States sits near 0.40, Canada around 0.31. The most unequal countries in 2024 include South Africa (~0.63) and Namibia (~0.59), with apartheid-era legacies still visible in the distribution of land, capital, and wages. Inequality matters geographically because it correlates with health outcomes (Wilkinson and Pickett's The Spirit Level, 2009), social trust, and political stability. Sharp inequality also concentrates pollution and disaster risk on lower-income communities — an environmental-justice issue documented in cities from Mumbai to Memphis.

Inequality is not just about income. Wealth inequality is typically much higher (Canadian wealth Gini ~0.72), because financial assets compound over time. Other dimensions include access to education, health care, broadband, and political voice. The Palma ratio (top 10% share divided by bottom 40% share) is an alternative measure that is especially sensitive to elite concentration.

  • Gini coefficient — single number summarising income inequality (0 = perfect equality, 1 = total inequality).
  • Lorenz curve — graphical representation of cumulative income distribution.
  • Palma ratio — top 10% income share ÷ bottom 40% income share.
  • Wealth vs income inequality — wealth is stock (assets owned), income is flow (annual earnings); wealth is usually more unequal.
  • Tax-and-transfer system — government policies that redistribute income through progressive taxation and social transfers.
  • Environmental justice — the geography of who bears environmental costs.

Worked example — Computing a simplified Gini coefficient

A village of 5 households earns, from poorest to richest: $10, $20, $30, $40, $100 (total $200). Compute the Lorenz-curve points and estimate the Gini.

Cumulative population shares: 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%. Cumulative income shares: \(\frac{10}{200} = 5\%\), \(\frac{30}{200} = 15\%\), \(\frac{60}{200} = 30\%\), \(\frac{100}{200} = 50\%\), 100%. Using the trapezoidal approximation, the Gini ≈ \(1 - \sum (X_{i+1} - X_i)(Y_{i+1} + Y_i) = 1 - [0.2(0+0.05) + 0.2(0.05+0.15) + 0.2(0.15+0.30) + 0.2(0.30+0.50) + 0.2(0.50+1.00)] = 1 - [0.01 + 0.04 + 0.09 + 0.16 + 0.30] = 1 - 0.60 = 0.40\). The village has a Gini of about 0.40 — comparable to the United States.

  • Saying "Gini = 1 means one person has all the money" — strictly, that limit is approached but never reached in real populations.
  • Confusing pre-tax-and-transfer Gini (market) with post-tax-and-transfer (disposable) — Canada's redistribution drops the Gini from about 0.43 to 0.31.
  • Mixing up income inequality with poverty — a country can have moderate Gini but high absolute poverty if average income is low.
  • Reading the Lorenz curve backwards (the cumulative axis must run from poorest to richest).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "analyse interrelationships between poverty, inequality, and other quality-of-life factors in different parts of the world."

👥 1.3 The Demographic Transition Model
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) describes a country's typical journey through changes in fertility and mortality as it industrialises. Built from European data by Warren Thompson (1929) and refined since, the model has four classical stages and a contested fifth. Stage 1 (pre-industrial) has high birth rates and high death rates that approximately cancel; populations grow slowly. Stage 2 begins when public-health gains (clean water, vaccines, food security) crash the death rate while birth rates remain high — population explodes. Stage 3 sees birth rates fall as urbanisation, female education, and contraception spread; population growth slows. Stage 4 reaches a new equilibrium with low birth rates and low death rates. Stage 5, proposed for Japan, Italy, and South Korea, has birth rates that fall below death rates, producing aging populations and outright decline.

The DTM is descriptive rather than predictive. Many sub-Saharan African countries are still in late Stage 2 or early Stage 3 — Niger's total fertility rate (TFR) is about 6.6 children per woman, compared with 1.8 in the United States and 1.3 in Italy. The replacement-level fertility rate is roughly 2.1. The DTM is best read alongside a population pyramid, a back-to-back bar chart of age cohorts. A wide-based pyramid (Niger) signals rapid growth and a young population. A column-shaped pyramid (Germany) signals stability and rising age. An inverted pyramid (Italy projected for 2050) signals decline. Canada's pyramid shows the bulge of baby boomers moving toward retirement.

The DTM has limits. It was built on European experience and assumes a particular sequence; some countries have not followed it. It also says little about migration, which is now the dominant driver of population change in many high-income countries, including Canada.

  • Crude Birth Rate (CBR) — births per 1000 people per year.
  • Crude Death Rate (CDR) — deaths per 1000 people per year.
  • Total Fertility Rate (TFR) — average lifetime children per woman.
  • Replacement-level fertility — TFR of ~2.1 needed for a stable population (without migration).
  • Natural increase rate — CBR − CDR, expressed per 1000.
  • Population pyramid — graph of population by age and sex.

Worked example — Stage classification

Country X has CBR = 32, CDR = 9, TFR = 4.2, infant mortality = 38 per 1000, and a youth-heavy pyramid. Country Y has CBR = 9, CDR = 11, TFR = 1.3, infant mortality = 3 per 1000, and an inverted pyramid. Classify each by DTM stage and predict population trend.

Country X has high CBR and falling but still moderate CDR — late Stage 2 or early Stage 3 — and a high natural increase of \(\frac{32 - 9}{1000} = 2.3\%\) per year. Doubling time \(\approx 70 / 2.3 \approx 30\) years. Country Y has CDR > CBR, well below replacement TFR, and an inverted pyramid — Stage 5, with shrinking and aging population unless migration offsets it. Natural increase = \(-0.2\%\); population will decline by roughly one-fifth in 50 years absent migration.

  • Calling the model predictive — it summarises European history and is not destiny for other countries.
  • Confusing fertility rate (per woman) with crude birth rate (per 1000 of total population).
  • Forgetting migration — high-income countries grow through migration even with TFR below 2.1.
  • Reading a population pyramid wrong: females are always plotted on one side (conventionally the right), males on the other.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "explain the demographic transition model and its application to countries at different levels of development."

⚖️ 1.4 Gender, Education, and Well-Being
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Gender equity is one of the strongest predictors of overall human development. Countries where women have similar access to education, health care, paid work, and political representation tend to have higher HDI scores, lower fertility, lower child mortality, and higher per-capita incomes. The UNDP's Gender Inequality Index (GII) captures three dimensions: reproductive health (maternal mortality, adolescent birth rate), empowerment (parliamentary seats held by women, secondary education by sex), and labour-market participation. As of 2024 the Nordic states report some of the lowest GII values (Norway 0.012, Switzerland 0.018), while Yemen, Afghanistan, and Papua New Guinea sit at the other extreme.

Education is the lever that moves these numbers. Each additional year of girls' schooling is associated with later marriage, fewer children, higher household income, and lower infant mortality. The economist Esther Duflo (2019 Nobel Prize) and others have shown that investments in girls' education produce some of the highest returns of any development intervention. Globally, girls' primary enrolment has nearly caught up with boys', but gaps persist at secondary and tertiary levels in many low-income countries, often because of early marriage, gender-based violence, distance to school, and menstrual hygiene barriers. Indigenous communities in Canada also face systemic gaps in graduation rates, especially in remote First Nations.

Beyond education, well-being depends on access to clean water and sanitation, vaccines, basic nutrition, and electricity. The UN's Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) rolls these into a single measure that complements income poverty. About 1.1 billion people live in multidimensional poverty as of 2024, more than half of them children. Geographic patterns are stark: about 84% of MPI-poor people live in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

  • Gender Inequality Index (GII) — UNDP composite measure of reproductive health, empowerment, and labour participation.
  • Maternal mortality ratio — deaths per 100 000 live births.
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) — captures non-monetary deprivations in health, education, and living standards.
  • Gender parity index (GPI) — female / male ratio in education enrolment.
  • Empowerment — capacity to make strategic life choices.
  • Girls' education multiplier — the cascading benefits of educating girls.

Worked example — Comparing GII components

Country A has maternal mortality 350, adolescent birth rate 80, female parliamentary share 18%, female secondary attainment 32%, female labour-force participation 45%. Country B has maternal mortality 9, adolescent birth rate 11, female parliamentary share 45%, female secondary attainment 88%, female labour-force participation 76%. Which has higher GII and why?

Country A has higher GII (worse gender equity). All three dimensions are worse: reproductive health (much higher maternal mortality and adolescent births), empowerment (lower parliamentary share and lower female secondary attainment), and labour participation. Country B's pattern is typical of Nordic states. Improving Country A's outcomes would require multi-sector intervention: maternal-health services, school retention for girls, and political quotas.

  • Treating gender equity as a "women's issue" — it strongly predicts overall national development.
  • Equating school enrolment with educational attainment — many girls enrol but do not complete secondary.
  • Ignoring intersectionality — rural Indigenous girls in low-income countries face compounded disadvantages.
  • Reporting income poverty alone while ignoring non-monetary deprivations captured by the MPI.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "analyse the contribution of gender equity, access to education, and access to health care to improvements in quality of life globally."

🏙️ 1.5 Urbanization, Cities, and Mega-Cities
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

For the first time in human history, more than half of all people live in cities — the threshold was crossed around 2008. The United Nations projects the urban share will reach 68% by 2050. Urbanization is the spatial shift of people from rural areas into cities; it is driven by rural push factors (declining agricultural employment, land scarcity, climate stress) and urban pull factors (jobs, services, education, marriage markets, freedom). The pace varies: Europe and North America are already 75–85% urban, while sub-Saharan Africa is currently around 43% but urbanising rapidly. A mega-city is conventionally defined as having 10 million or more inhabitants. Tokyo (~37 million in its metropolitan area), Delhi, Shanghai, and São Paulo are the largest; Lagos, Karachi, and Cairo are growing fastest.

Cities concentrate economic productivity, cultural exchange, and political power, but they also concentrate inequality. Roughly one-third of the world's urban population lives in informal settlements — Rio's favelas, Mumbai's Dharavi, Nairobi's Kibera — where land tenure, water, sanitation, and electricity access are precarious. Primate cities (Bangkok in Thailand, Mexico City in Mexico) dominate their national systems, drawing disproportionate investment and migration. By contrast, balanced urban hierarchies (Germany's polycentric system) distribute population and services more evenly. Canada is a relatively urban country (~82%), with the Greater Toronto, Montreal, and Vancouver areas concentrating the bulk of population growth and immigrant settlement.

Urban geographers also study suburbanisation, gentrification, and the urban heat-island effect, where built surfaces and reduced vegetation raise city temperatures 3–7 °C above surrounding countryside, increasing heat-related mortality during heatwaves (Vancouver 2021 heat dome: ~619 deaths). Climate-adaptive urban design — green roofs, light-coloured pavement, urban forests — is now a frontier of planning practice.

  • Urbanization — increase in the proportion of population living in urban areas.
  • Mega-city — urban area with population ≥ 10 million.
  • Primate city — disproportionately large city dominating its national urban system.
  • Informal settlement — unplanned, often unauthorised neighbourhood lacking secure tenure.
  • Suburbanization — outward expansion of low-density development from a city core.
  • Urban heat-island effect — elevated temperatures in urbanized areas.

Worked example — Annual urban-growth rate

A city had 5.0 million people in 2010 and 8.4 million in 2020. Calculate the average annual percentage growth rate.

Using the exponential growth model \(N_t = N_0 e^{rt}\): \(r = \frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{N_t}{N_0}\right) = \frac{1}{10}\ln(1.68) = \frac{1}{10}(0.519) \approx 0.0519\), i.e. about 5.2% per year. This is consistent with the world's fastest-growing urban areas (Kinshasa, Dhaka, Lagos). At that rate the city would double again in roughly \(\frac{70}{5.2} \approx 13\) years.

  • Equating "urban" with "wealthy" — many urban dwellers live in informal settlements.
  • Confusing urban population with city size — fast-growing cities can be small in absolute population.
  • Treating informal settlements as a problem to be cleared — many provide affordable shelter and economic dynamism.
  • Ignoring the climate vulnerability of cities, especially heat and flood risk.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.3 — "explain geographic factors that influence settlement patterns, including the location and growth of major urban areas globally."

🎯 1.6 The Sustainable Development Goals
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), adopted by all 193 UN member states in September 2015, are a 17-goal global plan for sustainable, equitable, and peaceful development to 2030. They succeeded the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000–2015) and are broader, applying to all countries (high- and low-income alike), with 169 targets and over 230 indicators. The goals cluster into three intertwined dimensions: people (Goals 1–5: poverty, hunger, health, education, gender), planet (Goals 6–7, 13–15: water, energy, climate action, life below water, life on land), and prosperity, peace and partnerships (Goals 8–12, 16–17). SDG 17, "partnerships for the goals", emphasises finance, technology transfer, and capacity-building between rich and poor countries.

Progress is mixed. According to the UN Sustainable Development Report 2024, no country is fully on track to meet all 17 goals by 2030. Nordic countries lead overall but lag on SDG 12 (responsible consumption) because their ecological footprints are large. Low-income countries score well on SDG 12 but lag on SDG 1 (poverty), SDG 3 (health), and SDG 4 (education). Climate shocks, COVID-19, and conflict have set back many goals; the world has missed the 2030 trajectory on roughly half of the targets. Still, several indicators have improved markedly since 1990: extreme poverty has fallen from about 36% to under 9% of world population, under-five mortality has more than halved, and global access to electricity has reached ~91%.

Canada's 2030 Agenda National Strategy outlines national targets for each SDG. Notable Canadian challenges include the Indigenous-community well-being gap (SDG 10), housing affordability (SDG 11), and progress on emissions reduction (SDG 13). Geographers use SDG indicators as a structured framework for analysing world issues and for evaluating the impact of national and local policy.

  • Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) — 17-goal UN agenda adopted in 2015 for the period 2015–2030.
  • Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) — predecessor 8-goal framework (2000–2015).
  • Indicator — measurable proxy for a target (e.g., maternal mortality ratio for SDG 3).
  • Universal applicability — the principle that the SDGs apply to all countries, not just developing ones.
  • Leave no one behind — guiding principle prioritising the most marginalised groups.
  • Voluntary National Review (VNR) — country self-report on SDG progress to the UN High-Level Political Forum.

Worked example — Linking goals

A small low-income country has high maternal mortality, low female secondary completion, low access to electricity, and degraded forests. Which SDGs are most relevant, and how do they reinforce one another?

Most relevant: SDG 3 (health), SDG 4 (education), SDG 5 (gender equality), SDG 7 (clean energy), SDG 15 (life on land). The reinforcing logic: rural electrification (SDG 7) allows clinics to refrigerate vaccines and run lights at night for school study, improving SDG 3 and SDG 4. Educating girls reduces fertility and maternal mortality (SDG 5 → SDG 3). Sustainable forestry (SDG 15) protects soil and water quality, supporting agriculture and reducing rural poverty (SDG 1). A government should sequence policies to exploit these positive feedbacks, not treat each goal in isolation.

  • Treating SDGs as a wish list rather than a measurable framework with indicators and targets.
  • Forgetting that the SDGs are universal — high-income countries are not "done".
  • Ignoring trade-offs between goals (e.g., aggressive growth in SDG 8 can worsen SDG 13).
  • Underestimating the role of SDG 17 (partnerships) — most progress requires finance, technology, and trade reform.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "explain the contribution of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and related international agreements to improving quality of life globally."
