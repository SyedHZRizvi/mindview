🔬 5.1 The Geographic Inquiry Process and Concepts of Geographic Thinking
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Ontario curriculum for Canadian and World Studies defines a five-stage geographic inquiry process that mirrors the broader social-sciences research cycle: (1) formulate questions, (2) gather and organise information, (3) interpret and analyse, (4) evaluate and draw conclusions, and (5) communicate results. Inquiry begins by transforming curiosity into focused questions that can actually be answered — open-ended enough to invite real investigation, specific enough to be feasible within available time and data. "Why is the world unequal?" is a starting prompt; "How has the income gap between the richest and poorest 10% of Brazilians changed since 2000, and what policies have shaped this change?" is a researchable question.

Strong geographic questions are anchored in the four concepts of geographic thinking set out in the 2015 Ministry curriculum: spatial significance (why a place or pattern matters), patterns and trends (recurring forms across space and time), interrelationships (links between human and physical phenomena), and geographic perspective (looking through environmental, economic, political, social, and cultural lenses). A culminating-task question on Arctic sea-ice loss could probe spatial significance (which communities are affected), patterns and trends (the post-1979 satellite record), interrelationships (ice–albedo feedback, shipping-route opening, sovereignty), and perspective (Indigenous, environmental, economic, geopolitical lenses).

The inquiry cycle is iterative, not linear. Findings during analysis usually prompt new questions and additional data gathering. Effective inquiry plans also build in positionality reflection: who is asking the question, from what vantage point, and with whose voices represented? Strong inquiries are also transparent: methods, sources, and limitations are stated so others can replicate or extend the work.

  • Inquiry question — focused, researchable question driving an investigation.
  • Spatial significance — why a place, pattern, or process matters.
  • Patterns and trends — recurring spatial forms and temporal changes.
  • Interrelationships — linkages among physical and human systems.
  • Geographic perspective — interpretive lens drawn from disciplines and stakeholder positions.
  • Positionality — researcher's social and geographic vantage point.

Worked example — Refining a question

A student begins with the topic "climate change in Canada." Refine the topic into a researchable geographic inquiry question and identify which concepts of geographic thinking it engages.

Topic: "Climate change in Canada." Refined question: "How are climate-driven changes in the boreal forest fire regime affecting Indigenous communities in northern Ontario, and what adaptive policies have communities and governments enacted since 2015?" Engages all four concepts: spatial significance (northern Ontario boreal zone, Indigenous community locations), patterns and trends (fire frequency, intensity, and area-burned trends), interrelationships (climate – ecosystem – community-health – policy linkages), and geographic perspective (Indigenous knowledge, environmental science, public-health, governance lenses).

  • Starting with a topic instead of a question — leads to descriptive rather than analytical work.
  • Confusing inquiry with a literature review — inquiry requires interpreting data, not just summarising sources.
  • Skipping the question-refinement step — most inquiry failure starts with poor questions.
  • Treating positionality as an add-on — it shapes every stage of inquiry.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "formulate questions to guide investigations into world issues from a geographic perspective."

📚 5.2 Sources, Data, and Evaluating Evidence
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Geographers draw on a wide range of sources. Primary sources are first-hand: field observations, interviews, satellite imagery, original photographs, and original statistical datasets like census microdata. Secondary sources interpret or analyse primary materials: peer-reviewed journal articles, government reports, NGO briefs, textbooks, and quality journalism. The geographer's task is to evaluate each source's credibility, accuracy, bias or perspective, relevance, and currency. The CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) is a useful checklist. Authoritative geographic and economic data come from the United Nations, the World Bank, the OECD, Statistics Canada, the IPCC, and major peer-reviewed journals (Nature, Science, Nature Climate Change, the Annals of the American Association of Geographers, the Canadian Geographer).

Data come in several types. Quantitative data (counts, rates, ratios) support statistical and cartographic analysis. Qualitative data (interviews, oral histories, field notes, narrative accounts) capture meaning, context, and lived experience. Mixed-methods work combines the two for richer inference. Common geographic data sources include census tables (Statistics Canada, USCB, Eurostat), remote-sensing archives (Landsat, Sentinel, MODIS), open data portals (Open Government Canada, City of Toronto Open Data), and survey microdata (CHASS, IPUMS). For Indigenous-related research in Canada, the OCAP principles (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession) developed by First Nations data sovereignty advocates set ethical standards on community consent and data governance.

Evaluating evidence requires intellectual honesty. Treat a single dramatic chart with caution; check whether independent sources tell the same story. Look for cherry-picked time windows (a temperature plot starting in 1998 obscures the long-term trend). Read methodology sections. Distinguish correlation from causation: countries with more storks and more babies share a third variable (population scale), not a causal stork-baby link. Recognise publication bias (significant results are easier to publish than null results) and confirmation bias (we notice evidence that fits our prior beliefs).

  • Primary vs secondary source — first-hand vs interpretive material.
  • CRAAP test — Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
  • Quantitative vs qualitative data — numeric vs interpretive evidence.
  • OCAP principles — Indigenous data sovereignty framework: Ownership, Control, Access, Possession.
  • Triangulation — corroborating findings across multiple sources or methods.
  • Confirmation bias — tendency to favour evidence consistent with prior beliefs.

Worked example — Evaluating two sources

Source A: a 2023 peer-reviewed article in Nature Climate Change reporting Arctic sea-ice extent trends since 1979 based on satellite observations. Source B: a 2018 blog post by an oil-industry lobby group claiming Arctic ice "is recovering." Compare the credibility, accuracy, bias, and currency of the two sources.

Source A: high credibility (peer review, recognised journal); high accuracy (satellite-based measurement with standardised methodology); identifiable perspective (climate-science research community, generally transparent about methods and uncertainties); currency strong (2023). Source B: low credibility (no peer review); accuracy unverified and inconsistent with NSIDC data; explicit perspective tied to financial interest (lobbying); currency adequate but conclusion not supported by subsequent data. Verdict: rely on Source A; treat Source B as evidence of views rather than of Arctic sea ice itself.

  • Treating Wikipedia citations as final evidence — use them as a launch point, not an end point.
  • Equating "first on Google" with "most authoritative."
  • Confusing perspective (legitimate analytical stance) with bias (distortion of evidence).
  • Skipping methods sections — most analytical mistakes are upstream of the headline.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — "evaluate evidence and information and draw conclusions about world issues."

🛰️ 5.3 GIS, Remote Sensing, and Geospatial Technologies
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is software that captures, stores, manipulates, analyses, and displays spatially referenced data. The conceptual building block is the layer — each layer represents one theme (rivers, soils, roads, population density) for a defined area, georeferenced to a common coordinate system. Layers can be vector (points, lines, polygons — best for discrete features like cities or boundaries) or raster (grid of cells — best for continuous phenomena like elevation or temperature). Spatial analysis combines layers: overlay (find areas where wetlands meet farmland), buffering (zones around features), network analysis (shortest routes), and spatial statistics (cluster detection, hot-spot analysis). Common GIS platforms include the open-source QGIS and the commercial ArcGIS Pro; web mapping platforms include Google Earth, Esri's ArcGIS Online, and Mapbox.

Remote sensing is the acquisition of information about Earth's surface from sensors mounted on satellites, aircraft, or drones. Passive sensors record reflected sunlight or emitted thermal energy in specific wavelength bands. The Landsat programme, jointly operated by NASA and USGS since 1972, is the longest continuous Earth-observation record. The European Space Agency's Sentinel-2 satellites provide 10-metre multispectral imagery globally every 5 days. Active sensors emit their own signal: radar (SAR) penetrates clouds and works at night; lidar provides centimetre-scale 3-D models. Drones and UAVs allow flexible, high-resolution local mapping.

GIS and remote sensing combine powerfully for world-issue analysis. The IPCC uses satellite-derived datasets for climate analysis; Global Forest Watch tracks tropical deforestation in near-real time; the UN World Food Programme uses NDVI vegetation indices to anticipate drought-driven food crises; humanitarian agencies map damage after earthquakes within hours. The ethical issues are significant: high-resolution imagery and movement data can be re-identified, enabling surveillance; algorithmic mapping can encode and amplify bias if input data are uneven across communities.

  • GIS — Geographic Information System: software for spatial data analysis.
  • Vector vs raster data — discrete features vs grid-based fields.
  • Georeferencing — assigning real-world coordinates to spatial data.
  • Remote sensing — measuring Earth from satellite, aerial, or drone platforms.
  • Spectral band — defined range of electromagnetic wavelengths (e.g., near-infrared).
  • NDVI — Normalised Difference Vegetation Index, a measure of plant greenness.

Worked example — Computing NDVI

NDVI is calculated from satellite reflectance values as \(NDVI = \frac{NIR - RED}{NIR + RED}\). Healthy vegetation strongly reflects near-infrared (NIR) and absorbs red. A pixel records NIR reflectance = 0.45 and RED reflectance = 0.05; another records NIR = 0.10 and RED = 0.15. Compute NDVI and interpret each value.

Pixel 1: \(NDVI = \frac{0.45 - 0.05}{0.45 + 0.05} = \frac{0.40}{0.50} = 0.80\) — dense, healthy vegetation. Pixel 2: \(NDVI = \frac{0.10 - 0.15}{0.10 + 0.15} = \frac{-0.05}{0.25} = -0.20\) — likely water or built-up surface (NDVI ranges from −1 to +1; values below 0 indicate non-vegetation). Time-series NDVI is used to track drought, deforestation, and crop health.

  • Confusing GIS (analysis software) with GPS (positioning system).
  • Ignoring map projection — distance and area calculations require an appropriate projection.
  • Trusting black-box web maps without checking underlying data sources.
  • Forgetting ethics — geospatial data can reveal sensitive locations.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.1 — "apply geospatial technologies (GIS, remote sensing, online mapping platforms) to analyse world issues."

🗺️ 5.4 Cartographic Communication and Thematic Mapping
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Maps are arguments expressed in space. Every cartographic choice — projection, scale, classification, symbology, colour — shapes the reader's understanding. The cartographer Mark Monmonier's classic How to Lie with Maps (1991, updated) underscores how easy it is to mislead even with accurate data. Effective thematic maps obey conventions to keep meaning honest. The title states the variable, the place, and the time. The legend defines symbols and classes. Scale can be expressed numerically (1:50 000) or graphically (a scale bar). A north arrow orients the reader. Data sources and projection are credited.

Several map types suit different data. A choropleth map shades enumeration units (countries, provinces, census tracts) by a quantitative value — best for rates, ratios, and densities, never for raw counts (which conflate area with magnitude). A proportional symbol map uses circle area or other symbols proportional to a value — best for raw counts of point phenomena. A dot density map distributes dots representing a fixed quantity each. Isoline maps (contours) trace lines of equal value, ideal for continuous fields like elevation or temperature. Cartograms distort area to represent a variable (e.g., world map with each country sized by population). Flow maps show movement (migration, trade).

Classification choices matter. Equal intervals divide the data range into equal-width classes — easy but can be uneven if data are skewed. Quantiles place an equal number of observations in each class — even visual emphasis but can mask outliers. Natural breaks (Jenks) minimise within-class variance — usually best for choropleth maps with skewed data. The number of classes (typically 4–7) trades simplicity against detail. Colour choice should match data type: sequential (light to dark) for ordered data, diverging (two-hue with neutral midpoint) for above-and-below-a-reference data, qualitative (different hues) for categorical data. Tools like ColorBrewer help select accessible palettes.

  • Choropleth map — areal units shaded by rate or ratio.
  • Proportional symbol map — symbols sized by magnitude.
  • Cartogram — area distorted to represent a variable.
  • Classification scheme — equal intervals, quantiles, natural breaks, standard deviation.
  • Map projection — mathematical transformation from sphere to plane (Mercator, Robinson, Goode, etc.).
  • Generalization — simplification appropriate to scale.

Worked example — Choosing a map type

You have data on (a) total population by province, (b) population density by province, (c) Canada–U.S. migration flows by province, and (d) global GDP per capita. Choose the most appropriate map type for each.

(a) Total population by province — proportional symbol, because populations are raw counts and provinces vary widely in area; a choropleth would conflate Ontario's large area with high values. (b) Population density — choropleth, because density is a rate already adjusted for area. (c) Migration flows — flow map, with arrows of varying width connecting origin to destination. (d) Global GDP per capita — choropleth, with a sequential or diverging palette (relative to world median) and a Robinson or Equal Earth projection to avoid the Mercator's exaggeration of high latitudes.

  • Mapping raw counts with choropleth shading — penalises areal units with larger area.
  • Using Mercator projection for global thematic maps — distorts Greenland and exaggerates high latitudes.
  • Choosing colour by aesthetics rather than data structure — diverging palettes for ordered data is misleading.
  • Forgetting the legend, scale, north arrow, source, and date — without them the map is decorative, not analytical.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.5 — "communicate judgements, decisions, and conclusions using cartographic, graphic, written, and digital forms."

🎓 5.5 The Culminating Inquiry — Putting It All Together
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The CGW4U culminating inquiry is an independent geographic investigation of a current world issue, integrating the concepts and skills developed across Strands A–E. The product can take various forms — a research paper (~2500 words), a policy brief, an infographic, a documentary short, an interactive map, a podcast, or an oral defence — but the underlying inquiry must address all five stages of the geographic-inquiry process and engage at least two of the four concepts of geographic thinking. A robust culminating task ties to the SDGs, the Ontario Ministry's Issue-Based Inquiry framework, and a clearly articulated argument.

Choose a focused, manageable, and substantively significant issue. Strong candidates include: critical-minerals supply chains and Indigenous consent in Canada; climate-induced displacement in Bangladesh; the role of AI in election misinformation; ocean acidification and Pacific fisheries; water rights along the Colorado River; the future of the Arctic shipping lanes; the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM); the geography of vaccine equity post-COVID. Spend at least a quarter of total time on the question itself; weak questions cannot be rescued by good writing.

Plan data and sources at the outset: identify at least one quantitative dataset, at least one cartographic representation you will produce, and at least three credible secondary sources from peer-reviewed or authoritative origins. Document methods transparently. Map your argument with a clear thesis, three to five evidence-based claims, a counter-argument acknowledged and rebutted, and a conclusion that returns to spatial significance ("why this matters, where, for whom"). Communicate visually with at least one original map or chart. Submit a reference list in APA or Chicago format. The strongest inquiries do not stop at description — they make a defensible argument grounded in evidence and an awareness of the perspectives at stake.

  • Culminating task — capstone independent inquiry, typically worth a significant share of the final 30%.
  • Thesis — central claim the inquiry argues and supports.
  • Methodology — explicit account of data sources and analytical steps.
  • Counter-argument — strongest alternative view, acknowledged and addressed.
  • Citation style — APA or Chicago author-date, consistently applied.
  • Academic integrity — accurate attribution, original work, declared use of generative AI tools.

Worked example — Inquiry plan

Sketch a culminating-inquiry plan for the question: "How should Canada balance critical-minerals development with Indigenous consent and environmental protection?"

Question type: policy / multi-perspective. Data plan: Natural Resources Canada production data; map of critical-mineral deposits overlaid with First Nations treaty territories; UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) text; case studies of the Ring of Fire, BHP Jansen, and Vital Metals projects. Concepts engaged: spatial significance, interrelationships, geographic perspective. Map deliverable: a choropleth of provincial mineral potential plus point locations of active projects, with Indigenous territorial overlays. Argument: Canada needs a federal Indigenous Consent Protocol with binding mediation, transparent royalty-sharing, and project-finance conditions tied to environmental performance. Counter-argument: economic urgency and global competition; rebuttal: projects without Indigenous consent face legal injunctions and capital flight, slowing development further. Conclusion: aligning consent and environmental standards with development goals is both ethically required and practically faster than the current ad-hoc model.

  • Choosing a question too broad to finish — narrowing in scope is itself an analytical skill.
  • Skipping the counter-argument — strong inquiries treat the opposing view fairly.
  • Padding with unrelated background — every paragraph should serve the thesis.
  • Underusing maps and charts — a single strong map can carry an analysis that 1000 words cannot.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.6 — "use geographic perspectives to make informed judgements and decisions about world issues."
