🌏 4.1 Globalization and the Logic of Trade
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Globalization is the increasing integration of economies, societies, and cultures across the world, driven by trade, investment, technology, migration, and communication. Geographers usually distinguish four dimensions: economic (trade and capital flows), political (intergovernmental institutions and global governance), cultural (media, food, language, fashion), and environmental (shared climate and ecosystems). Globalization is old — Silk Road trade, the Atlantic slave-trade triangle, and the 19th-century gold standard were all global systems — but its pace and depth since the 1990s are unprecedented. Container shipping (introduced 1956), satellite communications (1960s), the World Wide Web (1991), and the rise of China (WTO accession 2001) drove a doubling of world trade as a share of GDP between 1986 and 2008.

The economic logic of trade rests on comparative advantage, articulated by David Ricardo in 1817. Two countries each gain if each specialises in the goods where it has the lowest opportunity cost, then trades for the rest. Comparative advantage explains why even high-wage countries import many goods: it is not about who is "best" at making something, but who gives up least to make it. The theory is silent on distribution within countries — gains are shared in the aggregate, but specific workers, firms, and regions can lose. Free-trade theory thus implies a need for redistribution and adjustment assistance, which often does not materialise.

Globalization has been blamed for and credited with widely divergent outcomes. It has lifted hundreds of millions out of extreme poverty in China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, and India. It has also concentrated profits in transnational corporations, displaced manufacturing workers in deindustrialising regions (the U.S. Rust Belt, parts of southern Ontario), and exported pollution. The 2008 financial crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the war in Ukraine have driven a partial pullback — sometimes called slowbalization or friend-shoring — as countries seek resilience over pure efficiency.

  • Globalization — increasing integration of economies and societies worldwide.
  • Comparative advantage — Ricardian principle: trade is mutually beneficial if each party specialises in its lowest-opportunity-cost goods.
  • Opportunity cost — value of the next-best alternative foregone.
  • Container shipping — standardised intermodal containers that revolutionised freight costs.
  • Slowbalization / friend-shoring — recent partial reversal favouring resilience and political alignment.
  • Gross World Product — total value of goods and services produced worldwide.

Worked example — Comparative advantage

Suppose Canada can produce in one hour either 4 bushels of wheat or 2 barrels of maple syrup; the U.S. can produce either 6 bushels of wheat or 1 barrel of maple syrup. Which country has comparative advantage in each good?

Canada's opportunity cost of 1 syrup = 2 wheat; U.S.'s opportunity cost of 1 syrup = 6 wheat. Canada gives up less wheat to make syrup, so Canada has comparative advantage in syrup. Canada's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 0.5 syrup; U.S.'s opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 0.167 syrup. The U.S. gives up less syrup to make wheat, so the U.S. has comparative advantage in wheat. Both gain by specialising and trading, even though the U.S. is more productive per hour at both goods (absolute advantage).

  • Confusing absolute advantage (raw productivity) with comparative advantage (opportunity cost).
  • Assuming trade benefits everyone equally — gains and losses are unevenly distributed.
  • Treating globalization as new — it has waxed and waned over centuries.
  • Forgetting that comparative advantage assumes resources can shift between sectors — adjustment is rarely costless.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.1 — "analyse the consequences of economic globalization for workers, consumers, and the environment in different parts of the world."

📜 4.2 Trade Agreements and the World Trade Organization
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

International trade is governed by a layered web of rules. At the multilateral level, the World Trade Organization (WTO), headquartered in Geneva, was established on 1 January 1995 as successor to the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT, 1947). It has 164 members covering approximately 98% of world trade. The WTO administers trade agreements, hosts negotiations (the long-stalled Doha Round began in 2001), and operates a dispute-settlement body. Its core principles are most-favoured-nation (MFN) treatment (a country's best trade terms must apply equally to all WTO members), national treatment (imported goods must be treated like domestic goods once across the border), tariff binding, and the prohibition of quantitative restrictions.

Bilateral and regional agreements have proliferated as WTO multilateral progress has slowed. Canada–United States–Mexico Agreement (CUSMA/USMCA), in force 1 July 2020, replaced NAFTA (1994). It updates rules on digital trade, dairy market access, labour, environment, and rules of origin — notably increasing the regional content required for tariff-free automobiles to 75%. The Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), signed by 11 Pacific Rim countries in 2018 (the United States withdrew in 2017; the UK joined as the 12th party in 2023), covers about 15% of world GDP. The Canada–European Union Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), provisionally in force since 21 September 2017, opens most goods trade and harmonises many regulations. Canada is also part of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) outreach, the ASEAN dialogue partnership, and ongoing Indo-Pacific negotiations.

Trade liberalisation lowers consumer prices and expands choice but exposes domestic producers to competition. Many agreements now include investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS) provisions allowing foreign investors to sue host governments — controversial because tribunals can override domestic regulations. The 2017 NAFTA renegotiation responded to U.S. concerns about trade deficits; CUSMA's sunset clause requires the parties to review the agreement every six years.

  • WTO — World Trade Organization (1995), successor to GATT.
  • MFN — most-favoured-nation rule: trade concessions extended to one member apply to all.
  • National treatment — imported goods treated equally to domestic goods.
  • Tariff binding — commitment not to raise tariffs above agreed ceilings.
  • Rules of origin — criteria determining where a good was "made" for tariff purposes.
  • ISDS — investor-state dispute settlement, allowing investors to sue states under treaty law.

Worked example — Tariff impact

Suppose a Canadian importer brings in steel from a country with which Canada has no FTA. The MFN tariff on the steel is 25%. The same steel from a CUSMA partner enters duty-free. If the duty-free price is $1000/tonne and the MFN price is $1100/tonne (CIF, before tariff), which source is cheaper landed in Canada?

CUSMA price landed = $1000 × 1.00 = $1000/tonne. MFN price landed = $1100 × 1.25 = $1375/tonne. CUSMA steel is $375/tonne cheaper. This is called trade diversion — the FTA shifts trade toward partners regardless of underlying efficiency. Trade creation happens when the FTA enables imports that previously weren't viable; both effects shape the welfare verdict on an FTA.

  • Treating tariffs as the only barrier — non-tariff barriers (quotas, regulations, subsidies) can matter more.
  • Confusing FTAs with customs unions (CETA is an FTA; EU is a customs union).
  • Forgetting that WTO membership requires accepting MFN — there is no "à la carte" option.
  • Assuming free trade always increases overall welfare — it usually does, but distribution effects matter.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.2 — "analyse the influence of trade agreements (CUSMA/USMCA, CETA, CPTPP, WTO rules) on the Canadian economy."

🏢 4.3 Transnational Corporations and Foreign Direct Investment
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A transnational corporation (TNC) — sometimes called a multinational corporation (MNC) — owns or controls production and service operations in more than one country. The largest TNCs by revenue in 2024 include Walmart, Amazon, Saudi Aramco, Apple, ExxonMobil, Toyota, and Berkshire Hathaway. UNCTAD estimates that about 80 000 TNCs operate roughly 800 000 foreign affiliates, accounting for around one-third of global GDP and over half of world trade (much of it intra-firm). The largest TNCs have revenues exceeding the GDP of most countries — Apple's 2024 revenue ($391 billion) is larger than Portugal's GDP.

TNCs decide where to locate stages of production based on costs (labour, capital, taxes, regulation), market access (proximity to customers), agglomeration (clusters of suppliers and skilled labour), and political risk. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) is when a firm establishes or acquires lasting interest in an enterprise in another economy — building a plant in Mexico, buying a competitor in Germany, or opening a research lab in India. Canada is both a major source and recipient of FDI; stocks of inward and outward FDI each exceed $1 trillion. The geography of FDI is shifting: China, India, and Southeast Asia attract growing inflows, while Europe and the U.S. remain dominant in services and high-value manufacturing.

TNCs can transfer technology, create jobs, and integrate countries into global production networks; they can also extract resources, evade taxes, suppress wages, and lobby for weak regulation. The OECD's Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) initiative, including the 2021 global agreement on a 15% minimum corporate tax, attempts to limit tax-arbitrage by TNCs. Cases like the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh, which killed 1134 garment workers and exposed the global fast-fashion supply chain, have raised expectations of corporate due diligence in supply chains.

  • Transnational corporation (TNC) — firm with production or services in multiple countries.
  • Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) — lasting cross-border investment, usually ≥ 10% ownership stake.
  • Global value chain (GVC) — sequence of value-adding activities split across countries.
  • Outsourcing vs offshoring — contracting to external firms (outsourcing) vs relocating own operations (offshoring); can overlap.
  • Race to the bottom — competitive lowering of standards (wages, regulation, taxes) to attract investment.
  • BEPS / 15% minimum tax — OECD-led effort to curb tax-base erosion by TNCs.

Worked example — TNC revenue vs national GDP

Apple's 2024 revenue was approximately US$391 billion. Pakistan's 2024 GDP was approximately US$338 billion. If Apple were a country, where would it rank, and what does this comparison reveal about TNC power?

Apple's revenue exceeds Pakistan's GDP (a country of about 240 million people) by about US$53 billion — Apple would rank around the 45th largest economy in the world. Strictly, revenue and GDP are not the same metric (GDP is value added; revenue includes intermediate inputs), but the comparison illustrates how a single firm can rival national economies in scale, raising questions about its political influence, regulatory leverage, and tax obligations. This is one reason for the global minimum tax.

  • Confusing TNC revenue with TNC value added — only value added is directly comparable to GDP.
  • Treating "outsourcing" and "offshoring" as synonyms.
  • Saying FDI is always greenfield investment — a large share is cross-border mergers and acquisitions.
  • Forgetting that many TNCs are state-owned (Saudi Aramco, China Petrochemical, Volkswagen post-bailout).

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.3 — "analyse the roles of transnational corporations in shaping global economic development."

🏦 4.4 The IMF, World Bank, and Global Financial Governance
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Bretton Woods institutions — the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (now the core of the World Bank Group) — were established at the Bretton Woods conference in New Hampshire in July 1944. They are headquartered in Washington, D.C., a few blocks apart, but have distinct mandates. The IMF promotes global monetary cooperation and financial stability, surveils member economies, and lends to countries facing balance-of-payments crises. The World Bank (which now includes the IBRD, the IDA for poorest countries, and three other affiliates) lends for long-term development projects and policy reforms. Voting power in both institutions is weighted by financial contribution (quota), giving the U.S. a near-veto on major decisions; emerging-economy quotas have been incrementally rebalanced but remain below their share of world GDP.

Both institutions have come under sustained critique. Structural adjustment programs (SAPs) of the 1980s–90s required borrowing countries to liberalise trade, privatise state enterprises, devalue currencies, and cut public spending. Critics argue SAPs deepened poverty in much of Africa and Latin America during the "lost decades." More recent IMF programmes (Argentina 2018, Sri Lanka 2023, Egypt 2024) emphasise social-spending floors and growth-friendly conditionality, but austerity remains controversial. The World Bank's anchor publication World Development Report sets development agendas; its more recent priorities include human capital, climate finance, and infrastructure.

Alongside the IMF and World Bank, other institutions and processes matter. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) coordinates central banks. The G7 (Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, the United States) and G20 (G7 plus major emerging economies) provide informal high-level coordination. Newer development banks — the BRICS-led New Development Bank (2014) and the China-led Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (2016) — offer alternatives shaped less by U.S. and European interests. The 2023 Bridgetown Initiative, championed by Barbados's Prime Minister Mia Mottley, proposes major reform of the international financial architecture for climate finance.

  • IMF — International Monetary Fund; surveillance and short-term lending.
  • World Bank Group — IBRD + IDA + IFC + MIGA + ICSID; development lending and advisory.
  • Quota and voting — IMF/World Bank shares determined by financial contribution.
  • Structural adjustment program (SAP) — conditional reform package historically tied to IMF/World Bank lending.
  • Balance of payments — record of a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world.
  • BRICS — Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa (expanded 2024 with several new members).

Worked example — Debt-to-GDP and IMF programme

A country's external debt is US$80 billion and its GDP is US$200 billion. Its annual debt-service cost is US$10 billion against exports of US$50 billion. Compute its debt-to-GDP ratio and its debt-service-to-exports ratio, and judge whether it is in distress by IMF guidelines (debt-service > 20% of exports is typically a warning).

Debt-to-GDP = \(\frac{80}{200} = 40\%\), moderate. Debt-service-to-exports = \(\frac{10}{50} = 20\%\), at the warning threshold. The country may negotiate an IMF stand-by arrangement to restructure debt and stabilise the currency. Programme conditions might include fiscal consolidation (raising taxes, cutting subsidies), monetary tightening, and exchange-rate flexibility — each politically painful but designed to restore confidence.

  • Confusing the IMF and the World Bank — different mandates, same address.
  • Saying IMF programmes always require austerity — recent programmes increasingly include social-spending floors.
  • Equating quota with population — quotas track financial contribution, not population.
  • Treating BRICS and the AIIB as direct competitors of Bretton Woods — they coexist and overlap.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.4 — "analyse the roles of the IMF and the World Bank in shaping global development."

🚢 4.5 Global Supply Chains and Critical Minerals
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The modern smartphone, electric vehicle, and pair of running shoes are each the product of a global value chain (GVC) in which design, components, assembly, and distribution are split across many countries. Apple's iPhone is designed in Cupertino, California; uses memory chips from South Korea (Samsung, SK Hynix); processors fabricated in Taiwan (TSMC); displays from South Korea or China; cobalt from the Democratic Republic of the Congo; and is assembled by Foxconn workers in Zhengzhou, China, with growing capacity in India and Vietnam. The economic geography textbook The World Is Flat (Thomas Friedman, 2005) overstated the case, but the basic insight — that production has been disaggregated across borders to an unprecedented degree — is sound.

The economics of GVCs reward firms that capture the highest-value-added stages — typically design, branding, and after-sales services (the famous "smile curve"). The middle of the curve, assembly, captures a small slice of the final retail price. A 2010 Asian Development Bank Institute study estimated that of the $179 retail wholesale value of an iPhone 4 at the time, only $6.50 was Chinese assembly value-added, while Japanese, German, Korean, and U.S. firms captured most of the rest. Critical-minerals supply chains for the energy transition are especially concentrated: China controls roughly 60% of global lithium refining, 80% of cobalt refining, and over 90% of rare-earth processing. About 70% of cobalt mining occurs in the DRC, often with documented child-labour concerns. Indonesia and the Philippines dominate nickel; Chile, Australia, and increasingly Argentina dominate lithium mining.

Recent disruptions — the 2020–2022 semiconductor shortage, the 2021 Suez Canal blockage by the Ever Given, the COVID-19 lockdowns, and the Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping in 2024 — have driven policy responses: re-shoring (returning production home), near-shoring (to allied neighbours), and friend-shoring (within trusted blocs). Canada has positioned itself as a critical-minerals supplier (lithium in Quebec, nickel in Ontario, cobalt and uranium in Saskatchewan), and the 2022 Canadian Critical Minerals Strategy commits $3.8 billion to develop the sector.

  • Global value chain (GVC) — geographic disaggregation of production stages.
  • Smile curve — U-shape of value capture along a GVC; high at design/services, low at assembly.
  • Critical minerals — materials essential for clean-energy or strategic technologies (Li, Co, Ni, REEs, Cu).
  • Re-shoring / near-shoring / friend-shoring — strategies to reduce GVC vulnerability.
  • Lead time — duration between order placement and delivery.
  • Bullwhip effect — amplification of demand variability up a supply chain.

Worked example — Mapping value capture

A pair of running shoes retails for $120 in Toronto. Bill of materials and labour costs: design in Oregon $8, leather and rubber from Brazil $11, assembly in Vietnam $5, transport to Canada $4. Retailer markup is $40, brand royalty $30, marketing $14, retailer overhead $8. Compute the share of final price captured at each location.

Oregon (design + royalty + marketing) = \(\frac{8 + 30 + 14}{120} = 43\%\). Brazil (materials) = \(\frac{11}{120} \approx 9\%\). Vietnam (assembly) = \(\frac{5}{120} \approx 4\%\). Transport \(\approx 3\%\). Retailer (Canada) \(\frac{40 + 8}{120} = 40\%\). The U.S.-based brand and the Canadian retailer capture more than 80% of the retail dollar; the country whose workers physically assemble the shoes captures about 4% — the smile-curve pattern in numbers.

  • Equating "Made in X" with "made entirely in X" — most consumer goods have multi-country origins.
  • Treating supply-chain resilience and efficiency as compatible — they usually trade off.
  • Forgetting that critical-mineral demand is forecast to grow 4–6 × by 2040 with the energy transition.
  • Conflating mining and refining — China's refining dominance is often the binding constraint, not mining.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.5 — "evaluate the geographic distribution of supply chains for selected goods and the impact on Canadian industries."

✈️ 4.6 Migration, Remittances, and Digital Connections
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

About 281 million people — roughly 3.6% of the world's population — live outside their country of birth, according to the UN Population Division. This figure has tripled since 1970. Migration is driven by combinations of push factors (lack of work, conflict, climate stress, persecution) and pull factors (job opportunities, family reunification, education, safety). Migration corridors are geographically distinctive: Mexico → United States (the largest single corridor for decades), India → United Arab Emirates, Syria → Türkiye, Ukraine → Poland and Germany, the Philippines → Saudi Arabia and Canada. Canada has the highest per-capita immigration intake in the G7 (~470 000 permanent residents in 2024, around 1.2% of population annually), and immigration accounts for nearly all of Canadian labour-force growth.

Migrant remittances — money sent home — are a major financial flow, estimated by the World Bank at US$685 billion to low- and middle-income countries in 2024. Remittances exceed both official development assistance and foreign direct investment to many recipient countries. The top recipients in absolute terms are India (~US$135 billion in 2024), Mexico, China, the Philippines, Pakistan, and Egypt. As a share of GDP, remittances dominate small open economies: Tajikistan (~50% of GDP), Tonga, Lebanon, Samoa, the Kyrgyz Republic. Average cost of sending remittances was 6.4% in 2023, well above the SDG 10.c target of 3%. Lower fees would put billions more dollars directly into recipient households.

Digital connectivity has reshaped global links beyond money. About 5.5 billion people are now internet users; mobile broadband covers ~95% of the world's population, though access remains uneven. Digital platforms (Meta, Google, TikTok, Amazon, Alibaba) operate planetary-scale networks subject to limited national regulation. Remote work is breaking the historical link between job and location — about 25% of paid days in advanced economies are worked from home in 2024. New geographic phenomena include digital nomadism, remote-first companies, and tax-arbitrage of digital workers.

  • International migrant — person living outside their country of birth.
  • Push and pull factors — conditions encouraging departure or attracting arrival.
  • Remittance — funds transferred by migrants to their home country.
  • Brain drain / brain gain / brain circulation — emigration of skilled workers and possible reversals.
  • Diaspora — dispersed community sharing common origins.
  • Digital divide — gap between those with and without effective digital access.

Worked example — Remittance impact

The Philippines received approximately US$40 billion in remittances in 2024 against a GDP of about US$465 billion. Calculate remittances as a share of GDP and discuss the macroeconomic implications.

Remittance share = \(\frac{40}{465} \approx 8.6\%\) of GDP. At this scale, remittances support household consumption, school enrolment, and small-business formation, and contribute to balance-of-payments stability. They are counter-cyclical (Filipinos abroad often send more home during domestic downturns), unlike portfolio flows. The downside is exchange-rate appreciation that can weaken export competitiveness ("Dutch disease"-style effects), and a labour-market dependence on overseas employment that exposes families to host-country shocks (Saudi labour-policy reforms, Gulf War evacuations, COVID lockdowns).

  • Treating remittances as charity — they are family transfers tied to migrant earnings.
  • Confusing migrants with refugees — most migrants move voluntarily for work or family.
  • Ignoring brain-drain offsets: remittances and diaspora networks can produce brain circulation.
  • Treating internet access as universal — about 30% of the world is still offline.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.4 — "explain how digital connectivity, remittances, and migration link distant places."
