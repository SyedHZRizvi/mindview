🌡️ 2.1 The Greenhouse Effect and Anthropogenic Climate Change
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The greenhouse effect is a natural process that makes Earth habitable. Solar radiation peaks in the visible spectrum and passes through the atmosphere; Earth's surface absorbs it and re-radiates energy at longer infrared wavelengths. Certain atmospheric gases — water vapour, carbon dioxide (\(CO_2\)), methane (\(CH_4\)), nitrous oxide (\(N_2O\)), and fluorinated gases — have molecular bonds that vibrate at infrared frequencies, absorbing outgoing radiation and re-emitting some of it back toward the surface. Without these greenhouse gases (GHGs), Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C; with them it averages about +15 °C, supporting liquid water and complex life.

Since the start of the Industrial Revolution, humans have raised atmospheric \(CO_2\) from roughly 280 ppm (pre-1750) to over 422 ppm in 2024 — a 50% increase — by burning fossil fuels (~75% of emissions), clearing forests, and producing cement. Methane has more than doubled, and \(N_2O\) has risen sharply from synthetic fertiliser use. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), founded in 1988 by the WMO and UNEP, concluded in its Sixth Assessment Report (2021–2023) that "it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land." Global average surface temperature is now ~1.2 °C above the 1850–1900 baseline; the Arctic has warmed more than three times the global average.

Observed impacts include sea-level rise (~21 cm since 1900, accelerating), more intense heatwaves and wildfires, shifted growing seasons, ocean acidification (~0.1 pH drop, a 30% increase in hydrogen-ion concentration), and the loss of more than half of Arctic summer sea ice. Climate change is not weather — it is the long-term shift in the statistics of weather. Geographers analyse uneven impacts: small island states, the Sahel, the Arctic, and densely populated low-elevation coastal zones face the greatest exposure.

  • Greenhouse effect — natural warming caused by GHGs absorbing outgoing infrared radiation.
  • Anthropogenic — caused by human activity.
  • Parts per million (ppm) — units used to express atmospheric trace-gas concentrations.
  • Radiative forcing — change in energy balance at the top of the atmosphere, measured in W/m².
  • Global Warming Potential (GWP) — relative warming impact of a gas compared to \(CO_2\) over 100 years (\(CH_4\) GWP ≈ 28, \(N_2O\) ≈ 273).
  • IPCC — Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, scientific advisory body to the UN.

Worked example — Converting methane emissions to \(CO_2\)-equivalent

A landfill emits 1500 tonnes of methane per year. Convert to \(CO_2\)-equivalent (\(CO_2e\)) using GWP-100 of 28.

\(CO_2e = \text{mass of gas} \times GWP = 1500 \text{ t} \times 28 = 42\,000\) tonnes \(CO_2e\) per year. The same site, captured and burned in a generator, would release \(CO_2\) equivalent to about 4125 tonnes (from the carbon content of the captured \(CH_4\)) — roughly a tenfold reduction in radiative forcing plus electricity generated. This is why landfill-gas capture is one of the most cost-effective climate interventions.

  • Confusing weather with climate — climate is the statistics of weather over ≥30 years.
  • Saying "\(CO_2\) is plant food, so more is better" — yes, plants use \(CO_2\), but warming, drought, and pest shifts net out negatively for most crops above ~2 °C.
  • Treating ppm as a small number — molecule-for-molecule effects are large because of band-saturated infrared physics.
  • Ignoring methane and \(N_2O\) — \(CO_2\) is the largest single contributor, but other gases together add ~30%.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "analyse the causes and consequences of anthropogenic climate change."

♻️ 2.2 The Carbon Cycle and Climate Feedbacks
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Carbon cycles between four major reservoirs: the atmosphere (~875 Gt C as \(CO_2\)), the ocean (~38 000 Gt C, mostly as dissolved inorganic carbon), the terrestrial biosphere (~550 Gt C in plants and ~1500 Gt C in soils), and the lithosphere (sedimentary rocks and fossil fuels, ~5 000 000 Gt C). Natural fluxes between these pools are enormous but roughly balanced over geological timescales. The fast cycle moves carbon among atmosphere, biosphere, and surface ocean through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, and air–sea gas exchange. Annual gross photosynthesis is about 120 Gt C, almost balanced by respiration. The slow cycle, over millions of years, transfers carbon to and from rocks via weathering, burial, volcanism, and (now) human extraction of fossil fuels.

Human activities have added a one-way pulse to the cycle. Burning fossil fuels currently injects about 9.6 Gt C/year (~35 Gt \(CO_2\)/year) into the atmosphere; land-use change adds another ~1.5 Gt C. The ocean and terrestrial biosphere together absorb about half of this; the rest accumulates in the atmosphere — which is why concentrations rise. The ocean is the largest active sink, but absorption is acidifying surface waters and slowing as warming reduces gas solubility.

Climate feedbacks are processes that amplify (positive feedback) or dampen (negative feedback) the initial perturbation. The most powerful positive feedbacks include the ice–albedo feedback (loss of bright ice exposes dark ocean, absorbing more sunlight), permafrost methane release (thawing tundra releases stored \(CH_4\)), and water-vapour feedback (warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a GHG). Negative feedbacks include increased outgoing infrared radiation (Planck response) and enhanced \(CO_2\) uptake by plants (the "fertilisation effect"). Net feedbacks are strongly positive, meaning warming begets more warming.

  • Carbon reservoir — a storage pool of carbon (atmosphere, ocean, biosphere, lithosphere).
  • Flux — rate of carbon transfer between reservoirs (Gt C/year).
  • Sink — a reservoir absorbing more carbon than it releases.
  • Positive feedback — process that amplifies the original change (warming → more warming).
  • Albedo — fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected; ice ~0.8, open ocean ~0.06.
  • Permafrost — ground frozen for ≥ 2 consecutive years; stores ~1500 Gt C, twice atmospheric carbon.

Worked example — Airborne fraction

In a given year, fossil-fuel and land-use emissions total 11 Gt C. Atmospheric \(CO_2\) rose by 2.6 ppm. Given that 1 ppm \(CO_2\) ≈ 2.13 Gt C in the atmosphere, calculate the airborne fraction (the share of emissions that remained in the atmosphere).

Atmospheric increase = \(2.6 \times 2.13 = 5.54\) Gt C. Airborne fraction = \(\frac{5.54}{11} \approx 0.50\), or 50%. The remaining 50% was absorbed roughly evenly by the ocean and the land biosphere. If the ocean sink saturates as it warms, the airborne fraction will rise — accelerating atmospheric accumulation even at constant emissions.

  • Confusing carbon (C) and \(CO_2\) — 1 t C = 3.67 t \(CO_2\) because \(CO_2\) is heavier.
  • Treating all sinks as permanent — soils and oceans can become sources under warming.
  • Ignoring methane oxidation: \(CH_4\) breaks down to \(CO_2\) in about 12 years, so its climate effect is large and short.
  • Forgetting that the slow geological cycle is overwhelmed by the rapid human pulse.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.3 — "explain the carbon cycle, the water cycle, and feedback loops in the climate system."

👣 2.3 The Ecological Footprint and Planetary Boundaries
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The ecological footprint, developed by Mathis Wackernagel and William Rees at the University of British Columbia in the 1990s, measures the area of biologically productive land and water that a population requires to produce the resources it consumes and to absorb the wastes it generates. It is reported in global hectares (gha) per person. Biocapacity is the supply side — the productive area available. Earth Overshoot Day, calculated annually by the Global Footprint Network, marks the date by which humanity has used all of the resources the planet can regenerate that year; in 2024 it fell on 1 August. Beyond that date, the world runs on ecological deficit.

Per-capita footprints vary widely. The world average is about 2.6 gha against a biocapacity of about 1.6 gha — overshoot by a factor of roughly 1.7. Canadians average about 7.7 gha, Americans 8.0 gha, while citizens of Bangladesh or Pakistan average about 0.9 gha. If every person lived like the average Canadian, humanity would need approximately 4.8 Earths. Most of the footprint comes from carbon (the area needed to absorb \(CO_2\) emissions); the rest comes from cropland, grazing land, fishing grounds, forest products, and built-up area.

Johan Rockström and colleagues proposed the planetary boundaries framework in 2009 (updated 2023): nine Earth-system processes, each with a "safe operating space" beyond which abrupt or irreversible change becomes likely. Six of the nine — climate change, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus), land-system change, freshwater change, and novel entities — have already been transgressed. The footprint and boundary frameworks complement each other: footprint measures aggregate human pressure, boundaries flag which Earth-system limits are nearest. Doughnut economics (Kate Raworth) combines a social floor (no one falls below basic needs) with an ecological ceiling (no one pushes past boundaries).

  • Ecological footprint — global hectares required per person to support a given lifestyle.
  • Biocapacity — productive area available to supply resources and absorb wastes.
  • Earth Overshoot Day — date when annual demand exceeds annual regeneration.
  • Carbon footprint — area required to absorb \(CO_2\) emissions; often the largest component.
  • Planetary boundaries — nine Earth-system thresholds; six already crossed.
  • Doughnut economics — framework combining social floor and ecological ceiling.

Worked example — How many Earths?

If everyone consumed at the level of an average resident of Country Z, with a footprint of 6.4 gha and current global biocapacity of 1.6 gha per person, how many Earths would humanity require?

Number of Earths = \(\frac{6.4}{1.6} = 4.0\). Four planets' worth of regenerative capacity would be needed. Reducing the carbon component (largest share, mainly transportation and home energy) is the highest-leverage individual action; structural change (electricity grid, building codes, transit, food systems) is more important than personal choice alone.

  • Treating the footprint as the only measure — it under-counts biodiversity loss and certain pollutants.
  • Assuming individual action is enough — most of the footprint is determined by infrastructure choices.
  • Confusing biocapacity with the area of a country — Canada is biocapacity-rich because of its forests and farmland.
  • Reading planetary boundaries as cliffs — most are gradient zones where risk rises with transgression.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "calculate and interpret ecological footprints and identify strategies to reduce them."

📜 2.4 International Climate Policy — Kyoto, Paris, and Carbon Pricing
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

International climate cooperation began at the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio, where 154 nations signed the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), agreeing to "stabilize greenhouse-gas concentrations at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system." The UNFCCC enshrined the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities: all countries share the problem, but rich, historically large emitters bear greater obligation. The treaty established annual Conference of the Parties (COP) meetings.

The Kyoto Protocol, adopted at COP3 in 1997, set binding emissions reduction targets for industrialised countries (Annex I) for 2008–2012 — roughly 5% below 1990 levels. It introduced carbon-market mechanisms (Clean Development Mechanism, Joint Implementation, emissions trading), but had no targets for developing economies and was never ratified by the United States. The Paris Agreement, adopted at COP21 in December 2015 and entered into force November 2016, replaced Kyoto's top-down architecture with bottom-up Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) from every party. Its goal is to hold warming "well below 2 °C" above pre-industrial, pursuing efforts toward 1.5 °C. NDCs are updated every five years, with a global stocktake; the first stocktake in 2023 concluded that current pledges put the world on track for about 2.5–2.9 °C of warming — short of the goal but a sharp reduction from the 4 °C trajectory of the early 2000s.

Domestically, countries use a mix of policy tools. Carbon pricing attaches a cost to each tonne of \(CO_2\)-equivalent emitted, either through a carbon tax (price set, quantity floats) or a cap-and-trade system (quantity set, price floats). Canada's federal carbon price reached $80/tonne in 2024 and is scheduled to rise to $170/tonne by 2030. Other tools include renewable-portfolio standards, fuel-economy mandates, building codes, subsidies for EVs and heat pumps, and methane regulations. Effective policy mixes target the largest emission categories first (electricity, transport, buildings, industry).

  • UNFCCC — 1992 framework treaty on climate change.
  • Kyoto Protocol — 1997 treaty with binding targets for Annex I countries.
  • Paris Agreement — 2015 treaty with universal but voluntary NDCs.
  • Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) — country's self-set climate pledge under Paris.
  • Carbon tax — explicit price per tonne of emitted \(CO_2e\).
  • Cap-and-trade — emissions cap with tradable permits (e.g., EU ETS).

Worked example — Carbon-tax impact on gasoline

Burning 1 L of gasoline releases approximately 2.3 kg of \(CO_2\). If a federal carbon tax is $80 per tonne of \(CO_2\), what is the per-litre tax cost?

Per-litre tax = \(\frac{2.3 \text{ kg}}{1000 \text{ kg/t}} \times 80 \text{ \$/t} = 0.184 \text{ \$/L}\), about 18.4 cents per litre. At $170/t (2030), the per-litre cost would be about 39 cents. The intent is to make consumers and firms internalise the climate cost of their choices, nudging them toward fuel-efficient vehicles, transit, or electric alternatives.

  • Confusing the Paris Agreement's 1.5/2 °C goal with the actual policy commitments, which currently fall short.
  • Treating carbon pricing as the only tool — economists recommend it alongside complementary regulations and public investment.
  • Forgetting that revenue-neutral carbon pricing (rebated to households) can be progressive — most Canadian households receive more back than they pay.
  • Saying climate policy is "anti-development" — emerging-economy NDCs are increasingly economic-development plans.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "analyse the role of the IPCC, UNFCCC, Paris Agreement, and national climate plans in addressing climate change."

💧 2.5 Water Security and the Global Water Crisis
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Of the roughly 1.4 billion km³ of water on Earth, only about 2.5% is fresh water and only ~0.5% is readily accessible (lakes, rivers, shallow groundwater). Water security, defined by UN-Water, is the capacity of a population to safeguard sustainable access to adequate quantities of water of acceptable quality for sustaining livelihoods, human well-being, and socio-economic development. As of 2024, approximately 2.2 billion people lack safely managed drinking water, and 3.5 billion lack safely managed sanitation. Roughly 4 billion people experience severe water scarcity for at least one month each year. The geography is uneven: arid and semi-arid regions (the Middle East, North Africa, the U.S. Southwest, parts of India and China) face chronic scarcity, while tropical regions face episodic scarcity from poor infrastructure and pollution.

Water is used in three main sectors: agriculture (~70% globally), industry (~20%), and domestic use (~10%). Irrigation has tripled food production since 1950 but has also drawn down major aquifers (the Ogallala in the U.S. Great Plains, the North China Plain, northern India). Surface-water disputes are common in transboundary basins: the Nile (Egypt, Sudan, Ethiopia, with the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam reshaping flows), the Mekong (China's upstream dams), the Indus (India and Pakistan under the 1960 Treaty), and the Tigris-Euphrates (Turkey, Syria, Iraq). Climate change is intensifying the cycle — wetter where it rains, drier where it doesn't — and shrinking glacial melt that feeds rivers like the Ganges and Indus.

Canada holds about 7% of the world's renewable fresh water and 20% of fresh water by volume (much of it locked in lakes), but distribution is uneven and many First Nations communities have been under long-term drinking water advisories. As of late 2024, 27 long-term advisories remained on First Nations reserves, despite a federal commitment to eliminate them by 2021. Bottled-water companies extracting groundwater (Nestlé in Aberfoyle, Ontario, until 2020) have drawn criticism.

  • Water security — sustainable access to adequate quantity and quality of water.
  • Renewable water resources — long-term average annual flow from precipitation through runoff and recharge.
  • Aquifer — water-bearing permeable rock or sediment formation.
  • Transboundary basin — watershed crossing political borders.
  • Virtual water — water embedded in the production of goods (1 kg beef ≈ 15 000 L water).
  • Long-term drinking water advisory — water-quality warning in effect for over a year (Canada).

Worked example — Per-capita renewable water

Country P has renewable water of 12 km³/yr and a population of 30 million. Country Q has renewable water of 100 km³/yr and a population of 12 million. Compute per-capita renewable water and classify each using the Falkenmark indicator (1700 m³/person·yr = stress, 1000 = scarcity, 500 = absolute scarcity).

Country P: \(\frac{12 \times 10^9 \text{ m}^3}{30 \times 10^6} = 400\) m³/person — absolute scarcity. Country Q: \(\frac{100 \times 10^9}{12 \times 10^6} \approx 8333\) m³/person — water-abundant. Country P will need to invest heavily in efficiency (drip irrigation), reuse (treated wastewater), or supply augmentation (desalination, transfers). Country Q can afford less efficiency but should still protect quality.

  • Confusing fresh water with potable water — most fresh water needs treatment before drinking.
  • Treating Canada as immune to water issues — quality, access, and aquifer over-pumping all matter.
  • Forgetting that water-intensive imports (virtual water) shift water demand across borders.
  • Confusing scarcity (physical lack) with stress (high demand pressure on supply).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "analyse the geographic distribution of water resources and the impact of water scarcity on quality of life."

🌾 2.6 Food Security and Sustainable Agriculture
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Food security, defined by the FAO 1996 World Food Summit, exists "when all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life." It rests on four pillars: availability (sufficient supply), access (income and infrastructure to obtain it), utilization (nutritional quality and food safety), and stability (resilience to shocks). The world produces enough calories per capita to feed everyone, yet about 733 million people faced hunger in 2024 and roughly 2.3 billion were food-insecure, according to the State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI) report.

Hunger geography reflects conflict, climate, and economic inequality. The largest absolute numbers of undernourished people are in South Asia; the highest prevalence is in sub-Saharan Africa. Famines remain rare (Somalia 2011, South Sudan 2017, Yemen 2018, Gaza 2024 — almost all conflict-driven) but the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) system grades food-security crises from Phase 1 (minimal) to Phase 5 (famine/catastrophe). The Green Revolution of the 1960s–80s tripled cereal yields in Asia and Latin America by introducing high-yielding varieties, irrigation, and fertilisers, but it left a legacy of soil degradation, monocultures, groundwater depletion, and chemical runoff.

Sustainable agriculture today aims to maintain or increase yields while reducing environmental footprint. Strategies include precision agriculture (sensors, variable-rate fertilisation), conservation tillage, cover cropping, agroforestry, integrated pest management, and dietary shifts toward less meat-intensive diets. About 26% of land-system greenhouse-gas emissions come from agriculture; reducing food waste (about a third of food produced is lost or wasted) could free large quantities of land and water. Canadian agriculture faces particular pressures from changing growing seasons, drought (especially in the Prairies), and pest range expansion.

  • Food security — sufficient, safe, nutritious, and accessible food for all.
  • Four pillars — availability, access, utilization, stability.
  • IPC — Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, a 5-phase scale.
  • Green Revolution — mid-20th-century technology-driven yield increases.
  • Food waste — edible food lost between farm and fork; roughly one-third of all food produced.
  • Sustainable intensification — increasing yields per hectare while reducing environmental impact.

Worked example — Calories vs cropland required by diet

An average North American diet provides about 3600 kcal/day and requires roughly 4500 m² of cropland-equivalent per person per year. A primarily plant-based diet provides 2400 kcal/day and requires about 1200 m². If a city of 1 million switched halfway between these two diets, how much cropland-equivalent would it save annually?

Current land use = \(10^6 \times 4500 = 4.5 \times 10^9\) m² = 4500 km². Half-shift land use = \(10^6 \times \frac{4500 + 1200}{2} = 10^6 \times 2850 = 2.85 \times 10^9\) m² = 2850 km². Savings = 1650 km² per year, roughly the area of Greater Vancouver. The shift also reduces emissions, water use, and antibiotic use in livestock.

  • Confusing hunger (insufficient calories) with malnutrition (insufficient nutrients) — both can co-exist.
  • Saying "we need to produce more food" — the existing supply could feed everyone if distribution and waste were addressed.
  • Treating GMOs and conventional crops as inherently better or worse — both can be deployed sustainably or unsustainably depending on practice.
  • Ignoring the food-water-energy nexus — the three are tightly coupled.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.4 — "evaluate strategies to improve food security and the sustainability of agricultural practices globally."
