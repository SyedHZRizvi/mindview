⚔️ 3.1 Geographies of Conflict — Causes and Patterns
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Conflict is a recurring feature of human geography, and its causes are usually layered rather than singular. Political scientists distinguish interstate wars (between sovereign states) from intrastate or civil wars (within a state), and internationalised conflicts where outside powers intervene. Since 1945 the vast majority of armed conflicts have been intrastate. The Uppsala Conflict Data Program counted around 59 active state-based conflicts in 2023, the most since 1946, with violence concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia and Eastern Europe. Geographers study conflict spatially: where it occurs, what natural and human geography conditions are correlated, and how it moves across borders.

Recurring drivers include competition over natural resources (oil in Iraq and South Sudan, diamonds in Sierra Leone and the DRC, water in the Tigris-Euphrates basin), ethnic or sectarian divisions compounded by inequality and exclusion (Rwanda 1994, Sri Lanka, Myanmar's Rohingya), contested borders (Kashmir, Western Sahara, the Russia–Ukraine border), governance failure and weak institutions, and climate stress. The 2007–2010 drought in Syria, the worst in instrumental record, displaced 1.5 million rural Syrians to cities and contributed to the conditions that fed the 2011 civil war (Kelley et al., 2015). Climate alone does not cause war, but it interacts with other vulnerabilities. Paul Collier's conflict trap identifies countries that are caught in a loop where conflict damages the economy, which raises the risk of further conflict.

Conflict imposes long-lasting human costs. The number of forcibly displaced people reached a record 122 million in mid-2024 (UNHCR), including 43 million refugees and 75 million internally displaced. The Global Peace Index, produced annually by the Institute for Economics and Peace, ranks countries on 23 indicators of safety and security; Iceland is consistently most peaceful, while Afghanistan, Yemen, and Syria are at the bottom. Canada ranks among the top 15 most peaceful countries.

  • Interstate vs intrastate conflict — between states vs within a state.
  • Conflict trap — feedback loop where conflict damages economies and raises risk of recurrence.
  • Resource curse — paradoxical underperformance of resource-rich economies, often linked to corruption and conflict.
  • Forcibly displaced person — refugee, asylum seeker, or internally displaced person.
  • Fragile state — government unable to perform basic functions; Fragile States Index lists about 30 countries.
  • Climate-conflict nexus — interaction between climate stress and conflict risk.

Worked example — Conflict-cost ratio

A country's pre-war GDP is $50 billion. A six-year civil war reduces GDP growth by an average of 3 percentage points per year relative to its peacetime trend of 4% per year. Estimate the cumulative GDP lost over the war.

Without war: GDP after 6 years = \(50 \times (1.04)^6 \approx 63.3\) billion. With war: effective growth = 4% − 3% = 1%, GDP after 6 years = \(50 \times (1.01)^6 \approx 53.1\) billion. Annual gap accumulates: integrating the difference, total foregone output is on the order of \(\$30\)–\(40\) billion plus losses in human capital, infrastructure, and trust that take decades to rebuild. War is among the most economically destructive of all geographic processes.

  • Treating conflict as caused by a single factor — most conflicts have layered causes.
  • Confusing correlation with causation — climate and conflict can both depend on weak institutions.
  • Generalising from one case to all conflicts — comparative case-study work is essential.
  • Ignoring how conflict reshapes geography (refugee flows, landmines, scorched-earth land use).

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "analyse the geographic factors that contribute to selected current and historical conflicts."

🚷 3.2 Refugees, IDPs, and the Geography of Displacement
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Displacement is the human face of conflict and disaster. Three legal categories matter. A refugee, defined by the 1951 Refugee Convention, is a person who is outside their country of nationality, with a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, and who is unable or unwilling to return. An asylum seeker has applied for refugee status but not yet been recognised. An internally displaced person (IDP) has been forced to leave their home but has not crossed an international border; IDPs remain under their own state's jurisdiction and protection. The principle of non-refoulement prohibits returning a refugee to a country where they face threats.

The geography of displacement in 2024 is concentrated. Syria, Afghanistan, Ukraine, Venezuela, Sudan, and Myanmar produce the largest refugee outflows. Türkiye, Iran, Germany, Colombia, Pakistan, and Uganda host the largest refugee populations. Contrary to a common assumption, the majority of refugees are hosted by low- and middle-income countries — Lebanon hosts roughly 1.5 million Syrians in a country of 5.5 million. Internal displacement is even larger: about 75 million IDPs worldwide, including 3.4 million in Sudan after April 2023 and an estimated 5 million in Ukraine. The UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) coordinates international protection; the International Organization for Migration (IOM) covers a broader set of migrants.

Three "durable solutions" are recognised: voluntary repatriation (return home in safety), local integration in the host country, and resettlement to a third country. Canada has resettled over 40 000 Syrian refugees since 2015 and is one of the world's leading resettlement countries per capita, including through the Private Sponsorship of Refugees Program, which Canada pioneered. Climate-displaced people are not yet recognised as refugees under international law, despite an estimated 26 million climate-related new displacements per year (IDMC).

  • Refugee — person fleeing across an international border due to fear of persecution.
  • Internally Displaced Person (IDP) — displaced within their own country.
  • Asylum seeker — applicant for refugee status.
  • Non-refoulement — prohibition against returning a refugee to danger.
  • UNHCR — UN refugee agency, founded 1950.
  • Durable solution — voluntary repatriation, local integration, or resettlement.

Worked example — Refugee burden indicator

Lebanon hosts 1.5 million Syrian refugees and has a population of 5.5 million. Germany hosts approximately 1.2 million refugees and has a population of 84 million. Compute and compare the per-capita refugee burden.

Lebanon: \(\frac{1\,500\,000}{5\,500\,000} \approx 0.273\), or 273 refugees per 1000 residents. Germany: \(\frac{1\,200\,000}{84\,000\,000} \approx 0.0143\), or 14 refugees per 1000 residents. Lebanon's per-capita burden is approximately 19 times Germany's. Adjusting for income further widens the burden gap. The data refute the narrative that wealthy countries shoulder most of the responsibility — they do not.

  • Conflating refugees with economic migrants — the legal definitions are distinct.
  • Assuming most refugees are in wealthy countries — most are in poorer neighbouring states.
  • Forgetting IDPs — they outnumber refugees globally.
  • Saying climate-displaced people are "climate refugees" — international law does not yet recognise that status.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.4 — "analyse the impact of conflict on refugees, internally displaced persons, and host communities."

🇺🇳 3.3 The United Nations and Multilateral Cooperation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The United Nations, founded in San Francisco on 24 October 1945 to "save succeeding generations from the scourge of war," has 193 member states as of 2024. Its Charter sets out the purposes — maintaining international peace and security, developing friendly relations, achieving cooperation in solving economic, social, cultural, and humanitarian problems, and promoting respect for human rights. Six principal organs govern the system. The General Assembly is the plenary deliberative body where each state has one vote. The Security Council has primary responsibility for peace and security, with 5 permanent members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, the United States — each with a veto) and 10 elected members serving two-year terms. The International Court of Justice (the World Court, seated at The Hague) adjudicates disputes between states. The Secretariat is the UN's civil service, led by the Secretary-General (António Guterres since 2017). The Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) coordinates social and development work, and the now-rarely-active Trusteeship Council supervised the decolonisation process.

Specialised agencies, programmes, and funds — the WHO, UNESCO, UNICEF, UNDP, FAO, IAEA, ILO, IMF, World Bank — make up the broader UN family. Treaties and conventions adopted under UN auspices form much of modern international law, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), the Genocide Convention (1948), the Geneva Conventions on the laws of war (1949), the Refugee Convention (1951), the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), and the Paris Agreement (2015). The Security Council can authorise peacekeeping operations (currently 11 active missions including MINUSCA in CAR, MONUSCO in DRC, and UNFICYP in Cyprus) and impose sanctions or authorise force.

Critics argue that the Security Council veto paralyses the UN on issues where any of the P5 has a strategic interest — Syria, Ukraine, Gaza. Reform proposals include expanding permanent membership (a "G4" of Brazil, Germany, India, Japan) and limiting the veto in mass-atrocity cases. Despite limits, the UN remains the only universal forum, and its agencies routinely deliver vaccines, food aid, and disaster response on a scale no other organisation can match.

  • UN Charter — founding treaty, signed June 1945, entered into force October 1945.
  • P5 — five permanent Security Council members with veto power.
  • Peacekeeping operation — UN-mandated multinational force, usually with consent of host parties.
  • Specialized agency — autonomous organisation in the UN system (WHO, UNICEF, FAO, etc.).
  • General Assembly resolution — non-binding but politically significant.
  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) — 1948 UN-adopted statement of fundamental rights.

Worked example — Veto effect on resolutions

From 1990 to 2023, the Security Council voted on more than 1500 resolutions. Approximately 40 vetoes were cast — fewer than 3% of votes. Yet on specific issues (Syria 2011–present, Israel-Palestine, Ukraine 2014–present), vetoes have been more frequent. Why does a small absolute number of vetoes produce such a large geopolitical effect?

Vetoes are concentrated on the most consequential issues. A single veto on a Syria resolution prevents binding Chapter VII enforcement action — including sanctions, arms embargoes, or authorised force — for the duration of the conflict. The veto's power is thus structural rather than statistical: it preserves great-power latitude precisely where international action would be most consequential.

  • Confusing the UN with a world government — it is a forum of sovereign states.
  • Saying General Assembly resolutions are binding — they are not (Security Council Chapter VII resolutions are).
  • Treating peacekeeping as combat — most missions involve monitoring, mediation, and stabilisation rather than war-fighting.
  • Forgetting Canada's role: Lester B. Pearson's 1956 Suez proposal launched modern peacekeeping and earned the Nobel Peace Prize.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "explain the roles of the United Nations and other international organizations in maintaining international peace and cooperation."

🛡️ 3.4 NATO, Regional Alliances, and Collective Security
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Alongside the UN, regional security organisations shape the geography of cooperation. The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), founded on 4 April 1949 with the Washington Treaty, is a transatlantic military alliance built around Article 5: an armed attack on one member is considered an attack on all. Article 5 has been invoked once — by the United States after the 11 September 2001 attacks. NATO began with 12 members; following several rounds of enlargement (notably the 1999 and 2004 eastward expansions, then Finland in 2023 and Sweden in 2024), it now has 32 members. Canada is a founding member and contributes troops, ships, and command staff to NATO operations. The headquarters are in Brussels; the Supreme Allied Commander Europe (SACEUR) has always been an American officer.

Other regional security bodies include the African Union (AU), which has deployed peace operations in Somalia (AMISOM/ATMIS), Sudan, and the Central African Republic; the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE), which monitors ceasefires and elections; the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), which leans toward dialogue rather than enforcement; and the Arctic Council, which coordinates non-military cooperation among Arctic states (though its work has been disrupted since 2022 by Russia's war in Ukraine). The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine and the renewed Sino-American strategic competition have reactivated alliance thinking — including the AUKUS pact (Australia, the UK, and the US, 2021) and the Indo-Pacific Quad (United States, Japan, India, Australia).

Canada's foreign policy has long combined multilateralism (UN, NATO, the Commonwealth, La Francophonie, the G7, the G20, the Arctic Council) with bilateral relationships, especially with the United States. The country's traditional emphasis on middle-power diplomacy, mediation, and human security shapes its approach to global cooperation.

  • Article 5 — NATO's collective-defence clause.
  • Collective security — arrangement where all members defend any member under attack.
  • Multilateralism — diplomatic practice of coordinating through institutions including more than two states.
  • Middle power — state with moderate global influence (Canada, Australia, Norway, the Netherlands).
  • Arctic Council — intergovernmental forum of Arctic states and indigenous peoples.
  • NORAD — North American Aerospace Defense Command, a binational Canada–US command.

Worked example — Burden sharing in NATO

NATO's 2014 Wales Summit set a target that each member spend at least 2% of GDP on defence. In 2024 Canada spent approximately 1.37% of GDP on defence; the US spent 3.4%; Poland spent 4.1%. Canada's GDP is about $2.1 trillion. Compute the additional annual spending Canada would need to reach 2%.

Current defence spending = \(0.0137 \times 2\,100 \approx \$28.8\) billion. Target spending = \(0.02 \times 2\,100 = \$42\) billion. Additional spending required = \$42 − \$28.8 = \$13.2 billion/year (about \$340 per capita). Canada has committed to reaching 2% by 2032, with intervening procurement announcements (NORAD modernization, F-35 fleet, surface combatants).

  • Calling NATO an offensive alliance — its founding charter is defensive (Article 5 is reactive).
  • Saying NATO is purely military — it also includes civil emergency planning and partnerships.
  • Mixing up NATO and the EU — they overlap heavily but Ireland, Austria, Cyprus, Malta, and Sweden's pre-2024 status are/were EU but not NATO members.
  • Equating regional bodies with the UN — regional bodies have specific geographic scope.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "analyse the role of regional security organizations (NATO, AU, OSCE) in maintaining international peace."

🤝 3.5 Humanitarian Intervention and the Responsibility to Protect
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The principle of sovereignty established in the 1648 Peace of Westphalia gives each state authority over its territory and people. But what happens when a state itself commits mass atrocities against its own population? The 1994 Rwandan genocide, in which roughly 800 000 Tutsis and moderate Hutus were murdered in 100 days while UN peacekeepers stood by under a restrictive mandate, and the 1995 Srebrenica massacre of more than 8 000 Bosniak men and boys, drove a re-examination of when intervention is permissible. Canada led the response: in 2000–2001, the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS), chaired by Gareth Evans and Mohamed Sahnoun and co-sponsored by Canada, produced the report The Responsibility to Protect. The doctrine was adopted by all UN member states at the 2005 World Summit.

The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) rests on three pillars: (1) the state has the primary responsibility to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity; (2) the international community has a responsibility to assist states in meeting that responsibility; and (3) if a state is manifestly failing to protect its population, the international community has a responsibility to take timely and decisive action — through diplomatic, humanitarian, and, as a last resort, military means authorised by the Security Council. R2P was invoked in UN Security Council Resolution 1973 (2011), which authorised the use of "all necessary measures" to protect civilians in Libya. Critics argue that the NATO-led mission expanded beyond protection into regime change, contributing to the political backlash that has paralysed Security Council R2P action since (notably on Syria).

Other forms of humanitarian action operate outside the R2P doctrine. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), founded in Geneva in 1863, monitors compliance with the Geneva Conventions and offers neutral humanitarian assistance. Médecins Sans Frontières / Doctors Without Borders (MSF), founded 1971, provides medical care in conflict and disaster zones (Nobel Peace Prize 1999). The World Food Programme (WFP), the UN's frontline food-assistance agency (Nobel Peace Prize 2020), delivers food to about 150 million people annually.

  • Sovereignty — supreme authority of a state over its territory.
  • Responsibility to Protect (R2P) — 2005 UN-adopted doctrine on preventing mass atrocities.
  • Geneva Conventions — four treaties (1949) and additional protocols governing the laws of war.
  • International Criminal Court (ICC) — Hague-based court trying individuals for genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and aggression (Rome Statute, 1998).
  • Humanitarian space — physical and political conditions allowing aid workers to operate.
  • Neutrality vs solidarity — ICRC operates by strict neutrality; MSF by témoignage (bearing witness).

Worked example — Applying R2P criteria

In a hypothetical conflict, government forces have begun systematic attacks on a minority ethnic group, with credible reports of mass killings, deportations, and destruction of villages. The state has refused outside humanitarian access. How would each R2P pillar apply?

Pillar 1: the state has failed its primary responsibility. Pillar 2: the international community should attempt assistance — diplomatic pressure, sanctions, humanitarian corridors, ICRC access. Pillar 3, last resort: if pillars 1 and 2 fail and the situation meets the four crime thresholds, the Security Council may authorise force. Practical hurdles include obtaining P5 agreement (likely to face vetoes), defining the operation's scope to prevent mission creep, and securing follow-on stabilisation. R2P has been described as "easier to invoke than to implement."

  • Confusing R2P with a unilateral right to intervene — the doctrine requires UN authorisation for the use of force.
  • Treating R2P as covering all human-rights abuses — it applies specifically to four mass atrocity crimes.
  • Forgetting prevention — pillars 1 and 2 (prevention and assistance) are emphasised over pillar 3.
  • Equating humanitarian intervention with regime change.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "evaluate the effectiveness of humanitarian organizations in responding to crises."

🇨🇦 3.6 Canada's Role in Global Peace and Cooperation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada has built a distinctive foreign-policy identity around middle-power diplomacy: working through multilateral institutions, mediating between larger powers, and championing human security. The country was a founding member of the United Nations (1945) and NATO (1949), and Canadian diplomat Lester B. Pearson's proposal during the 1956 Suez Crisis to create the first armed UN peacekeeping force earned him the 1957 Nobel Peace Prize. Canada has since participated in nearly every major UN peacekeeping operation. Roméo Dallaire, the Canadian Force Commander of UNAMIR during the 1994 Rwandan genocide, became one of the strongest advocates for stronger mandates and earlier action; his memoir Shake Hands with the Devil influenced the development of R2P.

Other Canadian contributions include leading the campaign for the 1997 Ottawa Treaty banning anti-personnel landmines (signed by 122 countries) and the 1998 Rome Statute establishing the ICC; co-authoring the R2P doctrine through ICISS; serving on the Security Council seven times (most recently 1999–2000); and chairing the Arctic Council. Canada's humanitarian assistance budget is about $7 billion annually, much of it channelled through the UN, the Red Cross, and Canadian NGOs. The Canadian Armed Forces currently contribute to NATO operations including Operation Reassurance (Latvia battle group), Operation Unifier (training Ukrainian forces, suspended after February 2022 and resumed abroad), and NORAD aerospace defence.

Critics point out that Canada's peacekeeping commitment has declined sharply since the late 1990s — only a few dozen uniformed personnel in UN missions in 2024, compared with about 3 300 in 1993. Defence spending remains below the 2% NATO target. Indigenous-led foreign policy, climate diplomacy, and feminist international assistance are emerging themes of contemporary Canadian global engagement.

  • Middle power — state with moderate global influence and an emphasis on multilateral diplomacy.
  • Pearsonian peacekeeping — model of armed neutral force monitoring ceasefires.
  • Ottawa Treaty — 1997 anti-personnel landmines convention.
  • ICC (Rome Statute) — 1998 treaty establishing the International Criminal Court.
  • Feminist foreign policy — Canadian approach centring gender equality in development assistance and diplomacy (announced 2017).
  • NORAD — North American Aerospace Defense Command, founded 1958.

Worked example — Comparing peacekeeping contributions

In 1993 Canada contributed about 3 300 uniformed personnel to UN peace operations; in 2024 the figure is about 40. Total UN peacekeeping personnel worldwide is about 70 000. Calculate Canada's share of total peacekeeping personnel for each year. (Estimate 1993 total at 75 000.)

1993: \(\frac{3300}{75\,000} \approx 4.4\%\). 2024: \(\frac{40}{70\,000} \approx 0.06\%\). Canada's relative contribution has dropped by a factor of about 75. The pivot reflects shifts toward NATO operations, capacity-building (training rather than blue-helmet deployment), and the political costs of casualties in the Somalia, Bosnia, and Afghanistan missions.

  • Saying Canada is "still a peacekeeping nation" without checking current contributions.
  • Confusing peacekeeping with peace enforcement — different mandates, different rules of engagement.
  • Forgetting Canada's NATO and NORAD contributions when discussing global security.
  • Ignoring Indigenous and feminist dimensions of contemporary Canadian foreign policy.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "explain how Canada contributes to global peacekeeping, multilateral diplomacy, and the Responsibility to Protect."
