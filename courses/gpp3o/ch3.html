🏆 GPP3O: Unit 3 — Theories of Leadership

Trait theory, behavioural theory, situational leadership (Hersey–Blanchard), transformational vs transactional (Bass), servant leadership (Greenleaf), ethical leadership, cultural and gender perspectives

Unit 3: Theories of Leadership

Leadership is a researched field, not a vibe. This unit introduces the major theoretical frameworks for understanding leadership — what makes leaders effective, how style varies by situation, and how culture and ethics shape what leadership looks like in diverse Canadian contexts.

🧬 3.1 Trait Theory — "Great Person" and Its Limits
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The earliest serious theory of leadership, developed in the early 20th century, argued that leaders are born, not made. Thomas Carlyle's 1840 "Great Man" theory proposed that history was shaped by exceptional individuals — Napoleon, Caesar, Cromwell — whose intrinsic traits drove great events. Twentieth-century researchers like Ralph Stogdill (1948, 1974) tried to test the idea empirically by listing the personal traits that correlated with leadership emergence. Stogdill's surveys identified a cluster: intelligence, dominance, self-confidence, energy, task-relevant knowledge, sociability, and adjustment. More modern Big Five research finds that extraversion (especially the assertiveness facet), conscientiousness, and emotional stability are the personality traits most consistently associated with leadership emergence and effectiveness.

But trait theory has structural problems. First, the same traits that look like "leadership" in one context look like "obstructiveness" in another — a dominant person in a consensus-driven culture is a problem, not an asset. Second, traits predict leadership emergence (who gets picked) better than they predict leadership effectiveness (how well they do); these are different questions. Third, the lists of traits tend to mirror the demographics of the people doing the studying — early-20th-century lists privileged traits associated with white, male, North American norms. Fourth, traits are not the whole story — situations, followers, and skills matter enormously.

The fairest summary is: trait theory is partly right. Some traits do help. Conscientiousness, sociability, emotional intelligence, and intellectual curiosity make leadership easier. But traits are necessary, not sufficient. A trait-rich introvert can lead effectively; a trait-shy person who has built the skills can lead better than a charismatic-but-unprepared peer. The "great person" framing also obscures the collective work that leadership actually requires — followers, conditions, timing.

For peer leadership, the takeaway is twofold. Don't be intimidated if you don't fit the stereotype of a "born leader." Don't be complacent if you do. The most-cited recent meta-analysis (Day & Antonakis, 2012) finds that approximately 30% of leadership variance is associated with relatively stable traits and the remaining 70% with learnable behaviours, situational fit, and experience. That is a hopeful number — most of leadership is learnable. Ontario's GPP3O curriculum reflects this: the course is explicitly built on the premise that leadership can be developed.

  • "Great Man" theory — Carlyle's 1840 idea that history is shaped by exceptional individuals.
  • Trait theory — the view that leaders share consistent personal traits.
  • Big Five — personality framework: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism.
  • Leadership emergence vs effectiveness — who gets chosen vs who does it well.
  • Stogdill's surveys — empirical reviews of trait correlates of leadership.
  • Day & Antonakis estimate — ~30% of leadership variance is trait-stable; ~70% learnable.

Worked Example — Two Profiles, One Role

Candidates for student-council president. Candidate A: charismatic, comfortable with public speaking, dominant in group conversation, known to interrupt teachers occasionally, hasn't done much committee work. Candidate B: quieter, careful, methodical, has chaired the eco-club for two years and delivered three completed initiatives. Trait theory would predict Candidate A wins the election (and often, they do — emergence). Behavioural research would predict Candidate B is the more effective leader (effectiveness). A trait-aware council might design the role to use Candidate A as the public face and Candidate B as the working chair — splitting emergence and effectiveness across two roles. The trait analysis becomes a design tool, not just a diagnosis.

  • Believing that some people are "natural leaders" and others can never lead — the data don't support that.
  • Assuming that high extraversion equals high leadership — Susan Cain's Quiet (2012) showed introverted leaders often outperform when their teams are proactive.
  • Treating trait lists from the 1950s as universal — they reflect the demographics of the studies.
  • Confusing emergence and effectiveness.
  • Using "leadership traits" to exclude people who don't fit the dominant cultural script.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "describe trait theory and explain its limits as a complete account of leadership."

🎯 3.2 Behavioural Theory — Ohio State and Michigan Studies
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Disillusionment with trait theory pushed mid-century researchers toward behaviour. If we cannot predict effective leaders from who they are, perhaps we can identify them by what they do. Two parallel research programs — at Ohio State University (Carroll Shartle, Edwin Fleishman, in the 1940s–50s) and at the University of Michigan (Rensis Likert, in the same period) — independently identified two dimensions of leader behaviour. Initiating structure / task-oriented: behaviours focused on the work — defining roles, setting goals, scheduling, monitoring progress. Consideration / relationship-oriented: behaviours focused on the people — listening, showing respect, building trust, supporting individual concerns. The two dimensions are independent — a leader can be high on both, low on both, or high on one and low on the other.

Blake and Mouton's 1964 Managerial Grid built on these findings, plotting concern for production (x-axis) against concern for people (y-axis) on a 1–9 scale. Their preferred quadrant was 9,9 ("team management") — high on both. Stogdill's later meta-analyses generally found that consideration was associated with team satisfaction, and initiating structure was associated with team productivity; the best leaders typically registered moderate-to-high on both. The behavioural research was a major step forward because it located leadership in observable, learnable actions rather than fixed traits.

But behavioural theory has its own limits. The same behaviour reads differently in different contexts. A "high initiating structure" leader is welcome on a confused team that needs direction; the same leader is suffocating on an expert team that needs autonomy. The next wave of theory — situational leadership, which we'll meet in Lesson 3.3 — emerged specifically to address this context-sensitivity. Behavioural theory was a necessary stepping-stone, not a complete account.

For peer leaders, behavioural theory gives two concrete tools. First, the two-dimensional model is a self-diagnostic: are you a high-task / low-relationship leader (you get things done but people feel run-over)? A high-relationship / low-task leader (people love you but the project drifts)? Both have something to add. Second, the model is a coaching tool: when a teammate's leadership isn't working, you can often diagnose what's missing — usually one of the two dimensions — and suggest a specific shift. The Ohio-Michigan research is sixty years old; the diagnostic is still useful.

  • Initiating structure — task-focused leader behaviours.
  • Consideration — relationship-focused leader behaviours.
  • Ohio State studies — Shartle, Fleishman, 1940s–50s, identified the two-dimension model.
  • Michigan studies — Likert and colleagues; parallel findings on task vs employee orientation.
  • Managerial Grid — Blake and Mouton's 1964 9×9 model.
  • Two-dimensional independence — a leader can be high or low on either dimension, in any combination.

Worked Example — Diagnosing a Struggling Club President

Maria has been president of the school's debate club for three months. The team likes her — she remembers birthdays, listens carefully, hosts pizza nights. But three tournaments in a row have gone poorly: paperwork submitted late, practice sessions unfocused, two members dropped out citing "no direction." Behavioural diagnosis: high consideration, low initiating structure. She is excellent on the relationship axis and weak on the task axis. The fix is not to become less considerate — that's a strength — but to add structure: weekly written agendas, deadline calendar, one-page tournament-prep checklist, clear practice goals. The goal is a 9,9 leader, not a 1,9 leader who has flipped to 9,1. Behavioural theory gives Maria the vocabulary for her own growth: "I need to lead the work, not just love the people."

  • Assuming the two dimensions are inverse — they're independent.
  • Treating "high task" as masculine and "high relationship" as feminine — both are leadership behaviours available to everyone.
  • Forgetting that context matters — the right balance shifts with the team and the situation.
  • Diagnosing without acting — the model is for choosing behaviour, not just labelling it.
  • Skipping the behavioural axis and going straight to vibes ("they have leadership presence").

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "describe behavioural theories and connect them to a school example."

🔀 3.3 Situational Leadership — Hersey & Blanchard
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard's situational leadership model (originally 1969, refined into "Situational Leadership II" by Blanchard in the 1980s) is one of the most-taught leadership frameworks in the world — and one of the most useful for a peer leader working with team members at different levels of skill and motivation. The core idea: there is no single best leadership style; the right style depends on the developmental level of the follower for a specific task.

Hersey-Blanchard names four developmental levels (D1 through D4) and four matching leadership styles (S1 through S4). D1 — Enthusiastic Beginner: high commitment, low competence. New to the task, eager but unskilled. Needs S1 — Directing: high task, low relationship behaviour — clear instructions, close supervision, "do it this way." D2 — Disillusioned Learner: low commitment, some competence. Has started but hit the wall; commitment dropping. Needs S2 — Coaching: high task, high relationship — direction plus emotional support and rationale. D3 — Capable but Cautious Performer: variable commitment, high competence. Knows how, but lacks confidence or motivation. Needs S3 — Supporting: low task, high relationship — listen, encourage, share decision-making. D4 — Self-Reliant Achiever: high commitment, high competence. Owns the work. Needs S4 — Delegating: low task, low relationship — hand over, get out of the way, be available if needed.

The model is task-specific, not person-specific. The same teammate can be D4 at one task (chairing meetings) and D1 at another (writing a grant application). Effective leaders flex their style by task and by person. The mistakes are predictable: leaders default to one style regardless of follower readiness. A leader stuck on Directing micromanages D4 performers, who disengage. A leader stuck on Delegating abandons D1 beginners, who flounder. A leader stuck on Coaching over-explains to D3 performers, who feel patronised. Style flex is the skill.

For a Grade-11 peer leader in a club, council, or initiative, situational leadership is the single most practical framework. You will lead teammates at different developmental levels for different parts of the work. The grade-9 volunteer who has never run a bake-sale stall is D1 and needs direction. The grade-12 returner who knows the routine is D4 and needs space. The grade-10 who has tried it once and is now anxious about doing it alone is D2 and needs both instruction and reassurance. Reading the developmental level and adjusting your style accordingly is the discipline of situational leadership.

  • Situational Leadership — Hersey-Blanchard's model that style should match the follower's task-specific developmental level.
  • Developmental Levels D1–D4 — Enthusiastic Beginner, Disillusioned Learner, Capable but Cautious Performer, Self-Reliant Achiever.
  • Styles S1–S4 — Directing, Coaching, Supporting, Delegating.
  • Task-specificity — a person's developmental level varies by task, not by personality.
  • Style flex — adjusting leadership style across people and across tasks.
  • Default-style trap — leading everyone the same way regardless of their readiness.

Worked Example — Flexing Across a Three-Person Team

You are leading a fundraising bake sale. Three teammates: Liam (Grade 9, first time volunteering), Aanya (Grade 11, ran the booth last year), Noah (Grade 12, was treasurer of student council). Liam is D1 for almost everything — give him a specific role with written instructions ("count change like this, recount at the end of each hour"). Aanya is D3 for the booth ("I know how, but I'm nervous to lead it alone again") — sit with her for the first 30 minutes, ask "what's your plan?", be available, then step back. Noah is D4 for handling the cash float — give him the role and don't hover. Same event, three different styles. The peer leader who applies a single style to all three has wasted the experience of one teammate and starved another.

  • Calling someone "a D2" — the level is task-specific, not personal.
  • Defaulting to one style — usually directing (you feel productive) or delegating (you feel cool).
  • Confusing supporting with abandoning — supporting is high relationship, present but not directive.
  • Misreading the level — a quiet, cautious D4 can look like a D2 if you only watch surface behaviour.
  • Forgetting to re-read the level when the task changes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "explain situational leadership and apply it to a group with members at different developmental levels."

⚡ 3.4 Transactional vs Transformational Leadership — Burns and Bass
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

James MacGregor Burns introduced the distinction between transactional and transformational leadership in his 1978 book Leadership. Bernard Bass extended and operationalised the framework in the 1980s and 1990s through the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ), making it the most-tested leadership model in academic research. The distinction is foundational for understanding what kind of leader you are choosing to be.

Transactional leadership is exchange-based. The leader offers rewards (recognition, grades, points, money, promotions) and applies consequences (reprimands, demotion, withdrawal of privileges) to shape follower behaviour. The framework includes contingent reward (clear "do X and get Y" exchanges) and management-by-exception (the leader intervenes only when problems arise, either actively by monitoring for deviations or passively by waiting until problems are reported). Transactional leadership is necessary in any organisation — payroll, deadlines, performance reviews, school marks are all transactional structures. Done well, it is fair and clear. Done poorly, it becomes carrot-and-stick manipulation that erodes intrinsic motivation.

Transformational leadership is identification-based. The leader offers a vision and helps followers see how their work connects to it, building motivation that comes from the work itself rather than from external rewards. Bass identified four components — the "Four I's." Idealised influence: the leader models the values they ask of others, becoming a person whose example matters. Inspirational motivation: the leader articulates a compelling vision in language followers find meaningful. Intellectual stimulation: the leader invites questioning, new ideas, and creative problem-solving. Individualised consideration: the leader treats each follower as a unique person with specific needs, strengths, and growth paths. Done well, transformational leadership creates high commitment and high performance. Done poorly, it slides into cult-of-personality charisma without substance, or into burnout when "vision" becomes pressure without support.

The two styles are not opposites. They are different layers. A strong leader is competently transactional (people are paid fairly, deadlines are clear, marks are honest) and transformational (the work has meaning, growth is invited, the team feels like more than a transaction). Pure transactional leadership produces compliance without ownership; pure transformational leadership without operational reliability produces inspiration without delivery. Canadian examples of transformational leaders are often invoked — Terry Fox, Roberta Bondar, Cindy Blackstock, Wab Kinew — but the honest reading is that each combined inspirational vision with concrete operational follow-through.

  • Transactional leadership — exchange-based; rewards and consequences shape behaviour.
  • Contingent reward — "do X, get Y" exchange.
  • Management-by-exception — leader intervenes only at deviations (active or passive).
  • Transformational leadership — identification-based; vision and meaning motivate.
  • The Four I's — idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration (Bass).
  • Full Range Leadership Model — Bass-Avolio combination of transactional and transformational components.

Worked Example — Designing a School Recycling Initiative

You are launching a recycling campaign. Pure transactional design: track classroom participation, offer a pizza party for the top class each month, post a leaderboard. Compliance will be high during the campaign and drop after. Pure transformational design: hold a town-hall where the school's per-capita waste is mapped against climate-impact data; invite students to redesign cafeteria sourcing; tell the story of a previous Ontario student-led waste reduction that influenced district policy. Engagement will be high among the inspired and patchy among the unengaged. Combined design: open with the transformational frame (why this matters, what we can do, how this connects to district goals), build the transactional structure (a clear monthly target with shared celebration), and individualise (each grade has a specific role; the leadership team checks in with each grade's champion monthly). Combined designs perform best in field studies.

  • Treating transactional as "bad" and transformational as "good" — they're complementary.
  • Doing transformational vision without transactional follow-through — the team burns out.
  • Confusing charisma with transformational leadership — charisma without substance is just persuasive marketing.
  • Ignoring individualised consideration — the most-frequently-skipped of the Four I's.
  • Imagining that you can be one without the other — the Full Range Model is the operational target.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.4 — "distinguish transactional and transformational leadership and identify Canadian examples of each."

🛠️ 3.5 Servant Leadership and Ethical Leadership
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Robert K. Greenleaf, a former AT&T management researcher, introduced "servant leadership" in his 1970 essay The Servant as Leader. The opening question is striking: "The servant-leader is servant first. … It begins with the natural feeling that one wants to serve, to serve first. Then conscious choice brings one to aspire to lead." The framework inverts the usual ordering — leadership is what arises from serving, not what justifies being served. Larry Spears's later articulation names ten characteristics: listening, empathy, healing, awareness, persuasion, conceptualisation, foresight, stewardship, commitment to the growth of people, and building community.

The framework resonates particularly well with peer-support work. A peer-helper's primary orientation is to the well-being of the peer, not to the helper's status. A peer-leader who chairs a club is in service of the club's purpose and members, not the other way around. Greenleaf's classic test question: "Do those served grow as persons? Do they, while being served, become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous, more likely themselves to become servants?" If the answer is yes, the leadership is well-directed.

Servant leadership has empirical support. A 2014 meta-analysis (Liden, Wayne, Liao, Meuser) found servant-leadership behaviours predicted follower well-being, organisational citizenship behaviour, and job performance. The strongest effects were among teams where psychological safety and trust were already established — servant leaders deepen these conditions further. The weakness is structural: in highly competitive or short-term contexts, servant leadership can be exploited or misread as weakness. The framework works best when followers can trust that the orientation is genuine and sustained.

Ethical leadership is the related and adjacent topic. Mike Brown, Linda Treviño, and David Harrison defined ethical leadership in 2005 as "the demonstration of normatively appropriate conduct through personal actions and interpersonal relationships, and the promotion of such conduct to followers through two-way communication, reinforcement, and decision-making." In simpler terms: the ethical leader is one whose behaviour models the standard, communicates the standard, and reinforces the standard when others act. Ethical leaders avoid four traps — conflicts of interest, abuse of position, coercion, and dishonesty. Each has a peer-leadership cognate. Conflict of interest: chairing a panel that judges your friend's submission. Abuse of position: using your peer-helper role to gain social information. Coercion: pressuring a teammate to vote your way. Dishonesty: telling the team one thing in private and another in public. The ethical leader's job is to notice these temptations and refuse them, visibly, in the small daily decisions.

  • Servant leadership — Greenleaf's frame: leadership grows out of service.
  • Spears's ten characteristics — listening, empathy, healing, awareness, persuasion, conceptualisation, foresight, stewardship, growth, community.
  • Greenleaf's test question — Do those served grow as persons?
  • Ethical leadership — Brown, Treviño, Harrison 2005 definition.
  • Four ethical traps — conflict of interest, abuse of position, coercion, dishonesty.
  • Psychological safety — Amy Edmondson's term for the team condition that lets people speak up without fear; deepened by servant leaders.

Worked Example — A Servant-Leader Decision

You are the elected co-chair of the school's Mental Health Awareness committee. The board offers you a slot at a public city event — an opportunity for personal visibility. A junior committee member, who has done most of the on-the-ground work and is from a community that is rarely visible in such events, has not been offered the slot. A servant-leadership reading: ask the board whether the junior member can take the slot, or whether the slot can be a co-presentation. A status-leadership reading: take the slot, frame it in terms of "I represent the committee." Both responses are defensible; the servant-leadership response prioritises the growth of the served (the junior member) over the recognition of the leader. The Greenleaf test ("do those served grow as persons?") would favour the first response. Note that this is not self-denial; it is a deliberate choice about whose growth the leader is here to support.

  • Confusing servant leadership with "say yes to everything" — it is service to a purpose, not to every demand.
  • Using servant-leadership language while actually consolidating personal status — the test is behaviour over time.
  • Ethical leadership only in public — the small daily choices are where it actually lives.
  • Treating "ethical" and "legal" as synonyms — many legal actions are unethical and vice versa.
  • Forgetting that servant and ethical leadership are complementary to (not replacements for) competent operational leadership.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.5 — "describe servant leadership and ethical leadership and evaluate their applicability to peer-helper roles."

🌍 3.6 Leadership in Diverse Canadian Contexts
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The leadership theories of this unit emerged largely from twentieth-century North American and European corporate, military, and academic contexts. They are useful — but they are not the only voices. Canadian leadership lives at the intersection of many traditions: Indigenous teachings, Black community organising, immigrant networks, queer and trans community building, disability-rights advocacy, women's movements across several waves, and the public-policy traditions of federal and provincial governance. A complete picture of leadership requires engagement with these traditions, not as supplements but as primary sources.

Indigenous leadership. Many Indigenous nations articulate leadership through teachings older than Confederation. The Seven Grandfather Teachings from Anishinaabe traditions — wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility, truth — describe the disposition of a person fit to lead. Decision-making in many Indigenous communities follows circle or consensus models rather than majority vote: a leader is heard among many voices, not above them. The role of elders, the importance of the seven-generations principle (decisions made with consideration for the impact seven generations ahead), and the relational ethic of leadership — leadership as accountability to community, land, and ancestors — are foundational. Truth and Reconciliation Call to Action 62 (2015) calls on Canadian K-12 systems to incorporate Indigenous teaching on residential schools, treaties, and Indigenous contributions; values education in GPP3O is one direct site of that work.

Gender and leadership. The double-bind for women in leadership (warm OR competent, but rarely allowed both at once) is well-documented in Alice Eagly's research; intersectional analyses (Kimberlé Crenshaw and others) show the additional pressures on racialised women, Indigenous women, queer women, and disabled women. Canadian leaders often cited in GPP3O include Kim Campbell, the first female Prime Minister (briefly, 1993); Jody Wilson-Raybould, former Minister of Justice; Adrienne Clarkson and Michaëlle Jean, former Governors-General; Roberta Bondar, astronaut; Cindy Blackstock, advocate for Indigenous children's rights; Tanya Talaga, journalist and author. The pattern is not exceptionalism; each navigated structural constraints that male peers did not, and each modified the role they entered.

Black, immigrant, and queer leadership. Lincoln Alexander, the first Black Member of Parliament (1968) and Ontario Lieutenant Governor (1985–91); Jean Augustine, the first Black woman MP (1993), who introduced the motion for Black History Month; Senator Anne Cools, Senator Bernadette Clement; community organisers like Itah Sadu and Rinaldo Walcott; queer Canadian leaders like Olivia Chow, Svend Robinson (the first openly gay MP, 1988), and El Jones. The Trans Day of Remembrance, founded by Gwendolyn Ann Smith in 1999, has been carried in Canadian communities by trans leaders including Susan Gapka and Anwar Knight. Each of these leaders worked from a community context, often in coalition; their leadership was relational and movement-based as much as positional.

For a Grade-11 peer leader, the takeaway is not a list of names to memorise. It is a recognition that leadership in Canada has many traditions, that the "neutral" theories of this unit are not actually neutral but situated, and that your own leadership will draw from the traditions you have lived inside and the traditions you have learned from. A leader who engages with multiple traditions has a richer toolkit than one who has only learned the Bass-Greenleaf-Blanchard lineage.

  • Seven Grandfather Teachings — Anishinaabe leadership values: wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility, truth.
  • Seven-generations principle — decisions made with consideration for impacts seven generations ahead.
  • Calls to Action (TRC) — 2015 recommendations including for K-12 education.
  • Double-bind — the warm/competent dilemma for women leaders (Eagly).
  • Intersectionality — Crenshaw's framework for understanding overlapping systems of advantage and disadvantage.
  • Movement-based leadership — leadership exercised through coalition rather than position alone.

Worked Example — Building a Leadership Reading List

For Unit 3's research task, you choose three Canadian leaders to compare. A weak choice: three white male prime ministers from different parties. A stronger choice that better honours the curriculum's equity commitments: Tommy Douglas (CCF/NDP, Medicare); Cindy Blackstock (First Nations Child and Family Caring Society, won a 2016 Canadian Human Rights Tribunal ruling against the federal government for discrimination in child welfare funding); and Roberta Jamieson (Mohawk lawyer, first Indigenous woman to graduate from a Canadian law school, longtime president of Indspire). Each represents a different leadership tradition — democratic-socialist legislative organising, legal advocacy through the courts, philanthropic-educational community work. Comparing them through trait theory alone misses what each accomplished; the analysis becomes much richer when situational, transformational, servant, and Indigenous-relational lenses are used together.

  • Treating the canonical theories as universal — they are situated, and other traditions exist.
  • Tokenising Indigenous, Black, or women leaders by adding them to a list without engaging the traditions they came from.
  • Ignoring movement-based leadership in favour of only positional leadership.
  • Forgetting Truth and Reconciliation Calls to Action when designing leadership curriculum.
  • Confusing diverse representation with diverse frameworks — both matter, but they are different moves.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "describe ways in which cultural context shapes the practice of leadership."
