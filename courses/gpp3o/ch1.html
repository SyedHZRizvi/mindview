Unit 1: Personal Awareness and Self-Management

Leadership begins inside. Before you can lead a group or support a peer, you need a working knowledge of your own strengths, blind spots, emotional patterns, and habits. This unit builds the self-knowledge foundation for everything else in GPP3O.

🪞 1.1 Self-Awareness: Knowing Who Shows Up
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Self-awareness is the foundation skill of leadership and peer support. The American Psychological Association defines it as "the capacity to introspect about one's own mental processes, motivations, and behaviours, and to recognise oneself as distinct from the surrounding environment." In plain terms: knowing what is going on inside you, and being able to describe it accurately to yourself. Tasha Eurich's research distinguishes two kinds — internal self-awareness (clarity about your own values, passions, and impact) and external self-awareness (clarity about how others see you). Most people overestimate both. Only about 10–15% of adults Eurich studied actually had what her research team would call meta-cognitive accuracy.

For a peer leader, the practical question is: which of my behaviours land the way I think they do? You may believe you are encouraging when in fact you are interrupting; you may believe you are calm when in fact you are visibly tense. Closing the gap takes deliberate practice. Useful tools include reflective journaling (a 5-minute end-of-day write: what went well, what didn't, what did I notice about myself?), structured peer feedback ("name one strength you saw and one thing I could try differently"), and observed practice (recording a role-play and watching it back). Each of these is a way of getting external evidence that you can hold against your internal self-image.

Ontario's Creating Pathways to Success (2013) builds reflection directly into the K-12 Individual Pathways Plan (IPP). Students are expected to revisit their self-knowledge each year and update it. GPP3O is the course where this self-knowledge becomes the working capital for leadership and helping decisions. Avoid two failure modes: (1) over-confident self-reading — refusing to update your self-picture when contradictory evidence appears — and (2) chronic self-doubt — never trusting any internal signal. Both are forms of disconnection. The goal is calibrated self-knowledge: confident where evidence supports confidence, curious where evidence is mixed.

Self-awareness is also relational, not purely individual. Your cultural background, family dynamics, racialised experience, gender expression, and neurotype all shape what you notice and what you miss. A student raised in a household where emotions were openly named will arrive in this course with vocabulary for inner states that a peer from a household with stricter emotional reserve may not. Neither is "more self-aware" in a moral sense — they each have learning to do, and the learning is different. A peer-helper trains themselves to ask, not assume.

  • Self-awareness — the capacity to notice and accurately describe one's own internal states and behaviours.
  • Internal self-awareness — clarity about your own values, passions, motivations, and impact (Eurich).
  • External self-awareness — clarity about how others see you.
  • Reflective journal — a daily or weekly written record of experience and inner observation.
  • Calibrated self-knowledge — self-belief that matches the available evidence; neither over-confident nor over-doubting.
  • Individual Pathways Plan (IPP) — Ontario's K-12 reflective career-and-life-planning portfolio.

Worked Example — Calibrating an Internal Self-Picture

Devon, a Grade 11 student, thinks of himself as "a quiet listener." After volunteering to facilitate a Grade 9 peer-mentoring session, he records the session (with permission) and watches the playback. He counts: he speaks for 70% of the airtime, interrupts twice, and asks one question. His internal self-picture (quiet, listener) does not match the external evidence. He shares this discovery with his GPP3O teacher in a brief conference. The teacher does not shame the gap — she names it as ordinary and asks: "What was driving the talking?" Devon notices: he was anxious that silence meant failure. They identify a target — for the next session, his goal is "ask three questions before offering any opinion, and let any silence run for at least six seconds." That is what calibrated self-knowledge looks like in motion: notice the gap, name what drove it, set a small testable target, try again.

  • Treating self-awareness as a personality trait you either have or don't — it is a learnable skill.
  • Refusing to record yourself because the playback feels uncomfortable — discomfort is the point.
  • Listening to peer feedback only when it agrees with your self-picture.
  • Using "I'm just an introvert / extrovert" as a stop-question instead of a starting point.
  • Confusing self-awareness with self-criticism — the goal is accurate description, not judgment.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "use a variety of self-assessment tools and processes to identify their personal characteristics relevant to leadership and peer support."

💗 1.2 Emotional Intelligence — Goleman's Five Domains
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Emotional intelligence (EI or EQ) is the ability to recognise emotions in yourself and others and to use that information to guide thought and behaviour. The construct was popularised by Daniel Goleman's 1995 book Emotional Intelligence, which synthesised earlier work by Peter Salovey and John Mayer. Goleman names five domains: self-awareness (recognising your own emotions), self-regulation (managing them), motivation (using emotion to pursue goals), empathy (recognising others' emotions), and social skills (managing relationships). The first three are intrapersonal; the last two are interpersonal. A useful mental model is that intrapersonal skills are the input, and interpersonal skills are the output — without the inner work, the outer work is fragile.

EI is not the same as being "nice" or "happy." A self-regulated person can be angry — they just choose when and how they express that anger. They notice the rising heat, name it ("I am getting frustrated because this conversation has gone in circles for ten minutes"), and choose a response that fits the situation. The opposite is what Goleman calls "amygdala hijack" — the moment when the limbic system overrides the prefrontal cortex and you do something you would never have chosen with five seconds of distance. Most regrettable workplace and friendship messages are amygdala hijacks. EI training is partly about building the small gap between trigger and response.

EI research is empirically supported but contested. Critics including Jordan Peterson and Adam Grant have noted that some EI assessments measure little beyond agreeableness and Big-Five conscientiousness. The honest summary: parts of EI are well-validated (especially Salovey-Mayer's ability-based assessments), other parts are more popular than scientific. For our purposes, Goleman's five domains are a useful vocabulary for a peer-leader — they give you words for inner states and a framework for skill-building, regardless of whether "EQ" is one number or twenty.

The most important EI skill for a Grade-11 peer-leader is the gap between feeling and action — what Viktor Frankl called the space between stimulus and response. A simple practice: when you notice an emotion rising, name it in your head ("I am embarrassed"; "I am defensive"; "I am hurt"). Naming an emotion deactivates some of its automatic response (the affect-labelling research from Matthew Lieberman's UCLA lab shows reduced amygdala activation when subjects name what they feel). This is one reason peer-mediation training spends so much time on emotion-naming language — it is a tool for the helper, but it is also a tool you are modelling for the people you are helping.

  • Emotional intelligence (EI/EQ) — the ability to recognise and use emotions in yourself and others.
  • Self-regulation — managing your own emotional response in service of a goal or value.
  • Empathy — recognising and resonating with another person's emotional state.
  • Amygdala hijack — the moment when limbic-system reaction overrides reasoned choice (Goleman).
  • Affect labelling — naming an emotion to reduce its automatic intensity.
  • Goleman's five domains — self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills.

Worked Example — Building the Gap Between Trigger and Response

Aisha, a peer mediator, is in a session where one student calls another "lazy." She feels a sudden flash of anger — that word lands hard for her because of family history. Without EI training, she would interrupt, defend the second student, and lose her neutral mediator role. With EI training, she silently names what just happened ("flash of anger, a personal trigger, not about this conversation"), takes a slow exhale (parasympathetic re-engagement), and then asks a structured question: "When you used the word 'lazy', can you describe the specific behaviour you saw?" The technique converts the anger from an interruption into a steering force — she is more attentive because she was triggered, but she is still mediating. The gap she built between trigger and response is roughly two seconds. With practice, it stretches. This is the daily work of EI: not feeling less, but choosing more.

  • Treating EI as a fixed score on a quiz — it is a set of learnable skills.
  • Confusing EI with suppressing emotion — self-regulation includes choosing when and how to express.
  • Calling empathy "feeling what someone else feels" — accurate empathy is recognising it, not identical feeling.
  • Pretending to be calm when you are not — peers usually see the gap.
  • Skipping the inner work and only practising the outer manners.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — "explain the components of emotional intelligence and assess their own profile."

🧠 1.3 Learning Styles and Strengths
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Learning-style models give us vocabulary for talking about how people prefer to take in and process information. The most common in Canadian classrooms is VARK (Visual, Auditory, Reading/Writing, Kinesthetic), developed by Neil Fleming in 1987. A visual learner prefers diagrams, charts, and visual organisers; an auditory learner prefers explanation, discussion, and podcasts; a reading/writing learner prefers text and notes; a kinesthetic learner prefers hands-on practice, role-play, and movement. Most people are mixed-modal, not pure. Howard Gardner's multiple intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic, existential) adds another layer — Gardner argues that intelligence is plural rather than a single g-factor.

An honest note: learning-style theory has been criticised by cognitive scientists (notably Daniel Willingham at the University of Virginia). The strong version — "teach a visual learner only visually and they will perform better" — has not been replicated in rigorous studies. The weak version — "different content is best presented in different formats; people have preferences that influence engagement but not necessarily achievement" — is well-supported. For a peer-leader, the weak version is enough. You will work with peers who prefer different modes, and a session that uses only one mode will lose people. Mixed-mode delivery (a sketch + an explanation + a hands-on try + a written summary) reaches more people than monomode.

Strength-spotting is the second tool of this lesson. The CliftonStrengths (formerly StrengthsFinder) framework identifies 34 talent themes; the StrengthsExplorer (its student version) names ten themes for ages 10–14. A simpler classroom alternative is Martin Seligman's VIA Character Strengths Survey, which yields a 24-strength profile organised into six virtue clusters (wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence). Whichever instrument you use, the practice is the same: identify your top five, look for patterns (do they cluster around relationships? thinking? action?), and ask yourself: "When do I feel most alive at school?" The answer usually points at a strength being exercised.

Leadership and peer support both depend on knowing your strengths and being able to invite the strengths of others. A peer-helper conversation can be reframed from "what is wrong with you?" to "what is working for you, and how do we build on that?" — a small but powerful pivot. The Ministry of Education encourages strength-based approaches throughout the IPP and the GCE curriculum. The goal is not to ignore challenges but to build from a foundation that the peer recognises in themselves.

  • VARK — Visual, Auditory, Reading/Writing, Kinesthetic learning preferences (Fleming, 1987).
  • Multiple intelligences — Gardner's plural model of intelligence (1983, 1999).
  • CliftonStrengths / StrengthsFinder — Gallup's 34-talent-theme framework.
  • VIA Character Strengths — Seligman's 24-strength survey rooted in positive psychology.
  • Strength-spotting — the practice of identifying what is working in self and others as a basis for growth.
  • Mixed-mode delivery — presenting content in several formats to reach more learners.

Worked Example — Designing a Tutoring Session Across Modes

You are peer-tutoring a Grade 10 student in linear equations. A single-mode delivery would be: "let me explain how to solve for x" (auditory only). A mixed-mode delivery: (1) show a diagram of a balance scale — what you do to one side, you do to the other (visual/kinesthetic via metaphor); (2) talk through one example aloud while pointing at each step (auditory + visual); (3) write the steps as a numbered list the student can keep (reading/writing); (4) hand them a problem to solve while you watch silently, then debrief (kinesthetic + metacognitive). The total time is similar to a single-mode session, but more of the student is engaged. You are not "teaching to a learning style" — you are teaching to the topic, with the recognition that humans are mixed-modal.

  • Treating VARK as a fixed identity rather than a preference profile.
  • Believing the strong learning-styles claim (it does not survive replication).
  • Identifying your strengths but never deploying them in actual leadership choices.
  • Skipping the weakness-mapping step — strengths and growth areas are both data.
  • Designing a session in one mode because that is your own preferred mode.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "use a variety of self-assessment tools to identify their personal characteristics, including learning preferences and strengths."

🧭 1.4 Values Clarification and Personal Mission
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A value is something you treat as important enough to choose for, even when it is costly. Values are not what we say we believe — they are what our calendar, our wallet, and our attention reveal. The classic exercise is a values card-sort: present 50 to 80 values (achievement, autonomy, balance, creativity, faith, family, fairness, generosity, growth, honesty, learning, loyalty, security, service, status, etc.); narrow to twenty; then to ten; then to your top five. The narrowing is the work — you cannot keep everything. A useful follow-up question is "When have I chosen against an easier path because of this value?" The values that show up in actual past choices are real; the ones that don't may be aspirational.

Values clarification matters for leadership and peer support for two reasons. First, leaders make decisions that involve trade-offs (who gets the spot on the team? how do we spend the council budget? what do I say to a friend who is doing something I think is wrong?). Without an articulated value-set, those decisions feel arbitrary or politically expedient. With one, you can explain to your team — and to yourself — why you chose the way you did. Second, peer-support conversations almost always reach the question "what is most important to you here?" If you do not know your own answer to that question, you will be unable to model what an answer can look like.

A personal mission statement is a one- or two-sentence articulation of "what I am here to do." Stephen Covey popularised the form in The Seven Habits of Highly Effective People (1989). The Ontario Skills Passport encourages a similar reflective exercise in the IPP. A good mission statement is concrete enough to test against (is this proposed activity in or out of mission?) and value-anchored (it names what you would not compromise). Bad mission statements are vague slogans ("be the best me I can be"). Good ones are specific ("to help younger students find a path through high school that fits them — with honesty, patience, and a sense of humour").

Indigenous frameworks of values are equally important to engage. The Anishinaabe Seven Grandfather Teachings (wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility, truth) and many other Indigenous teachings offer relational, place-based ways of articulating values that complement individualistic Western frames. The Ministry of Education's curriculum specifically encourages incorporation of Indigenous knowledge and perspectives. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 62 calls on schools to include curriculum on residential schools, treaties, and Indigenous contributions; values education in GPP3O is one place where this commitment becomes concrete.

  • Value — something one treats as important enough to choose for, even at cost.
  • Values card-sort — a structured exercise to narrow a values list to a top five.
  • Personal mission statement — a one- or two-sentence articulation of one's central purpose.
  • Aspirational value — a value one wishes to hold but cannot point to lived evidence of choosing for.
  • Seven Grandfather Teachings — Anishinaabe values framework: wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility, truth.
  • Calls to Action (TRC) — Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 2015 recommendations, including for K-12 education.

Worked Example — From Card-Sort to Decision

Jamal sorts a deck of 60 values and lands on a top five: family, growth, honesty, service, balance. He notices "growth" and "balance" can pull in opposite directions; he names that as a real tension, not a contradiction. The next week, he is offered a spot on the volleyball team that will require six-day-a-week training. He runs the offer through his value set: growth says yes (technical and physical growth); service says ambiguous (it depends whether he sees this as service to teammates); family and balance say warning (he is the eldest of three siblings whose parents both work shifts; he is needed at home). He decides to negotiate: he will join the team but ask the coach for permission to miss one weekly training to be home Tuesday evenings, and he will commit to one shorter training session at the school weight-room on his own. That is values-clarified decision-making — not a refusal of opportunity, not an unconditional yes, but a structured negotiation guided by what he has decided matters.

  • Listing values you wish you had instead of values your behaviour actually shows.
  • Letting parents, peers, or social-media culture dictate your value set without examining it.
  • Confusing values with rules — values are directional, rules are situational.
  • Writing a personal mission statement that is too vague to use as a decision filter.
  • Treating values as fixed at 16; they often refine through life, especially after major transitions.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "describe their personal strengths, values, and areas for growth as a leader and helper."

🌱 1.5 Resilience and Stress Management
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Resilience is not the absence of stress; it is the capacity to absorb stress, recover, and learn from it. Researchers including Ann Masten describe resilience as "ordinary magic" — most people develop it through the regular use of available supports (relationships, routines, meaning) rather than through extraordinary individual heroics. For peer leaders this is good news: resilience is built, not born. The factors that build it are well-mapped — what the American Psychological Association calls the "4 Cs" of psychological resilience: connection (relationships with trusted people), competence (skills and the experience of mastery), character (values and a sense of meaning), and contribution (a role that matters to others).

Stress is the body's response to a demand. Hans Selye's classic distinction is between eustress (productive stress that mobilises performance — a deadline, a game, a public-speaking moment) and distress (excessive or chronic stress that depletes). Both produce similar physiological signals (elevated cortisol and adrenaline, raised heart rate, narrowed attention); the difference is duration, intensity, and the person's appraisal of whether they can meet the demand. Kelly McGonigal's research at Stanford shows that even the belief about stress matters — students who believe stress can be useful show better cognitive performance under demand than students who believe stress is purely harmful.

Stress-management techniques fall into four categories. Physical: sleep (7–9 hours for adolescents; consistency matters more than total), exercise (any aerobic activity, three to five times a week), nutrition (regular meals, hydration). Cognitive: reframing ("this stress is my body getting ready to perform"), cognitive-behavioural strategies (catching catastrophising thoughts), values-based prioritisation. Relational: talking with a trusted person, asking for help, being part of a community. Restorative: mindfulness, prayer, time in nature, art, music, journaling. The combination matters more than any single technique. A peer-leader supports a stressed peer by helping them identify which categories they are currently neglecting.

Two boundaries matter for a Grade-11 peer-helper. First, scope of role: peer-helpers are not therapists. Significant stress, persistent low mood, suicidal thoughts, self-harm, or substance use require referral to a counsellor, family doctor, or crisis line — not a peer-conversation alone. Second, your own well-being: helping others is itself stressful. Compassion fatigue is real. The peer-helper who never refreshes their own resources is heading for burnout. Ontario's Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868) and Black Youth Helpline (1-833-294-8650) are 24/7 resources you can refer to; the Hope for Wellness Helpline (1-855-242-3310) provides culturally-grounded support for Indigenous peoples. These are not failures of peer-helping; they are part of competent peer-helping.

  • Resilience — capacity to absorb stress, recover, and learn (Masten).
  • 4 Cs of resilience — connection, competence, character, contribution (APA).
  • Eustress / distress — productive vs depleting stress (Selye).
  • Stress mindset — beliefs about stress that themselves shape physiological response (McGonigal).
  • Compassion fatigue — emotional depletion that accumulates when one supports others over time.
  • Referral — connecting a peer to a professional resource beyond the peer-helper's scope.

Worked Example — Building a Personal Stress-Management Plan

Priya is a high-performing Grade 11 student preparing for university applications, working a 12-hour weekend shift, taking GPP3O, captaining her debate team, and feeling increasingly flat. She maps her current life across the four categories. Physical: she's averaging 5.5 hours of sleep, no exercise, mostly skipping breakfast. Cognitive: she's catastrophising about university outcomes. Relational: she's pulled away from friends because "I have too much to do." Restorative: none. She designs a one-month plan: bedtime by 10:45 (alarm to remind), a 30-minute walk three mornings a week, lunch with one specific friend each week, one 5-minute journal entry on the way home from work, and a referral to the school's social-work-on-call service for one consultation. She does not try to "fix everything" — she fixes one item per category. Six weeks later her sleep is averaging 7 hours; the flatness has lifted. The plan worked because it was holistic and small.

  • Treating resilience as a personality trait rather than a built capacity.
  • Sleep-debt as a badge of honour — chronic sleep deprivation depresses mood, cognition, and immune function.
  • Trying to outsource emotional self-care to scrolling.
  • Not making the referral when the peer's situation is beyond peer-helper scope.
  • Helping others without protecting your own restorative time — leads to burnout, withdrawal, and ineffective help.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "demonstrate the use of healthy stress-management strategies."

🎯 1.6 SMART Goals and Time Management
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A goal turns intention into action. The SMART framework, developed in 1981 by George T. Doran in Management Review, gives goals five attributes: Specific (a vague goal cannot guide action), Measurable (you need to know whether you have hit it), Achievable (within your skills, resources, and time), Relevant (connected to a larger value or aim), and Time-bound (a deadline activates planning). Compare "get better at public speaking" with "deliver a five-minute presentation to the GPP3O class on emotional intelligence by Friday October 18, with no filler words for at least three of the five minutes." The second is SMART; the first is a wish.

Goals work best when stacked: a long-term goal (one to several years) anchored by a personal value; a set of medium-term goals (one to three months) that mark milestones; and a set of short-term goals (this week, this day) that are immediately actionable. The short-term goals are where time-management lives. Without the long-term anchor, you optimise for whatever feels urgent; without the short-term work, the long-term goal stays an idea. The Ontario IPP encourages students to record goals at all three time-scales.

Time management is the practical skill of allocating limited hours to chosen priorities. Four habits do most of the work. Plan ahead: a weekly review (Sunday afternoon or Monday morning, fifteen minutes) where you scan the week's commitments and pre-decide what gets the prime hours. Prioritise: Stephen Covey's matrix (urgent/important vs not-urgent/important) is a classic tool; spend more time in the "important, not urgent" quadrant (long-term work, relationships, health), where leadership growth lives. Time-box: assign a fixed window to a task and stop when the window ends (the Pomodoro technique uses 25-minute focused work + 5-minute break cycles). Protect attention: phone in another room, notifications off, single-task. Attention is the scarce resource, not time.

For a Grade-11 student in GPP3O, the leadership project (Unit 5) is the most demanding time-management exercise of the course. Students who back-plan from the implementation date, set weekly sub-goals, and protect their planning time finish strong. Students who treat the project as something they will "get to next week" run out of runway. Time management is also a peer-helper skill — many of the students you will help are struggling because they have not learned how to chunk a large task into next steps. Teaching them the chunk-and-plan habit is one of the highest-leverage supports you can offer.

  • SMART goal — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound (Doran, 1981).
  • Long/medium/short-term goal stacking — connecting daily action to multi-year aim.
  • Covey's Time Matrix — quadrants of urgent/important; the "important, not urgent" quadrant is where leadership growth happens.
  • Time-boxing — assigning a fixed time window to a task and stopping when it ends.
  • Pomodoro technique — 25-minute focused work + 5-minute break, repeated.
  • Back-planning — starting from a deadline and working backward to today's tasks.

Worked Example — A SMART Goal for Peer Mediation

Vague goal: "I want to get good at peer mediation." SMART rewrite: "By Friday December 6, I will complete the school's two-day peer-mediator training, observe two live mediations as a silent partner, and co-mediate one mediation under teacher supervision, journaling 200 words after each observation. I will track my progress on a weekly checklist with my GPP3O teacher." Notice: Specific (training, observation, co-mediation), Measurable (the checklist), Achievable (training is offered; the teacher has agreed to supervise), Relevant (anchored to her long-term aim to work in youth-services), Time-bound (December 6). The SMART version can be checked, revised, and either celebrated or troubleshot — a wish cannot.

  • Writing a goal that is so loose ("be a better leader") it cannot be tested.
  • Setting an unrealistically ambitious goal that demoralises after a week — start with what you'll actually do.
  • Skipping the weekly review — without it, the system collapses inside a fortnight.
  • Living in the urgent/important quadrant only — short-term firefighting at the cost of long-term growth.
  • Multitasking — attention-switching costs roughly 20–40% of effective work time.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.3 — "set short- and long-term SMART goals connected to leadership growth."
