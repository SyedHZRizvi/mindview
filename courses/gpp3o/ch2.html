Unit 2: Interpersonal Communication Skills

Communication is the medium through which leadership and peer support actually happen. This unit gives you the working models, the everyday techniques, and the conflict skills you will use every day for the rest of your life — at school, at work, in friendships, and in family.

📡 2.1 Communication Models — Sender, Message, Receiver, Noise
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Communication looks simple from the outside — one person speaks, another listens — but every communication researcher since Shannon and Weaver (1949) has shown that it is structurally complex. The Shannon-Weaver model identifies five elements: a sender who has an idea, an encoded message (the idea translated into words, tone, expression), a channel (face-to-face, phone, text, email, video), a receiver who must decode, and noise — anything that distorts the message between sender and receiver. Noise can be physical (a loud cafeteria), psychological (the receiver is tired or distracted), semantic (a word means different things to the two parties), or cultural (gestures and norms differ across communities). Wilbur Schramm's 1954 extension adds feedback — the receiver becomes a sender too, completing a loop.

For a peer-leader, the practical implication is that the message you intended is almost never the message the receiver experienced. When you say "you really care about this, don't you?" and your friend hears it as "you're overreacting," noise has done its work. The communication is the message-as-received, not the message-as-sent. This is why effective communicators check for understanding ("when I said X, did you hear me as saying X or something else?") instead of assuming. The check is not weakness; it is craft.

Albert Mehrabian's 1967 research is often misquoted as "communication is 7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language." That's a misreading — his study was specifically about messages where word and tone were inconsistent (e.g., saying "I'm fine" with a flat expression). His point was that when channels conflict, listeners trust non-verbal channels over verbal ones. The accurate generalisation: consistency across channels matters. If your words say "I want to hear you" but your body says "I want to leave," the listener will trust your body. Aligning channels is the goal — not because non-verbal communication is "the most important" but because dissonance erodes trust.

Channels also have different fit for different content. A text message is fine for a logistics question; it is not fine for a sensitive emotional conversation. Email is fine for a record of an agreement; it is not fine for delivering criticism for the first time. Face-to-face (or at least video) is required for high-stakes conversations because it lets the parties read non-verbal feedback and repair misunderstandings in real time. The Ontario curriculum specifically asks students to choose channels deliberately rather than defaulting to the most convenient. This is one of the small competencies that distinguish a thoughtful leader from a thoughtless one.

  • Shannon-Weaver model — sender, message, channel, receiver, noise (1949).
  • Encoding / decoding — translating an idea into a message and back into an idea.
  • Noise — anything that distorts a message in transit: physical, psychological, semantic, cultural.
  • Feedback loop — the receiver's response that closes the communication cycle (Schramm).
  • Channel fit — matching the communication channel to the sensitivity of the content.
  • Mehrabian's rule — when verbal and non-verbal channels conflict, receivers trust the non-verbal.

Worked Example — Diagnosing a Failed Communication

Mateo texts his friend Lucia: "We need to talk." She reads it during a stressful exam-prep period and spirals into anxiety, sure that she has done something wrong. The two meet two days later; Mateo wanted to ask whether she would co-host a charity event. Diagnose using the model: Encoding was poor — "we need to talk" is an emotionally-loaded phrase that channel-mismatched the content. Channel was wrong — text strips tone and context. Noise was high — Lucia was already stressed, and the text triggered a relationship-threat schema. Feedback was delayed two days. A better encoding: "Hey, want to co-host the Spring Drive with me? No pressure, I just want to throw the idea out — happy to chat at lunch tomorrow." The receiver decodes the same intent without spiralling. Same idea, much less noise.

  • Believing that "what I said" is what the other person heard.
  • Using texts and DMs for high-stakes conversations.
  • Citing Mehrabian's "7-38-55" as if it applies to all communication, not just channel-conflict cases.
  • Ignoring cultural and neurodivergence-related variation in how non-verbal cues are read.
  • Not checking for understanding — assuming silence equals agreement.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "interpret non-verbal cues and explain how culture, gender, neurodivergence, and power shape their meaning."

👂 2.2 Active Listening
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Active listening is the highest-leverage skill in peer support. Carl Rogers, the psychologist who developed person-centred therapy, named the practice "active listening" in a 1957 paper with Richard Farson: listening "with a purpose" — to understand the speaker's experience, not to respond to it. The opposite is what most of us do by default — we listen with half attention while the other half is already preparing what we will say next. Active listening replaces preparation-listening with what Stephen Covey called "empathic listening: seek first to understand, then to be understood."

Five techniques operationalise active listening. Attending behaviours: body turned toward the speaker, comfortable eye contact (calibrated for cultural and neurodivergence variation), open posture, occasional nods. Open questions: questions that invite reflection rather than yes/no answers ("what was that like for you?" instead of "did that make you sad?"). Paraphrasing: reflecting back the content of what was said in your own words ("So what I'm hearing is that the assignment landed at the same week your grandfather went into hospital"). Summarising: at intervals, pulling together what has been said so far ("Let me see if I have this — three things have happened this week, and they are connected because…"). Reflecting feeling: naming the emotion you sense, tentatively ("It sounds like there's a lot of frustration in that"). Notice that none of these techniques include "and here's what you should do." Advice is the death of active listening.

Silence is also a technique. Most peer-helpers under-use silence. A six-to-ten-second silence after a question feels uncomfortable, but it is often what the speaker needs to find the next sentence. Filling the silence with another question, or with reassurance, almost always cuts off whatever was about to surface. Practice tolerating silence; it is one of the kindest things you can offer.

Active listening is hard. Genuine attention is metabolically costly — your brain spends real glucose on it. You cannot listen actively to ten people in one day; you may not be able to listen actively to three. Knowing your capacity and protecting it is part of the discipline. The Ontario curriculum frames this within Strand B as a core peer-helper skill — and within Strand D as part of the boundary work of competent helping. You will not be useful to peers if you have spent all your active listening on social-media debates and arrived at the conversation with nothing left.

  • Active listening — purposeful listening to understand the speaker's experience (Rogers).
  • Attending behaviours — body posture, eye contact, and presence that signal "I'm with you."
  • Open question — a question that invites reflection rather than a yes/no answer.
  • Paraphrasing — restating content in your own words to check understanding.
  • Reflecting feeling — tentatively naming the emotion in what was said.
  • Empathic listening — listening to understand, not to respond (Covey).

Worked Example — A 90-Second Helping Exchange

Peer: "I don't know, I just feel like everything is falling apart." (You resist the urge to say "It'll be okay" or "Have you tried…")
You (reflecting feeling): "It sounds like things feel really overwhelming right now." (silence — six seconds)
Peer: "Yeah, I mean my mum's been sick and the chemistry test is Friday and my dad keeps asking about university and I can't even think about it."
You (paraphrasing + open question): "Three really big things hitting at once — your mum's health, the test, the university pressure. Which of those weighs on you most right now?"
Peer: "Honestly? My mum. I'm pretending I'm fine for everyone but I'm scared."
You (silence — eight seconds): the peer cries briefly. You stay present, no fixing.
Notice: you have given no advice; you have asked one open question; you have reflected feeling once and paraphrased once. The peer has reached the real thing under the surface. That is what 90 seconds of active listening can do. The next step might be a referral to the school social worker — but the peer would not have accepted that referral if you had not first listened.

  • Jumping to advice — "Have you tried..." closes the conversation.
  • Filling silence with reassurance — "It'll be okay" cuts off the actual feeling.
  • Performing listening (nodding, eye contact) while planning your reply.
  • Asking yes/no questions — they hand the speaker only two responses.
  • Forgetting that listening is metabolically costly and you have a finite capacity.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "use active-listening techniques in helping conversations."

💪 2.3 Assertive Communication: Passive, Aggressive, Assertive
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Communication style is the typical pattern with which you ask for what you need, set limits, and respond to disagreement. The classic four-style framework, developed in clinical psychology in the 1970s by Manuel Smith and others, names: passive (you avoid your own needs to keep the peace; build-up of resentment; "I'm fine, whatever you want"), aggressive (your needs at the expense of others'; intimidation, blame; "you always make a mess of this"), passive-aggressive (indirect expression of frustration; sarcasm, silent treatment, deliberate lateness), and assertive (your needs clearly stated while respecting others' needs; "I felt frustrated when X happened, because Y; I'd like Z"). Assertive communication is the target for healthy adult and peer-leader functioning.

Assertive language has a structure. The classic formula is I feel X when Y because Z; I would like W. "I feel discouraged when meetings start 20 minutes late, because then we never finish on time, and I miss my bus. I would like us to start within five minutes of the scheduled time, even if not everyone is here." Notice: it owns the feeling ("I feel"), names a specific behaviour ("meetings start 20 minutes late," not "you're always late"), explains the impact ("we don't finish, I miss my bus"), and offers a specific request ("start within five minutes"). It does not blame, attack, or globalise. It is hard, but learnable. It is also culturally calibrated — direct assertiveness reads differently in different cultural contexts and you should adapt while keeping the substance.

Why does this matter for peer support and leadership? A passive leader cannot hold a team to a standard. An aggressive leader cannot hold a team's trust. A passive-aggressive leader corrodes the team without ever stating the actual concern. An assertive leader can say to a struggling teammate "I noticed you missed the last two meetings; what's going on?" without making it an attack or pretending it didn't happen. The skill applies in peer-helper situations too: you can assertively decline to keep a confidence that involves risk-of-harm (a critical competency that you'll meet in Unit 4). Assertiveness is what makes the rest of peer-helping safe and honest.

Assertiveness training works. CBT-based assertiveness programs show measurable improvements in social functioning, self-efficacy, and reduction of anxiety in randomised trials. The most effective elements are role-play (practising assertive scripts in a safe setting), feedback (recording and reviewing), and graduated real-world practice (start with low-stakes assertiveness — ordering food, returning a purchase — before moving to high-stakes — naming a friend's hurtful behaviour). The Ontario curriculum embeds these techniques in Strand B; many schools run separate assertiveness modules in peer-helper training.

  • Passive style — avoiding one's own needs to keep peace; resentment accumulates.
  • Aggressive style — pursuing one's needs at others' expense; trust erodes.
  • Passive-aggressive style — indirect expression of frustration; sarcasm, withholding.
  • Assertive style — clear statement of one's needs while respecting others.
  • I-statement — "I feel X when Y because Z; I would like W."
  • Graduated practice — starting with low-stakes assertiveness and building toward high-stakes situations.

Worked Example — Three Versions of the Same Conversation

Setup: Your group-project partner has missed the last two work sessions. The deadline is in six days.
Passive: "Oh, no worries, I can just do the rest myself, it's fine." (You finish exhausted; the resentment poisons the friendship; the partner learns nothing.)
Aggressive: "You're so useless. You never do your share. I'm not covering for you again." (The partner feels attacked, defends, the friendship cracks; the project still suffers.)
Passive-aggressive: Sigh in their direction. Post a vague Instagram story about "doing all the work as usual." Decline to answer their texts. (Nothing changes except the relationship gets colder.)
Assertive: "I want to flag something — you've missed our last two work sessions, and I've ended up taking on more than the agreed share. The deadline is Friday. I'd like to meet tomorrow afternoon for an hour to redistribute the remaining work. Are you in?" The partner can now respond honestly (maybe a family situation? maybe carelessness?). Either way, the conversation has moved the work forward.

  • Treating assertiveness as the same as aggression — they are opposite.
  • Using "I-statements" formulaically without honestly owning the feeling.
  • Saying "I feel that you are…" — that is still a you-statement disguised; it is a thought, not a feeling.
  • Going from passive to aggressive without ever practising assertive — the swing of someone who has been holding it in.
  • Forgetting that cultural calibration matters — directness reads differently across communities.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "distinguish among passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, and assertive communication and apply assertive language in role-plays."

🪞 2.4 Constructive Feedback
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Feedback is information about behaviour, given by someone who saw it, intended to help the receiver perform better next time. Good feedback is one of the cheapest, highest-leverage tools available to a peer-leader — and one of the most commonly mishandled. The two failure modes are equal-and-opposite: feedback that is too soft (vague praise that gives no useful information) and feedback that is too harsh (criticism that lands as personal attack and triggers defensiveness). Both fail because they do not change behaviour.

A reliable structure is Behaviour – Impact – Suggestion (BIS). Behaviour: name the specific observable action ("When you presented Slide 3, you read the slide word-for-word"). Impact: describe the effect ("The audience couldn't tell which words were the main idea, and three people looked at their phones"). Suggestion: offer a concrete alternative ("Next time, try summarising the slide in one sentence in your own words, then pointing at the slide for evidence"). The structure works because it does not attack the person, only the behaviour; it explains why the behaviour matters; and it offers something the person can actually do differently. Marshall Goldsmith's "feed-forward" variant goes further: focus entirely on what could be done next time, with no archaeological dig into what was wrong.

Three principles strengthen feedback. Timing: feedback closest to the behaviour has the most effect; delays of more than a week or two lose force. Channel: as with all sensitive communication, face-to-face beats text; never deliver hard feedback in a public Slack channel or a group chat. Ratio: Marcial Losada's positivity-ratio research suggested high-performing teams tend toward more positive than negative feedback (the "3 to 1" ratio is sometimes overstated, but the direction is right) — though all feedback must be honest. Sugar-coating without substance is worse than nothing. The goal is honest, specific, growth-oriented feedback delivered with care.

Receiving feedback is its own skill. Sheila Heen and Douglas Stone's Thanks for the Feedback (2014) names three triggers that make us defensive: truth triggers (the feedback feels wrong), relationship triggers (the feedback comes from a person we don't trust), identity triggers (the feedback contradicts our self-image). Naming the trigger to yourself ("I'm having a relationship trigger right now") helps you separate the message from the noise. Ask clarifying questions. Take time before responding. You don't have to accept all feedback, but you should hear it fully before you decide.

  • Constructive feedback — specific information about behaviour, given to help the receiver perform better.
  • BIS model — Behaviour, Impact, Suggestion.
  • Feed-forward — feedback focused on what could be done next time, not what went wrong (Goldsmith).
  • Truth, relationship, identity triggers — three sources of defensiveness when receiving feedback (Heen & Stone).
  • Sandwich method — praise-criticism-praise; commonly taught but often delivers neither honest praise nor clear criticism.
  • Timing — feedback delivered close to the behaviour is more effective.

Worked Example — Rewriting Feedback

Original feedback (vague): "Good job on the presentation, you have nice energy. Maybe just slow down a little."
BIS rewrite: "You opened with a strong question that hooked the room — I saw three people lean forward (Behaviour + Impact). In the middle, you sped up so much that I missed the second sub-point on Slide 4 — I had to read it from your slide instead of hearing it (Impact). Try slowing your pace through the technical sections and pausing for two seconds before each transition (Suggestion). The opening and close were excellent; the middle would land better with that adjustment." This feedback is honest, specific, growth-oriented, and respectful. The receiver knows exactly what worked and what to adjust.

  • Sandwich method without substance — empty praise framing a vague criticism gives the receiver nothing.
  • Delivering hard feedback by text or in a group chat.
  • Personal attacks ("you're a careless person") rather than behavioural specifics ("you submitted three slides without proofreading").
  • Giving feedback only on what went wrong — the receiver does not learn what to keep doing.
  • Refusing to receive feedback when you give it — the most effective leaders are also the most curious about how they could be better.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "give and receive constructive feedback using a structured model."

⚔️ 2.5 The Thomas–Kilmann Conflict Styles
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Conflict is unavoidable in any group of more than one person. The question is not whether conflict will happen but how the parties handle it. Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann's 1974 conflict model — still the most-cited framework in the field — plots conflict behaviour on two axes: assertiveness (how much you pursue your own concerns) and cooperativeness (how much you pursue the other party's concerns). The intersections produce five styles. Competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness): "I win, you lose." Useful in emergencies, on principle, when speed is critical; corrosive as a default. Accommodating (low assertiveness, high cooperativeness): "You win, I lose." Useful when the issue matters more to the other party, when preserving the relationship is paramount, when you discover you were wrong; corrosive when used to avoid every difficult conversation. Avoiding (low both): "Neither of us wins." Useful for trivial issues, when emotions need to cool, when you have no power to change the outcome; destructive when significant issues stay unaddressed. Compromising (moderate both): "We each give up something." Useful under time pressure, with peers of equal power, when a temporary settlement is needed; suboptimal when a creative integrative solution was possible. Collaborating (high both): "We both win." Useful for important issues with relational future, when learning matters, when both parties' commitment is needed; expensive in time and energy, so reserved for the issues that warrant it.

No single style is best. Each is right for some situations and wrong for others. Healthy adults and effective leaders are flexible — they can deploy any of the five depending on the situation. The most common growth direction in conflict-style training is for people who default to one style (typically avoiding or accommodating, in the case of nonconfrontational students) to add competing and collaborating to their range. Less commonly, students who default to competing learn to add accommodating and collaborating.

For peer leaders specifically, two situations matter most. Mediating between two peers (Unit 4): you are not a party to the conflict; your job is to help both parties move toward collaborating, or at least compromising. Your own conflict with a teammate: when emotions are hot, choose avoiding briefly (give yourself an hour to cool); when you return, choose collaborating if the relationship and issue both matter, or compromising if they don't. The choice is conscious, not default.

Cultural and gendered patterns shape conflict style. Research on cross-cultural conflict (notably Mary Jane Collier and others) shows that direct-confrontation styles read as "honest" in some cultural contexts and as "rude" in others; harmony-preserving styles read as "respectful" in some contexts and as "passive" in others. The Ontario curriculum, in its equity strand, asks students to consider how conflict reading is culturally calibrated and to avoid the assumption that one style ("direct talk") is universally healthy.

  • Thomas–Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI) — five styles plotted on assertiveness × cooperativeness axes.
  • Competing — high assertiveness, low cooperativeness.
  • Accommodating — low assertiveness, high cooperativeness.
  • Avoiding — low on both.
  • Compromising — moderate on both.
  • Collaborating — high on both; the "integrative" style aimed at a both-win outcome.

Worked Example — Matching Style to Situation

Three scenarios. (1) Fire alarm during your group meeting: Competing is right — "Everyone, out, now." This is not the moment for consensus. (2) Your friend insists on the table by the window, you don't care: Accommodating is right — "Sure, the window is fine." Saving the energy for things that matter. (3) Two teammates disagree on the project topic — both feel strongly, the topic will shape the whole project: Collaborating is right — invest time to find a third option that captures what each cares about. Different situations, different correct answers. A skilled conflict-handler reads the situation in seconds and picks the appropriate style. A novice has only one tool.

  • Treating one style as morally superior — they are situational tools, not personality verdicts.
  • Defaulting to avoiding for every conflict — the issues stack up and the next one detonates.
  • Calling compromise "collaborating" — they are different; collaboration finds a third option, compromise splits the difference.
  • Ignoring cultural calibration in conflict style reading.
  • Forgetting that mediating between two parties is different from being a party — the styles look different in the third-party role.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "describe sources of conflict in groups and identify five conflict styles (Thomas–Kilmann)."

🔄 2.6 Restorative Practices in Schools
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Restorative practices are a set of relational tools, rooted in Indigenous justice traditions (including from Aotearoa-New Zealand Māori communities and several Indigenous nations in Canada), that focus on repairing harm and rebuilding relationship rather than on assigning blame and applying punishment. In schools, the practices include community circles (regular, structured group conversations to build relationship before harm occurs), responsive circles (after harm, bringing those affected together to talk about impact), and restorative conferences (more formal meetings between a person who caused harm and a person who was harmed, with trained facilitators). The Ontario Ministry of Education has supported restorative-practices adoption in many school boards as an alternative to purely disciplinary responses to conflict.

The five restorative questions developed by the International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP) structure conversations in ways that peer-mediators and leaders find useful. For the person who caused harm: (1) What happened? (2) What were you thinking at the time? (3) What have you thought about since? (4) Who has been affected by what you did, and how? (5) What do you think you need to do to make things as right as possible? For the person who was harmed: (1) What did you think when you realised what had happened? (2) What impact has this incident had on you and others? (3) What has been the hardest thing for you? (4) What do you think needs to happen to make things right? Notice that the framing is curious, not accusatory; it asks for the person to do their own work of accountability rather than receive judgment.

Restorative practices are not the same as conflict-avoidance or "letting things go." When a serious harm has occurred, restorative work is harder than punitive work, not easier — the person who caused harm has to face the impact, the person who was harmed has to articulate it, and both have to participate in repair. Research on restorative discipline in schools (Anne Gregory at Rutgers, the WestEd evaluations) shows reductions in suspension and disciplinary disparities — particularly for racialised students who have historically been over-disciplined — and modest improvements in school climate.

For a peer leader, two takeaways. First, you can use community-circle elements (a structured opening question, a talking piece) in any group meeting to build the relational foundation that prevents many conflicts. Second, when a small conflict arises in a group you lead — a hurt comment, a missed expectation — a single restorative question ("What happened? Who's been affected?") is often enough to surface and repair before the situation escalates. The skills sit alongside the conflict-styles of Lesson 2.5; they don't replace them, but they expand the toolkit.

  • Restorative practices — relational tools focused on repairing harm and rebuilding relationship.
  • Community circle — a regular structured group conversation to build relationship.
  • Restorative conference — a formal facilitated meeting between people who caused and were affected by harm.
  • The five restorative questions — IIRP framework.
  • Talking piece — an object passed in circle; only the holder speaks. Roots in many Indigenous traditions.
  • Repair — the action a person takes to make things as right as possible after harm.

Worked Example — A Restorative Conversation

Two Grade 11 students, in a GPP3O group project, have stopped speaking after one of them made a joke that the other read as racist. The teacher proposes a restorative conversation. Each student meets privately with the trained peer-mediator first. Then a circle: facilitator, both students, and a trusted adult. Question 1 to the speaker: "What happened?" He describes the joke and adds, "I thought it was funny in the moment; I didn't think about how it landed." Question 2 to the other student: "What did you think when you heard it?" She names what she felt and why — that she has heard the same joke from strangers her whole life and was tired of hearing it again from someone she had thought was a friend. Question 3 to the speaker: "Now that you've heard that, what's your thought?" He apologises, specifically, for the harm. Question 4 to the second student: "What needs to happen for things to move forward?" She names two things: a public correction in the group, and that the joke not be repeated. The speaker agrees to both. The relationship is not immediately whole, but the conversation has happened — both parties are clearer, the harm is named, and the path forward has been agreed. That is restorative work.

  • Treating restorative practices as "soft discipline" — they are often harder than punitive responses.
  • Skipping the relationship-building circles and using restorative tools only after harm.
  • Using the five questions as a checklist rather than as an invitation to genuine reflection.
  • Forgetting that restorative practices have roots in Indigenous justice traditions; honouring those origins matters.
  • Trying to facilitate a restorative conference without training — a trained adult facilitator is usually required.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "demonstrate steps in a peer-mediation framework and apply restorative-practice questions to a school conflict."
