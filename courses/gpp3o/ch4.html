Unit 4: Peer Support and Mentoring Models

Peer support is a specific kind of helping with specific frameworks, ethics, and limits. This unit builds the core peer-helper skills and — equally important — names the boundaries that make peer support safe for both helper and helped.

🤝 4.1 What Peer Support Is (and What It Isn't)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Peer support has many forms. The distinctions matter because each form has its own scope, training, and ethical structure. Peer tutoring is task-focused — one student supports another's academic learning in a specific subject (math, English, science). Peer mentoring is relationship-focused — an older or more experienced student walks alongside a younger one through a transition (Grade 9 orientation, new-student welcomes, post-injury return to sport). Peer mediation is conflict-focused — a trained student helps two peers in dispute reach a resolution. Peer support is emotion- and listening-focused — a trained student offers a listening conversation to a peer who is struggling, without taking the role of counsellor or therapist. The four overlap, but the training and the ethical considerations differ.

Peer support is rooted in the recognition that young people often talk to other young people before they talk to adults. Research from the Mental Health Commission of Canada and from international peer-support programs consistently shows that adolescents are more likely to disclose distress to peers than to parents, teachers, or counsellors — often by a large margin. The implication: trained peers can be a critical first-line of recognition and referral. The implication is not that peers replace professionals; it is that peers can recognise what is happening early and connect their friend to professional help. The peer-helper is a bridge, not a destination.

What peer support specifically is not: therapy, counselling, diagnosis, prescription, or rescue. A peer-helper does not interpret a friend's dreams, recommend medication, conduct CBT exercises, or pull a friend back from a crisis through their own effort. A peer-helper listens, recognises, supports, encourages, and refers. The boundary is not optional — it protects both parties. Peer-helpers who try to act as professionals frequently burn out and sometimes do harm. Professional helpers who do not have the peer's daily proximity miss what peer-helpers can see.

Peer-support programs in Ontario schools (variously titled Link Crew, Peer Helpers, Peer Mentors, Best Buddies, and others) typically include structured training — 15 to 40 hours — covering listening skills, confidentiality, referral pathways, safeTALK or similar suicide-recognition training, equity and inclusive language, and a supervised practicum. The structure of training matters. An untrained peer-helper can still be a good friend; the training adds a specific competency for harder situations. Ontario's Guidance and Career Education curriculum embeds peer-helper training within the Strand D expectations of GPP3O.

  • Peer tutoring — academic task-focused peer support.
  • Peer mentoring — relationship-focused walking-alongside.
  • Peer mediation — trained third-party help in a peer-to-peer conflict.
  • Peer support — trained listening-and-referral conversation.
  • Bridge, not destination — peer-helpers connect peers to professional help, not replace it.
  • safeTALK — LivingWorks 3-hour training in suicide-alertness and connecting to help; widely used in Ontario.

Worked Example — Identifying the Right Form of Support

A Grade 9 student approaches you with three different concerns over three weeks. (1) "I don't get linear equations and the test is Monday." — Peer tutoring is the right form; help with the math or refer to a tutor. (2) "I just transferred from another country and I don't know how anything works here." — Peer mentoring is the right form; offer to be a regular contact for the first term and connect with the Link Crew program. (3) "I haven't been sleeping for two weeks and I think about not wanting to exist." — Peer support listening for now, immediately combined with referral to a school counsellor or Kids Help Phone; this is not your problem to solve alone. The skill of the peer-helper is to recognise which form of support is needed and to match the response.

  • Treating all peer support as the same — the four forms have different scopes and trainings.
  • Trying to be a peer-therapist — beyond the scope and unsafe.
  • Underestimating peer support — early recognition can prevent crises.
  • Not getting trained — friend instinct is good; structured training is better for harder situations.
  • Forgetting that you are a bridge — your job is to connect, not to carry alone.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "distinguish among peer tutoring, peer mentoring, peer mediation, and peer support."

🗣️ 4.2 The Five-Step Helping Conversation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A structured framework keeps a peer-support conversation focused, useful, and safe. The five-step framework taught in many Ontario peer-helper programs adapts Carol Tilley's and others' work: Engage – Listen – Clarify – Options – Next Steps. Each step has its own purpose; skipping or rushing a step is the most common cause of unhelpful peer-support conversations.

Engage. The opening matters. The peer needs to know that this conversation is real (you are present, not distracted), confidential (with clear limits — see Lesson 4.3), and safe (you will not judge or report indiscriminately). A simple opener: "Want to find somewhere quiet? I've got 20 minutes and I'm not in a rush." The setting matters more than students initially realise — a noisy hallway is not where vulnerable talk happens. Listen. This is the heart of the conversation; apply the active-listening skills from Unit 2 — attending behaviours, open questions, paraphrasing, reflecting feeling, tolerating silence. The peer talks; you listen. The temptation to fix is strongest here. Resist it.

Clarify. When the peer has talked themselves out, summarise back: "Let me see if I have this — three things are happening: X, Y, Z. The hardest right now is X. Is that right?" The clarification step does two things: it confirms you've heard them accurately (and lets them correct), and it helps them see their own situation more clearly. People often only know what they think after they've heard it back. Options. Not advice — options. "Some things people find helpful in situations like this are A, B, or C. Is any of those something you'd want to try?" The peer chooses. Offering options instead of advice respects the peer's autonomy and avoids the trap of "you should just X" prescriptions that rarely work. Next Steps. Concrete and small. "Okay, you said you'd try talking to the counsellor. Want me to walk over with you now, or would you rather book it yourself? Either is fine." Make a specific plan with a specific window. Then check in — within a day or two — to see how it went.

The framework is a guide, not a script. Real conversations loop — you may listen, clarify, listen more, clarify again before reaching options. The key discipline is not to skip Listen (which novices do, jumping to options) or to skip Clarify (which leaves both parties uncertain whether the right thing was heard) or to skip Next Steps (which leaves the conversation feeling complete but produces no change). For serious situations — risk of self-harm, suicidal ideation, abuse — the framework yields immediately to crisis-response steps (see Lesson 4.4); peer-support skills support but do not replace professional crisis response.

  • Engage — set the time, place, and frame for the conversation.
  • Listen — apply active-listening skills; let the peer talk themselves out.
  • Clarify — summarise and check understanding.
  • Options — offer two or three pathways rather than prescribing one.
  • Next Steps — agree a specific, small first action with a follow-up.
  • Loop — real conversations cycle through listen-clarify multiple times before reaching options.

Worked Example — A Helping Conversation Mapped to the Five Steps

Engage: "Want to grab a quiet spot before practice? I have 25 minutes." Listen: peer describes feeling overwhelmed at home and at school; you reflect feeling and ask one open question; silence; peer adds the part underneath ("I haven't told anyone but my dad lost his job two weeks ago"). Clarify: "Three things — your dad's job loss is changing things at home, you're feeling pressure to keep your grades up to protect your scholarship, and you're trying to handle both alone. The most pressing one right now sounds like the loneliness. Is that right?" Peer agrees and adds detail. Options: "Some things people in this situation find helpful are talking to the school's social worker who has confidential resources for families in financial transition; reaching out to a friend or two so the secret feels less heavy; or considering Kids Help Phone if you want to talk anonymously first. Any of those feel like a possible start?" Next Steps: "Okay, you said you'd try the social worker. Want me to book that for tomorrow morning together, or take it from here? Either way I'll text Friday to see how it went." The whole conversation took 22 minutes. The peer left lighter, with a real next step, and the peer-helper has done their job — bridge, not destination.

  • Skipping Engage — diving in without setting the frame.
  • Skipping Listen — jumping to advice in the first two minutes.
  • Skipping Clarify — leaving both parties uncertain about what was heard.
  • Offering advice instead of options — prescriptions reduce the peer's autonomy.
  • Not following up — without a check-in, the conversation can feel performative.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "apply a five-step helping conversation in a role-play."

🔒 4.3 Confidentiality and Its Limits
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Confidentiality is the trust that what a peer tells you in a helping conversation will not be shared without permission. It is foundational — peers who do not believe their words are safe will not say what is actually true. Most peer-support programs teach a clear opening sentence that establishes confidentiality and names its limits at the same time: "What you tell me stays between us, with three exceptions. If you tell me you might hurt yourself, or someone else might hurt you, or you're worried about a child under 16 being hurt, I have to bring in a trusted adult — and I'll tell you when I'm about to." Stating the limits upfront protects the peer (they know what will trigger help) and protects you (you are not betraying anyone by responding to those triggers).

Ontario law gives this structure legal force. The Child, Youth and Family Services Act, 2017, section 125, imposes a duty on every person — including youth — who has reasonable grounds to suspect that a child under 16 is being or has been physically, sexually, emotionally abused, or neglected, to report directly and immediately to a children's aid society. The duty is personal — you cannot delegate it by telling a teacher and assuming they will handle it (although telling a trusted adult who can support the report-making is wise). The duty applies even if you are not certain — "reasonable suspicion" is the threshold, not "proof." Peer-helpers carry this duty like any other person.

Three categories of disclosure require breaking confidentiality. (1) Risk of harm to self. Suicidal thoughts, plans, means, or active self-harm — bring in a trusted adult immediately. The safeTALK and ASIST frameworks emphasise: ask directly ("Are you thinking about suicide?"), listen carefully, connect to someone who can help, do not promise to keep this secret. (2) Risk of harm to others. A peer planning violence, sharing intent to harm a specific person — must be reported. (3) Child abuse or neglect. Per the CYFSA — if you have reasonable grounds, you must report to the Children's Aid Society. The phone number for Ontario is 1-833-882-8868 (provincial); each region has a local CAS number too.

Breaking confidentiality is hard. The peer may feel betrayed. The right approach is to tell them honestly: "I told you at the start I'd bring in an adult if you told me you might hurt yourself. I'm doing that now, and I'll stay with you while we do." Confidentiality is also broken by carelessness — gossiping with a friend about a conversation, sharing details with a partner, posting on social media. Peer-helpers protect confidentiality not only against deliberate sharing but against drift. Within a peer-helper team or in formal supervision, confidential discussion is appropriate (and required) for support and learning — but it is shared only with people who need to know and only in protected settings.

  • Confidentiality — the trust that what is shared in a helping conversation will not be passed on without permission.
  • Three exceptions — harm to self, harm to others, abuse of a child under 16.
  • CYFSA section 125 — Ontario duty to report reasonable suspicion of child abuse to a CAS.
  • Reasonable grounds — the legal threshold; not certainty but evidence-based suspicion.
  • safeTALK / ASIST — LivingWorks frameworks for suicide-alertness and intervention.
  • Confidentiality drift — the gradual erosion of confidentiality through gossip, social media, or careless sharing.

Worked Example — A Disclosure Requiring Action

A peer in a listening conversation discloses: "I've been thinking it would be easier if I weren't here." You ask directly: "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" The peer hesitates, then says yes, and shares that they have looked up methods. This is the moment confidentiality must yield. You say: "I'm really glad you told me. I want you to know I'm not going anywhere, and I have to bring in an adult so we can keep you safe — that's what I told you at the start. Who feels safest to you right now — your guidance counsellor, the social worker, your aunt I know is your safe adult?" Together, you walk to the counsellor's office. If after-hours, you call Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868) with the peer and stay on the line. You document briefly afterward, share with the peer-helper coordinator within 24 hours, and check in with the peer within 48 hours. The conversation was a peer-support conversation that became a crisis-response moment. The framework gave you the structure to make that transition cleanly.

  • Promising blanket confidentiality — "I won't tell anyone, I promise" — that you can't keep.
  • Skipping the limits-of-confidentiality opening sentence.
  • Treating reasonable suspicion as needing certainty before reporting.
  • Confidentiality drift — gossip with a roommate, vague social media post.
  • Trying to handle a crisis disclosure alone instead of bringing in a trusted adult.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 and D2.2 — "explain the limits of confidentiality" and "describe Ontario's mandatory reporting duty under the Child, Youth and Family Services Act."

📞 4.4 Referral Pathways — Knowing Who Can Help
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A peer-helper's most valuable knowledge is often not psychological — it is logistical. Knowing exactly which adult, agency, or hotline serves which kind of need allows you to make a warm hand-off when the conversation moves beyond peer-helper scope. A vague referral ("you should talk to someone") is much weaker than a specific one ("Mr. Yang in the guidance office sees Grade 11 students at lunch on Mondays and Wednesdays; would you like me to text him to book a slot?"). Specificity reduces the activation energy required of the peer.

In-school resources. Every Ontario secondary school has a guidance counsellor, often more than one. Most boards have school social workers, child-and-youth workers, attendance counsellors, and special-education teachers. Some have on-site nurses or partnerships with public-health units. Boards also have student-support services, mental-health leads, and crisis-response teams. A peer-helper learns the names, the schedules, and the specific scopes of these adults — your school's safeARRIVAL, your school's pathway for students experiencing housing insecurity, your school's process for academic accommodations. The Vice-Principal handles discipline; the Principal handles policy and complaints; the social worker handles family-context and mental-health support; the guidance counsellor handles academic, postsecondary, and many emotional concerns.

Community resources. Beyond the school, Ontario has a dense network of services available to young people, many free and confidential. Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868; text CONNECT to 686868; live chat at kidshelpphone.ca) — 24/7 bilingual counselling and crisis support; the most-used youth mental-health service in Canada. Black Youth Helpline (1-833-294-8650) — culturally-grounded support for Black youth. Hope for Wellness Helpline (1-855-242-3310) — 24/7 culturally-grounded support for Indigenous peoples; chat at hopeforwellness.ca. LGBT YouthLine (1-800-268-9688; text 647-694-4275) — peer support for LGBTQ2S+ youth. Talk4Healing (1-855-554-4325) — culturally-grounded support for Indigenous women. Ontario 211 — community services directory; 24/7 referrals to housing, food, financial, and counselling services. Telehealth Ontario (1-866-797-0000) — 24/7 nursing consultation, useful for health questions. ConnexOntario (1-866-531-2600) — addiction, mental health, problem gambling support.

For acute crises, the relevant numbers are 9-8-8 (Canada-wide suicide crisis helpline, launched 2023, available 24/7 by call or text), 911 (life-threatening emergency), and local mobile crisis intervention teams where available. Peer-helpers should know that for non-violent mental-health crises, a non-police response (mobile crisis team) is often preferable; in Ontario, several regions now operate community crisis teams alongside or instead of police response. Make sure the in-school crisis-response plan is one you have read; ask if you haven't.

  • Warm hand-off — specific referral that reduces activation energy for the peer.
  • Guidance counsellor — school-based academic, postsecondary, and emotional-support resource.
  • School social worker / child-and-youth worker — mental-health and family-context support.
  • Kids Help Phone — 1-800-668-6868; text CONNECT to 686868.
  • 9-8-8 — Canada-wide suicide crisis helpline (2023+).
  • Hope for Wellness Helpline — Indigenous-grounded 24/7 support.

Worked Example — Building a Referral Card

You produce a wallet-sized referral card for your peer-helper team. One side: In-school — Ms. Adebayo, Guidance, Office 217, Tue-Thu, ext 305; Mr. Lin, Social Worker, Office 209, Mon-Wed, by booking through guidance; Mr. O'Brien, Vice-Principal, Office 101 for discipline/safety. Other side: Community 24/7 — Kids Help Phone 1-800-668-6868; Hope for Wellness 1-855-242-3310; 9-8-8 for suicide crisis; 211 for community services; LGBT YouthLine 1-800-268-9688; Black Youth Helpline 1-833-294-8650; 911 for life-threatening emergency. You laminate twenty cards; you give one to every peer-helper and ask peer-helpers to give them to peers they think might need them. The simple object — a card in a pocket — is more useful in a crisis moment than any speech.

  • Vague referrals — "talk to someone" doesn't guide action.
  • Not knowing your in-school adults' specific scopes — guidance vs social work vs special education are different.
  • Forgetting culturally-specific lines — Hope for Wellness, Black Youth Helpline, LGBT YouthLine each serve specific needs.
  • Defaulting to 911 for non-violent mental-health crises when a mobile crisis team or 9-8-8 would serve better.
  • Carrying the referral knowledge only in your head — a card or list is more reliable.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "identify community and school resources to which a peer can be referred."

🧭 4.5 Mentoring — Formal and Informal
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Mentoring is a sustained, intentional relationship in which a more experienced person supports the growth of a less experienced one. Unlike peer support (which is typically conversational and reactive) or peer mediation (which is conflict-focused), mentoring is developmental — its purpose is the mentee's ongoing growth over weeks, months, or years. Research from Big Brothers Big Sisters Canada, Pathways to Education, and the United Way's youth-mentoring programs shows substantial benefits: improved school attendance and grades, higher rates of high-school completion, lower rates of risky behaviours, and improved well-being. The effects are largest when the mentoring relationship is sustained for at least 12 months and is structured.

Two broad models are useful to distinguish. Formal mentoring happens within a structured program — matched pairs, training for mentors, regular check-ins, defined goals, supervision. Examples: a school's official Link Crew or Best Buddies program, Big Brothers Big Sisters, the Ontario Indigenous Youth Mentorship Program, Boys & Girls Clubs of Canada. Formal mentoring has the strongest evidence of benefit and the most safeguards. Informal mentoring happens naturally — the senior cousin, the older volleyball captain, the after-school employer — without external structure or training. Informal mentoring is powerful but variable; without structure, it can drift, end suddenly, or cross boundaries. Most adolescents benefit from a mix.

Effective mentors share several practices. They listen first. They treat the mentee as a person with capacity, not a project to fix. They set explicit expectations — frequency of meetings, communication channels, what's in and out of scope. They maintain boundaries (see Lesson 4.6) — no money, no transportation outside agreed contexts, no over-sharing of mentor's personal life. They celebrate growth in specific terms, not vague praise. They introduce networks — a good mentor connects the mentee to additional people and resources. They plan the ending — the formal mentoring relationship usually has a defined endpoint, and a thoughtful close matters.

For Grade-11 peer leaders, the most common mentoring role is mentoring younger students — Grade 7-8 transitions, Grade 9 onboarding, course-specific support. The Ontario Skills Passport encourages students to document mentorship experience as part of the IPP. Mentoring is also a strong pathway into many careers — social work, teaching, coaching, youth work, community development. Programs like the Black Youth Action Plan, the Ontario Native Education Counselling Association, and many board-specific Indigenous education initiatives offer culturally-grounded mentoring pathways that you may want to learn about or eventually contribute to.

  • Mentoring — sustained, intentional supportive relationship for growth.
  • Formal mentoring — structured program with matching, training, supervision.
  • Informal mentoring — naturally-occurring developmental relationship without external structure.
  • Mentor practices — listen first, treat as capable, set expectations, maintain boundaries, celebrate growth, introduce networks, plan endings.
  • Link Crew / Best Buddies — common Ontario school-based formal mentoring programs.
  • Mentee — the person receiving mentoring; sometimes called "protégé."

Worked Example — Designing a Grade 9 Onboarding Mentorship

You volunteer to mentor a Grade 9 student through their first semester. Without structure: you'd exchange numbers, mean well, maybe meet twice, then drift. With structure: you and the mentee agree to meet for 30 minutes every second Wednesday during lunch; the first meeting establishes shared goals (in this case: navigating timetable, finding club for interest area, getting comfortable with the cafeteria); subsequent meetings check in on those goals and add new ones; you exchange one or two text messages a week about how things are going; you introduce the mentee to two senior peers in the activities they're interested in; you plan a final meeting in late January to review the term and discuss whether to continue. You agree to refer concerns beyond your scope to the guidance counsellor. The structure is light — about 90 minutes a month — but it transforms an informal goodwill into a meaningful developmental relationship. The mentee gets a stable adult-like figure during a hard transition; the mentor gets a portfolio-worthy experience and the satisfaction of contribution.

  • Confusing mentoring with becoming the mentee's best friend — different role.
  • Not setting expectations — leads to drift and inconsistent meetings.
  • Crossing boundaries (financial, social-media, family-context) without supervision.
  • Holding too long — formal mentoring relationships should have planned endings.
  • Not connecting the mentee to other people — a good mentor expands the mentee's network.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "distinguish among peer tutoring, peer mentoring, peer mediation, and peer support" (mentoring focus).

⚖️ 4.6 Boundaries and Burnout
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Boundaries are the rules that protect both the peer-helper and the peer being helped. They are not coldness — they are the architecture that lets the helping continue. A peer-helper without boundaries is heading for burnout, ineffective help, or harm. Boundaries are often hardest in peer-helping precisely because the helper is a peer — same age, similar life context, often friends with the helped — so the natural limits of professional roles are not built in. They must be constructed.

Several specific boundary areas need attention. Time boundaries: peer-helping conversations have beginnings and ends. A friend can text you at 2 a.m.; a peer-helper role has agreed hours and means. Texting all night with a peer in crisis is not effective help — it leads to your exhaustion and to dependence rather than referral. Agree with your program coordinator on hours of availability and refer to crisis lines outside those hours. Dual-relationship boundaries: avoid combining roles. If you are a peer-helper, you should not also be the peer's romantic partner or close personal friend without explicit consideration; if you are a peer-mediator, do not mediate a dispute involving your sister or your best friend. The conflict of interest will compromise both roles. Information boundaries: what you learn in a helping conversation is not currency in your other relationships. Do not bring it up with friends, even oblique references. Do not post anything that could identify the peer.

Financial and material boundaries: peer-helpers do not lend money, give gifts beyond small culturally-normal gestures (a coffee, a graduation card), or transport peers in personal vehicles outside program-sanctioned contexts. Social-media boundaries: the helper-helped relationship does not move automatically to private DMs, follower-relationships, or shared accounts. Consult your program's social-media policy — most boards have one. Sexual boundaries: no romantic or sexual contact within a helping relationship; if attraction develops, end the helper role and refer to a coordinator. Scope boundaries: peer-helpers do not provide therapy, prescribe medication, diagnose, or pretend to expertise they do not have. The scope is listening, supporting, and referring.

Burnout is the predictable consequence of helping without boundaries. It looks like emotional exhaustion (you have nothing left to give), depersonalisation (you start feeling cynical or detached toward the people you're helping), and reduced sense of accomplishment (you feel ineffective). Christina Maslach's research on burnout — originally on professional helpers, now extended to peer-helpers — names six conditions that drive it: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values mismatch. The fix is not motivational pep-talk; it is structural: reduce workload, increase control, recognise contribution, build a supportive community of fellow helpers, ensure fair distribution of cases, and align with values. Most importantly, take breaks. The Ontario peer-helper coordinator's job includes monitoring helper wellbeing. Your job includes telling them when you're depleted.

  • Time boundary — agreed hours and means for helping conversations.
  • Dual relationship — combining helper role with romantic, family, or close-friend role; usually to be avoided.
  • Information boundary — protecting what is learned in helping conversations.
  • Scope boundary — staying within listening-and-referral; not pretending to therapy.
  • Compassion fatigue / burnout — emotional exhaustion from sustained helping (Maslach).
  • Six drivers of burnout — workload, control, reward, community, fairness, values mismatch.

Worked Example — Building a Personal Sustainability Plan

You join the peer-helper team. You and your coordinator agree on a sustainability plan. Caseload: maximum two ongoing helping relationships at once. Hours: available during designated lunch periods Monday-Wednesday-Friday; not at home, not at night. Crisis coverage: after-hours peers are referred to Kids Help Phone or 9-8-8; you are not personally on-call. Supervision: 30-minute team check-in every two weeks; can request a private check-in with coordinator anytime. Personal restorative time: Tuesday and Thursday lunches are protected for your own friends, your own life. Self-check: a weekly two-question reflection ("Am I sleeping okay? Do I dread the next session?") — yes to dread, you talk to the coordinator. Termination criteria: if dread, sleep disruption, or detachment appear for two consecutive weeks, you take a break. The plan is concrete; it can be tested; and it is built before you start, not after you've burned out.

  • Treating boundaries as coldness rather than as the architecture that lets help continue.
  • Taking on too many helping relationships — every "yes" is also a "no" to something else.
  • Being on-call 24/7 by text — leads quickly to burnout.
  • Hiding burnout symptoms from your coordinator — they can adjust caseload only if they know.
  • Pretending one's friend would not become a problem to mediate or counsel — dual-relationship traps are common.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "describe boundary issues including dual relationships, gifts, social-media contact, and burnout."
