CHY4U — World History since the Fifteenth Century, Grade 12 University Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Canadian and World Studies (2015, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CHY4U  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: any university or university/college preparation course in Canadian and world studies, English, or social sciences and humanities

1. Course Description

This course traces major developments and events in world history since approximately 1450. Students will explore social, economic, and political changes, the historical roots of contemporary issues, and the role of conflict and cooperation in global interrelationships. They will extend their ability to apply the concepts of historical thinking and the historical inquiry process, including the interpretation and analysis of evidence, when investigating various forces that shaped world history. Students will develop their understanding of how individuals, organizations, and nations have contributed to conflict, peace, and prosperity around the world.

3. Historical Thinking Concepts (Seixas & Morton)

ConceptApplication in CHY4U
Historical SignificanceJudging which events, people, and developments deserve attention (e.g., why Luther's 95 Theses are more historically significant than thousands of contemporary sermons).
Cause and ConsequenceIdentifying long-term and short-term causes, intended and unintended consequences (e.g., causes of the First World War; consequences of the Columbian Exchange).
Continuity and ChangeRecognising what changed and what persisted across a period (e.g., continuities of monarchy across the long 18th century; change in European political legitimacy after 1789).
Historical PerspectiveReconstructing the worldview of people in past contexts without imposing present values (e.g., 17th-century religious framing of toleration vs heresy).
The Ethical DimensionMaking fair, evidence-based judgements about past actions, e.g., the Atlantic slave trade, residential schools, the Holocaust, decolonisation.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Historical Inquiry and Skill Development: Historians construct interpretations of the past from limited, fragmented, and often biased sources by applying historical thinking concepts and a disciplined inquiry process.
  • Strand B — 1450 to 1650 (Renaissance, Reformation, First Global Age): The Italian Renaissance, the Protestant Reformation, the Columbian Exchange, and the early scientific revolution remade European societies and tied them — often violently — to peoples across four continents.
  • Strand C — 1650 to 1789 (Absolutism and Enlightenment): Absolutist and constitutional states, Atlantic slave economies, and Enlightenment thought reshaped the political legitimacy, economic life, and intellectual horizons of the early-modern world.
  • Strand D — 1789 to 1914 (Revolutions and Industry): Political revolutions, the industrial revolution, the rise of nationalism, abolitionism, and the new imperialism produced both unprecedented prosperity and unprecedented violence on a global scale.
  • Strand E — 1914 to 1945 (World Wars and Ideologies): Two world wars, the Russian Revolution, the Great Depression, fascism, and the Holocaust represent the catastrophic working-out of forces unleashed in the long 19th century.
  • Strand F — 1945 to the Present: The Cold War, decolonisation, civil-rights movements, globalisation, the digital revolution, and the climate crisis shape today's interconnected world; the past continues to structure present conflicts.

5. Strand A — Historical Inquiry and Skill Development (embedded across all units)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Historical Inquiry: use the historical inquiry process and the concepts of historical thinking when investigating aspects of world history since the fifteenth century;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills developed through historical investigation, and identify careers in which these skills might be useful.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in world history since the fifteenth century;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources relevant to their investigations (e.g., evaluate authors' purpose, perspective, expertise);
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence and information relevant to their investigations using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for historical inquiry;
  • A1.6 evaluate evidence and draw conclusions about issues, events, and/or developments in world history since the fifteenth century;
  • A1.7 communicate their ideas, arguments, and conclusions using various formats and styles, as appropriate for the intended audience and purpose;
  • A2.1 describe ways in which historical investigation can help them develop skills, including the essential skills in the Ontario Skills Passport, that can be transferred to the world of work.
Inquiry focus: primary-source corroboration; bias and perspective; historiographical debate; the formal research essay with footnotes (Chicago / Notes-Bibliography).

6. Strand B — 1450 to 1650 (Unit 1)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: analyse social, economic, and political characteristics of selected societies in the West and in other regions of the world between c.1450 and 1650;
  • B2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse the development of, and interactions between, communities, including conflict and cooperation, in the period c.1450 to 1650;
  • B3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse how various factors contributed to a sense of identity, citizenship, and/or heritage in different societies in this period.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 analyse some of the main political and economic factors that contributed to the spread of European influence during this period (e.g., the printing press, gunpowder, joint-stock companies);
  • B1.4 analyse various social factors that contributed to changes in this period in the West and/or in other regions of the world (e.g., humanism, the Reformation, the Columbian Exchange);
  • B2.2 describe key factors that led to violent conflict (e.g., the Wars of Religion, the conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires, the trans-Atlantic slave trade);
  • B3.3 analyse ways in which religion contributed to a sense of identity for various peoples (e.g., Catholic vs Protestant identity, Sephardic Jewish diaspora after 1492).

7. Strand C — 1650 to 1789 (Unit 2)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: analyse social, economic, and political characteristics of selected societies in the West and in other regions of the world between 1650 and 1789;
  • C2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse interactions within and between communities in this period;
  • C3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse the contribution of various factors to a sense of identity, citizenship, and/or heritage between 1650 and 1789.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 describe key social characteristics of various societies in this period (e.g., the absolutist court under Louis XIV; Atlantic plantation slavery; Tokugawa social order);
  • C1.3 describe key political developments in various societies (e.g., constitutional monarchy in England after 1688; Qing consolidation under Kangxi; Petrine reforms in Russia);
  • C2.4 explain how various Enlightenment thinkers (e.g., Locke, Voltaire, Rousseau, Wollstonecraft) influenced political and social thought;
  • C3.2 analyse the impact of religious and secular ideas on identity and citizenship in this period (e.g., the secularising effects of Enlightenment reason).

8. Strand D — 1789 to 1914 (Unit 3)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: analyse social, economic, and political characteristics of selected societies in this period;
  • D2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse interactions within and between communities in this period;
  • D3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse contributions to identity, citizenship, and/or heritage in the long 19th century.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.2 analyse some of the main political factors that contributed to the spread of revolutionary ideas (e.g., the French Revolution, the Haitian Revolution, the 1848 revolutions);
  • D1.4 analyse the impact of the Industrial Revolution on various societies (e.g., factory labour, urbanisation, environmental change);
  • D2.5 analyse causes and consequences of the new imperialism (e.g., the Scramble for Africa, the British Raj, the Opium Wars);
  • D3.4 analyse the development of national identity in various states (e.g., Italian and German unification, Meiji Japan).

9. Strand E — 1914 to 1945 (Unit 4)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: analyse characteristics of selected societies during the era of the world wars;
  • E2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse global interactions in this period;
  • E3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse contributions to identity, citizenship, and/or heritage in this period.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 analyse the main causes of the First World War (long-term: militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism; short-term: the July Crisis);
  • E1.4 analyse the rise and consolidation of totalitarian regimes (e.g., Stalin's USSR, Nazi Germany, Imperial Japan);
  • E2.3 analyse the causes and global course of the Second World War (e.g., appeasement, the Pacific War, the Eastern Front);
  • E3.5 evaluate the historical significance of the Holocaust and other genocides of this period (e.g., the Armenian Genocide).

10. Strand F — 1945 to the Present (Unit 5)

Overall Expectations

  • F1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: analyse characteristics of selected societies from 1945 to the present;
  • F2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse global interactions in this period;
  • F3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse contributions to identity, citizenship, and/or heritage in the contemporary period.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • F1.2 analyse the origins and major events of the Cold War (e.g., the Iron Curtain speech, the Cuban Missile Crisis, the proxy wars);
  • F1.5 analyse the development of supra-national institutions (e.g., the UN, the European Union, the WTO);
  • F2.3 analyse decolonisation in Asia and Africa (e.g., partition of India 1947, Algerian independence, apartheid and its end in South Africa);
  • F3.4 analyse contemporary movements for human rights and recognition (e.g., civil rights, anti-apartheid, Indigenous rights, LGBTQ+ rights).

13. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, document-based questions, comparative essays, seminars, multimedia presentations, and historical-inquiry tasks. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of the Research Essay (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

14. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (timeline exercises, primary-source annotation, peer-review of essay drafts, reflection journals).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., diagnostic on prior chronology, source-reading skills, essay-structure check).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, document-based questions, formal essays, oral seminars, the research essay, and the final exam).

15. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (textbook excerpts, primary-source readings, documentary film, mapping, museum visits), multiple means of engagement (student-selected research-essay topic, choice of regional focus, choice of presentation form), and multiple means of expression (essay, podcast, video, infographic, museum-style exhibit) to meet the needs of diverse learners and to honour the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools.

16. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CHY4U for sustaining the multi-week research essay, for managing extensive primary-source reading, and for participating in Socratic seminars on contested ethical questions.
