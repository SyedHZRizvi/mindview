⚔️ 4.1 The Causes of War — Structural Theories
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

International relations scholars classically distinguish three "levels of analysis" of war causation (Kenneth Waltz, Man, the State, and War, 1959): the individual level (the psychology and decisions of leaders), the state level (regime type, economic structure, internal politics), and the system level (the structure of the international order — anarchy, polarity, alliance dynamics). Different schools of international relations emphasise different levels: classical realism focuses on individual leaders; liberal theories focus on state-level factors (democratic peace, economic interdependence); structural realism (Waltz, Mearsheimer) focuses on the international system.

The security dilemma (John Herz 1950, Robert Jervis 1978) is one of the most powerful structural explanations. In an anarchic international system (no central authority above states), every state must provide for its own security. When State A increases its defensive capability, State B cannot be sure whether the action is purely defensive or preparation for attack; B therefore increases its own capability; A then sees B's response as confirming threat. The result is an arms race between states that may both prefer peace. The 2014–24 NATO–Russia dynamic over Ukraine illustrates the security dilemma — NATO's eastern expansion was framed by NATO as defensive; framed by Russia as offensive encirclement; each side took actions that confirmed the other's worst interpretation.

Resource conflict theories explain wars driven by competition for material resources — oil, water, minerals, arable land. Michael Klare's Resource Wars (2001) tracks the role of oil in 20th-century conflict (Gulf War 1990–91; the Iraq War 2003 oil-justification debate). The "minerals curse" or "resource curse" literature (Paul Collier, Daron Acemoglu) explores why countries with abundant natural resources often have worse governance, higher conflict rates, and lower growth — diamond conflicts in Sierra Leone and Angola; coltan in the Democratic Republic of Congo; oil in Nigeria and Venezuela. Climate change is producing new resource conflicts (water in the Sahel, food in Yemen) and reshaping geopolitics in the Arctic, where Canada, Russia, the U.S., Norway, and Denmark have overlapping claims to seabed minerals and shipping routes.

Ethnic nationalism and identity conflicts explain civil and inter-state wars where identity claims (national, ethnic, religious, sectarian) mobilise populations. Donald Horowitz's Ethnic Groups in Conflict (1985) is the classical text; subsequent work (Stuart Kaufman, Andreas Wimmer) has refined the theory. The 1990s saw multiple ethnic-nationalist conflicts (Yugoslav wars 1991–95 and 1998–99; Rwanda 1994; Chechen wars 1994–96 and 1999–2009; Eritrea-Ethiopia 1998–2000); contemporary versions include the Russia-Ukraine war (Russian nationalism framing Ukrainian identity as constructed and the Donbas as Russian); India-Pakistan tensions over Kashmir; the Myanmar military's Rohingya operations 2017–. Ethnic nationalism is not a "primordial" force but a political construction — leaders activate identity claims to mobilise supporters and demonise opponents.

  • Levels of analysis (Waltz) — individual, state, and system levels of explaining war.
  • Security dilemma (Herz, Jervis) — defensive actions by one state appear offensive to others; produces arms races.
  • Resource conflict — wars driven by competition for material resources (oil, water, minerals).
  • Ethnic nationalism — identity-based mobilisation that can drive civil and inter-state war.
  • Democratic peace theory — claim that mature democracies do not fight one another (Doyle, Russett); contested but influential.

Causation Analysis — The Russia-Ukraine War (2014–present)

Apply the three levels of analysis. Individual level: Vladimir Putin's personal worldview — neo-imperial Russian nationalism (the 2021 essay "On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians" denied Ukrainian distinctiveness); his post-2008 trajectory from Georgia to Crimea to the 2022 full invasion suggests a long-developing intent. State level: Russia's authoritarian regime structure (no elite checks on executive war-making); economic dependence on hydrocarbon exports; the political utility of "external enemy" framing for domestic legitimacy. System level: the security dilemma (NATO expansion since 1999 framed by Russia as encirclement); the unipolar/post-unipolar transition; the energy and minerals economy. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: largest European inter-state war since 1945; reshaped European security; revitalised NATO; ended the Russian-European energy interdependence; reshaped Canadian foreign policy (Strong, Secure, Engaged update; LAVs to Ukraine; Operation Reassurance Baltic deployment). Objectives and results: Russia's stated objective was "demilitarisation and denazification" of Ukraine; results include Russia's loss of moral standing, sanctions costs, military attrition, and an expanded NATO (Finland 2023, Sweden 2024). Stability and change: the war has produced fundamental change in European security architecture. Political perspective: realist scholars (Mearsheimer) blame NATO expansion; liberal scholars (Snyder, Applebaum) blame Russian imperialism; Ukrainian and Eastern European perspectives emphasise sovereignty. A Level-4 analysis names all four positions and all three levels.

  • Treating war as caused by single factors — major wars typically involve causes at multiple levels.
  • Confusing "ethnic conflict" with "ancient hatreds" — identity is politically constructed and historically contingent.
  • Forgetting the role of leadership and individual choice — the security dilemma describes a structure, but states can choose how to respond to it.
  • Treating democratic peace theory as universally accepted — it is contested (early-democracy cases, definition of "democracy," dyad scope).
  • Underestimating the role of misperception — Robert Jervis's work on perception and misperception in international politics emphasises this.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "analyse the structural causes of war (security dilemma, resource competition, ethnic nationalism, ideological hostility, state failure)."

⚖️ 4.2 Just War Theory — Jus ad Bellum, Jus in Bello, Jus post Bellum
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Just war theory is the ethical-political framework developed over two millennia to evaluate whether and how war can be morally justified. The tradition originates with Cicero's De Officiis (c. 44 BCE) and was developed by Augustine (4th–5th c.), Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologica, 13th c.), Francisco de Vitoria (16th c.), Hugo Grotius (De Jure Belli ac Pacis, 1625), and Michael Walzer (Just and Unjust Wars, 1977 — the modern foundational text). The tradition is internally diverse but converges on a common structure: three sets of criteria addressing different moments of conflict.

Jus ad bellum — the conditions for justly going to war. The classical list comprises: just cause (typically self-defence against armed aggression, or in some contemporary formulations, humanitarian intervention); legitimate authority (the war is declared by a sovereign authority, today often understood as the UN Security Council or a recognised state acting in self-defence); right intention (the war aims at peace and justice, not at conquest, revenge, or domination); last resort (peaceful alternatives have been exhausted); proportionality (the expected good outweighs the expected harm); reasonable chance of success (the war is not futile slaughter). Each criterion has been contested in practice. The 2003 Iraq War was justified by the U.S. as preventive self-defence; most international lawyers and many philosophers considered it failed multiple criteria (just cause, last resort, legitimate authority through UN Security Council).

Jus in bello — the conduct of war once begun. Two principles dominate: discrimination (non-combatants — civilians, prisoners of war, the wounded — must be protected and not directly targeted) and proportionality (the means used must be proportionate to the military objective). These principles are codified in international humanitarian law (IHL): the four Geneva Conventions (1949) and their three Additional Protocols (1977, 2005). The 1977 Additional Protocols include explicit protections for civilians and prohibitions on attacks that may cause "incidental loss of civilian life or damage to civilian objects, or a combination thereof, which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated." The Israel-Hamas war from October 2023 has produced extensive jus in bello debate, including ICJ provisional measures (January 2024) and ICC arrest warrants (November 2024).

Jus post bellum — the conditions for a just peace after a conflict ends. This relatively newer branch of just war theory (developed by Brian Orend, Michael Walzer's Arguing About War, 2004) addresses: war termination (when and how to end fighting); peace settlements (proportional rather than vindictive); reconstruction obligations; accountability for war crimes; reparations. The unfinished post-2003 reconstruction of Iraq; the post-2001 Afghanistan reconstruction (and 2021 collapse); the proposed post-conflict trajectories for Ukraine and Gaza — all are jus post bellum questions. Justice after war is at least as difficult as justice in starting or fighting it.

  • Jus ad bellum — justice of going to war (just cause, authority, intention, last resort, proportionality, chance of success).
  • Jus in bello — justice in war (discrimination and proportionality).
  • Jus post bellum — justice after war (peace terms, reconstruction, accountability).
  • Michael Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars (1977) — the foundational modern just-war text.
  • "Supreme emergency" exception — Walzer's controversial argument that in extreme cases (Nazi victory in 1940) the discrimination principle might be relaxed.

Ethical Analysis — The 1999 Kosovo Intervention

NATO's 78-day air campaign against the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (24 March – 10 June 1999) was conducted without UN Security Council authorisation (blocked by Russian and Chinese veto) in response to Serbian forces' ethnic cleansing of Kosovar Albanians. Apply the just war framework. Jus ad bellum: Just cause — protecting an oppressed population from large-scale ethnic violence (most just-war theorists, including Walzer, accept humanitarian intervention as a just cause). Legitimate authority — disputed; some argue NATO collective action satisfied authority; others argue UN authorisation is required. Right intention — generally judged genuine. Last resort — Rambouillet negotiations had failed; though some argue diplomacy was not exhausted. Proportionality — debated; the air-only campaign was less proportional than ground engagement might have been. Reasonable chance of success — debated; the war lasted longer than expected. Jus in bello: NATO used precision-guided munitions and largely respected discrimination, though incidents (the Chinese embassy bombing 7 May 1999; the Grdelica train bombing 12 April; civilian casualties estimated at 488–528) were debated. Jus post bellum: Kosovo declared independence 2008; recognised by ~100 states; ICJ Advisory Opinion 2010 said declaration did not violate international law; status remains unresolved (not recognised by Russia, China, Spain, or Serbia). The Independent International Commission on Kosovo (2000, chaired by Richard Goldstone) concluded the intervention was "illegal but legitimate" — a phrase that captures the tension between legality (UN Charter) and morality (humanitarian protection). A Level-4 analysis applies all three branches of just-war theory explicitly.

  • Treating just-war theory as a checklist rather than as a structured framework for moral reasoning.
  • Confusing "just war" with "legal war" — many wars are legal but unjust; some wars are just but technically illegal.
  • Forgetting the discrimination principle in jus in bello — even justly initiated wars must be fought justly.
  • Treating jus post bellum as separate from jus ad bellum and jus in bello — they are linked; just causes require just terminations.
  • Assuming pacifism is the only morally serious alternative to just-war theory — some thinkers (Christopher Coker, the "tragic" school) emphasise the impossibility of fully just wars while accepting that some are required.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "examine the principles of just war theory and their limits in contemporary conflicts."

📜 4.3 International Humanitarian Law — Geneva Conventions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

International humanitarian law (IHL), also called the "law of armed conflict," is the body of treaty and customary law that regulates conduct during war. It rests on four principles: (1) distinction between combatants and civilians; (2) proportionality in the use of force; (3) military necessity as the only legitimate purpose of force; and (4) humanity — the limit on unnecessary suffering. IHL is older than human-rights law (the 1864 First Geneva Convention preceded the UDHR by 84 years) and applies in armed conflict situations where peacetime human-rights law may be partially suspended.

The four Geneva Conventions of 1949 codified IHL after WWII. GC I protects wounded and sick soldiers in the field; GC II extends those protections to wounded, sick, and shipwrecked at sea; GC III protects prisoners of war; GC IV protects civilians in time of war, including in occupied territory. The four Conventions have been universally ratified — 196 states — making them among the most universally accepted treaties in international law. The 1977 Additional Protocols (I — international armed conflict; II — non-international armed conflict) extended protections and codified the principle that civilians and civilian objects must not be the object of attack. Additional Protocol III (2005) recognised the Red Crystal as a protective emblem.

The Geneva-Convention framework has been challenged by post-2001 conflicts in which one or more parties is not a state (al-Qaeda, ISIS, the Taliban, Hamas, Hezbollah). The 2002 U.S. position that members of al-Qaeda were "unlawful combatants" outside the Geneva system was widely criticised and produced the 2006 SCOTUS decision Hamdan v. Rumsfeld requiring Common Article 3 protections. The contemporary debates address (i) cyber operations as armed conflict (the Tallinn Manual processes 1.0 2013, 2.0 2017, 3.0 forthcoming); (ii) autonomous weapons systems and the obligation of human judgment in lethal decisions; (iii) targeted killings outside declared zones of armed conflict (drone strikes 2002–24, particularly U.S. strikes in Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia); (iv) treatment of internally displaced persons and forced migration.

Canada's IHL position is shaped by its membership in NATO (since 1949), the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982), and the post-1998 Rome Statute commitments. The Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act (S.C. 2000, c. 24) implemented Canada's Rome Statute obligations and gave Canadian courts universal jurisdiction over war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide regardless of where committed. The 2005 Munyaneza case (Quebec Superior Court, the first prosecution under the new act, addressing the 1994 Rwandan genocide) and subsequent prosecutions established that Canadian courts can try perpetrators of mass atrocities. Canada has not, however, fully implemented all IHL protections in domestic law (e.g., the 1980 Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons protocols).

  • Four Geneva Conventions (1949) — universal IHL treaties covering wounded soldiers, sea, POWs, and civilians.
  • Additional Protocols I and II (1977) — extended IHL to international and non-international armed conflicts.
  • Common Article 3 — the "mini-convention" applicable to all armed conflicts including civil wars; minimum humane treatment.
  • Distinction principle — combatants must be distinguished from civilians; civilians not directly targeted.
  • Rome Statute (1998) — created the International Criminal Court; Canada ratified 2000 and implemented domestically through the Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act.

IHL Application — Civilian Targeting in the 2023–25 Gaza War

The 7 October 2023 Hamas attack on Israel (killing ~1,200 and taking ~250 hostages) and the subsequent Israeli military operation in Gaza (Palestinian Health Ministry estimates ~46,000+ deaths by January 2025) have produced extensive IHL debate. Apply the framework. Distinction: Hamas's deliberate targeting of Israeli civilians on 7 October was a clear violation. Israel's operations in Gaza — strikes on schools, hospitals, refugee camps, residential buildings — have been argued by Israel as targeting Hamas military infrastructure within civilian areas (a Hamas violation of distinction by placing forces among civilians, but not eliminating Israeli targeting obligations); argued by Palestinian, UN, and humanitarian organisations as systematic violations through over-broad targeting. The ICJ January 2024 provisional measures order in South Africa v. Israel required Israel to prevent acts that could amount to genocide; the ICC issued arrest warrants for senior Israeli and Hamas figures in November 2024. Proportionality: the assessment depends on whether the incidental harm is "excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated" — a context-specific calculation hotly disputed. Military necessity: military targets must be defined narrowly; "general damage to the enemy" is not military necessity. Humanity: the ICRC and UN OCHA have documented severe deprivation of food, water, and medical care. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: tests the resilience of IHL and the post-1945 international order. Objectives and results: each party's stated objectives (Hamas destruction; Hamas's "axis of resistance" / "Day After" planning) diverge sharply from observed humanitarian outcomes. Stability and change: the conflict has shaken global confidence in international humanitarian institutions. Political perspective: Israeli, Palestinian, Arab, Iranian, U.S., European, and Canadian perspectives differ substantially. A Level-4 analysis maintains the IHL framework while recognising the political contestation.

  • Confusing IHL with human-rights law — they overlap but have different scopes (armed-conflict-specific vs. peacetime-and-conflict).
  • Treating IHL as a "checklist" — proportionality and necessity are context-specific judgments.
  • Forgetting that IHL applies to all parties in conflict, including non-state actors and rebel groups.
  • Underestimating the ICC's role — the 2002 court can try individuals for war crimes when domestic courts cannot or will not.
  • Treating Geneva-Convention "civilians" as a simple category — modern conflicts blur the combatant/civilian line (insurgents in civilian clothes, civilian-targeting militias, drone-strike kill lists).

🛡️ 4.4 The Responsibility to Protect (R2P)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) is a normative framework that re-conceives sovereignty as conditional on a state's protection of its own population from mass atrocities. R2P was developed by the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS), chaired by Gareth Evans and Mohamed Sahnoun, which reported in December 2001 (The Responsibility to Protect) on a Canadian government initiative led by Foreign Affairs Minister Lloyd Axworthy. The Commission's central conceptual move was to shift the international focus from a "right of humanitarian intervention" (which had been controversial since Kosovo 1999) to a "responsibility" lying first with the sovereign state itself and only secondarily with the international community.

R2P was unanimously adopted by the UN General Assembly at the 2005 World Summit (paragraphs 138 and 139 of the Outcome Document). It has three "pillars": (1) each state has the responsibility to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity; (2) the international community has the responsibility to assist states in fulfilling that responsibility; (3) the international community has the responsibility to take collective action through the Security Council, "should peaceful means be inadequate and national authorities manifestly fail to protect their populations." The third pillar is contested — it implies (but does not require) coercive action including military force, and its invocation requires Security Council authorisation under Chapter VII.

R2P's flagship test case was the 2011 Libya intervention. After Muammar Gaddafi's forces threatened Benghazi with apparent atrocity, UN Security Council Resolution 1973 (17 March 2011) authorised "all necessary measures" to protect civilians. NATO's air campaign (Operation Unified Protector, March-October 2011) prevented the immediate atrocity but expanded into regime change, leading to Gaddafi's death (20 October 2011) and a decade of civil war and state collapse in Libya. The Libya case produced what is sometimes called "R2P backlash": Russia and China, which had abstained on Resolution 1973, have since vetoed multiple Security Council R2P actions (Syria 2011–24, sometimes citing the Libyan precedent as evidence that R2P is a Trojan horse for Western regime change).

Since 2011, R2P has been invoked rhetorically in many crises (Syria 2011–, South Sudan 2013–18, Myanmar 2017–, Yemen 2015–, Ethiopia 2020–22, Ukraine 2022–, Sudan 2023–) but with very limited collective coercive action. The "responsibility while protecting" Brazilian initiative (2011) tried to refine R2P with greater accountability mechanisms; subsequent diplomacy has not produced consensus. Canada's role has been substantial: as one of the doctrine's architects (Axworthy initiative); as host of the ICISS; as the country that contributed the original "human security" frame from which R2P grew. Canada has, however, struggled to translate doctrinal advocacy into substantive coercive action — Canadian forces participated in Libya (CF-18 strike operations) but not subsequently. A Grade-12 university-prep analysis recognises both the moral seriousness and the practical limits of R2P.

  • R2P (Responsibility to Protect) — sovereignty-as-responsibility framework for mass atrocity prevention.
  • ICISS Report (2001) — Canadian-led commission that developed R2P.
  • 2005 World Summit Outcome Document — paragraphs 138-139 endorsed R2P at the General Assembly.
  • Three pillars — state responsibility; international assistance; collective response.
  • Libya 2011 — flagship R2P intervention under UN Security Council Resolution 1973.

R2P Analysis — Syria 2011–24

The Syrian civil war began with peaceful pro-democracy protests in March 2011 and escalated rapidly. Despite documented mass atrocities (chemical weapons use 2013, 2017, 2018; Aleppo siege 2012-16; Eastern Ghouta operations 2013, 2018; estimated 500,000+ war-related deaths; ~13 million displaced including ~6 million refugees), no UN Security Council R2P military response was authorised. Russia (15+ Security Council vetoes) and China (multiple vetoes) blocked coercive action. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: the most consequential R2P failure in the doctrine's history; demonstrated the limit of UNSC consensus when permanent members back opposing sides. Objectives and results: stated R2P objective was civilian protection; the result was a decade of unprotected atrocities, a partial Russian-Iranian-Turkish-U.S.-Israeli proxy intervention, the December 2024 fall of Assad to Hayat Tahrir al-Sham, and continuing humanitarian crisis. Stability and change: confirmed the post-Libya stalemate; produced caution about future R2P invocations. Political perspective: humanitarian advocates see Syria as a moral catastrophe and a doctrinal failure; realist scholars see it as confirmation that great-power politics overrides normative frameworks; Russian and Chinese perspectives saw Western support for Syrian opposition as illegitimate intervention. A Level-4 analysis names all four positions and recognises that R2P's limits do not invalidate its moral case but do constrain its enforcement.

  • Treating R2P as a "duty to intervene militarily" — pillar 3 is conditional and last-resort.
  • Confusing R2P with "humanitarian intervention" — R2P deliberately reframes intervention through the lens of state responsibility.
  • Forgetting Canada's role in R2P's origin — the Axworthy initiative was a substantive Canadian foreign-policy contribution.
  • Treating R2P as a finished doctrine — it continues to evolve through state practice, UNGA debate, and academic refinement.
  • Underestimating the Security Council veto power — even unanimous moral judgment cannot override a P5 veto.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "analyse the doctrine of the responsibility to protect (R2P)."

💔 4.5 Case Studies — Rwanda, Bosnia, and Ukraine
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Rwanda (April–July 1994). Over 100 days, an estimated 800,000–1,000,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu were murdered by Hutu Power militias and government forces. The genocide had been planned since at least 1990, organised through "Hutu Power" propaganda (Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines, RTLM), and was triggered on 6 April 1994 by the shooting-down of President Habyarimana's plane. The United Nations Assistance Mission for Rwanda (UNAMIR), under Canadian commander Major-General Roméo Dallaire, had foreseen the genocide (the famous 11 January 1994 "genocide fax" to UN HQ) but was prevented from acting by the Department of Peacekeeping Operations under Kofi Annan and by Security Council reluctance (after the October 1993 Mogadishu losses). The international failure to prevent or stop the genocide is one of the founding traumas of post-Cold-War international relations. Dallaire's Shake Hands with the Devil (2003) and the 1999 Carlsson Report are essential sources. The genocide was a direct catalyst for ICTR (1994), R2P (2001), and ongoing African-Union peace operations.

Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992–95). The Yugoslav wars produced 100,000+ deaths, ~2 million displaced, and the largest mass atrocity in Europe since 1945 — the July 1995 Srebrenica genocide, in which Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić systematically executed 8,372 Bosnian Muslim men and boys. UNPROFOR (UN Protection Force, 1992–95) had declared Srebrenica a "safe area" in 1993; Dutch UN troops (Dutchbat) were present but vastly outgunned and stood aside. The September–October 1995 NATO Operation Deliberate Force (without UN Security Council authorisation) finally compelled Serb forces to the negotiating table. The Dayton Accords (November 1995) established the Bosnia-Herzegovina state with three constituent peoples and complex consociational institutions that remain in place but increasingly strained as of 2025. The ICTY (1993–2017) tried 161 people for war crimes including Slobodan Milošević (died in custody 2006), Radovan Karadžić (40-year sentence), and Ratko Mladić (life sentence). The 2007 ICJ ruling in Bosnia v. Serbia held that genocide had occurred at Srebrenica but Serbia was not directly responsible — a controversial finding.

Ukraine (2014–present). The Russian annexation of Crimea (March 2014, the first unilateral border change in Europe since 1945) and the Donbas war (2014–22) preceded Russia's February 2022 full invasion. As of 2025, the war has produced ~500,000+ Russian casualties, ~80,000+ confirmed Ukrainian military deaths plus tens of thousands of civilian deaths, ~6.4 million Ukrainian refugees, and ~3.7 million internally displaced. Documented international humanitarian law violations include the Bucha killings (March 2022), the Mariupol siege (February-May 2022), the Olenivka POW deaths (July 2022), the Kakhovka dam destruction (June 2023), and ongoing attacks on civilian infrastructure. The ICC issued arrest warrants for President Putin (March 2023, child deportations) and other Russian officials. Canada has provided ~$22 billion in military, humanitarian, and economic assistance through 2024; ~210,000 Ukrainians arrived under CUAET; the Royal Canadian Navy and CAF participate in Operation Reassurance (NATO eastern flank).

The three cases together illustrate the trajectory of international responses to mass atrocity. Rwanda 1994: no response, despite warning. Bosnia 1992–95: belated and limited response, after the Srebrenica genocide. Ukraine 2022–: substantial response but constrained by nuclear-armed adversary. The continuity is that great-power dynamics constrain humanitarian action; the change is that civilian-protection norms have become more politically central even as their enforcement remains conditional. Canada's role across the three cases has shifted from peripheral (Rwanda — Dallaire as exception) to engaged (Bosnia — substantial CAF contribution) to substantial (Ukraine — major aid, training, and infrastructure support). A Level-4 analysis traces these patterns and asks what they imply for future atrocity prevention.

  • Rwandan genocide (April–July 1994) — 800,000+ deaths in 100 days; UNAMIR failure; Dallaire's warnings ignored.
  • Srebrenica genocide (July 1995) — 8,372 Bosnian Muslims murdered under UN "safe area" designation.
  • ICTY/ICTR — Ad-hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia (1993-2017) and Rwanda (1994-2015).
  • Dayton Accords (1995) — peace agreement ending Bosnian war; consociational institutions.
  • Russia-Ukraine war (2014–) — annexation of Crimea, 2022 full invasion; largest European war since 1945.

Comparative Case Analysis — Rwanda 1994 vs. Ukraine 2022

Compare the international response to two civilian-protection crises. Rwanda 1994: UNAMIR force of ~2,500 (reduced to 270 in May 1994); no Western military intervention; ~$20 million in international aid during the genocide; UN Security Council failed to use the word "genocide" until June 1994. Ukraine 2022–: no UN-mandated military intervention (Russian veto); but ~$300 billion+ in Western and other assistance ($60 billion US, $40 billion EU, $22 billion Canada); ~$280 billion+ in Russian central bank assets frozen; ICC arrest warrants for Putin; massive refugee resettlement (4 million in Poland alone, 210,000 in Canada under CUAET). Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: the divergent responses reflect changes in international norms, communications technology (live war coverage), Western political alignment, and the strategic interests of major powers. Objectives and results: each response was shaped by the perceived stakes for great powers — Rwanda was peripheral, Ukraine is central to European security. Stability and change: civilian protection has moved from rhetorical commitment (1994) to substantial collective response (2022) when great-power interests align. Political perspective: African scholars (Mahmood Mamdani, Adam Branch) emphasise the racial dimensions of these divergent responses; realist scholars emphasise strategic interest; humanitarian advocates emphasise the protection imperative. A Level-4 analysis maintains the comparison while naming the analytical perspectives.

  • Treating Rwanda as a simple "international failure" — the failure was structured (UNSC reluctance, the post-Mogadishu hangover, US policy explicitly to avoid intervention).
  • Confusing genocide (specific intent to destroy a group) with crimes against humanity (widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population) — both are heinous; the legal definitions differ.
  • Treating Srebrenica as the only Bosnian atrocity — the wider Bosnian war involved widespread ethnic cleansing, mass rape, and detention.
  • Reading the Ukraine response as evidence that R2P "works now" — the Ukraine response is bilateral and coalition-based, not UN-mandated R2P.
  • Missing the role of communications technology — live coverage of Ukraine vs. delayed coverage of Rwanda has shaped political response.

🍁 4.6 Canada's Role — Peacekeeping, NATO, NORAD
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada's contemporary defence and foreign-policy posture rests on three pillars: NATO (since 1949), NORAD (since 1958), and UN peacekeeping (since 1956). Each has shaped and been shaped by Canadian political choices over the past three-quarters of a century. Canada's identity as a "middle power" is constituted partly through these institutional commitments — the 2017 Strong, Secure, Engaged defence policy and 2024 Defence Policy Update articulate the contemporary version.

NATO (1949–). Canada was a founding member of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization. External Affairs Minister Lester Pearson and Under-Secretary Hume Wrong negotiated the 1949 Washington Treaty; Article 2 — the "Canadian article" — explicitly called for economic and cultural cooperation, reflecting Canadian discomfort with a purely military alliance. NATO's Article 5 collective-defence clause has been invoked once — by Canada and other allies after the 9/11 attacks (12 September 2001), leading to Canadian involvement in Afghanistan 2001–14 (Operation Athena, ~40,000 troops rotated, 158 Canadian dead). Canada has met its 2% of GDP defence-spending NATO target only briefly in the late Cold War; current spending is ~1.4% (2024), with a 2032 target announced in 2024 to reach 2%. Canada leads the Latvia battle group in NATO's Enhanced Forward Presence (since 2017, recently expanded as part of Operation Reassurance).

NORAD (1958–). The North American Aerospace Defense Command is the binational US-Canada continental air-defence command, headquartered in Cheyenne Mountain, Colorado (with Canadian region HQ in Winnipeg). NORAD's mission has evolved from Cold War bomber detection (the DEW Line of 1950s arctic radars) to ballistic missile warning (since 1968) to maritime warning (since 2006) and now to cyber and space-domain awareness. The 2022 NORAD Modernization announcement committed $38.6 billion over 20 years to upgrade the Northern Approaches surveillance system, replacing the 1980s North Warning System with new over-the-horizon radar (the 2023 contract with Raytheon). Canadian Arctic sovereignty depends substantially on NORAD; the 2024 Defence Policy Update emphasises Arctic infrastructure investment.

UN Peacekeeping (1956–). Canadian peacekeeping was effectively invented by Lester Pearson during the 1956 Suez Crisis. When Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt over the nationalisation of the Suez Canal, Pearson (then External Affairs Minister) proposed the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) — the first armed UN peacekeeping mission. Pearson won the 1957 Nobel Peace Prize for this proposal. Subsequent Canadian peacekeeping included Congo (1960–64), Cyprus (1964-93), Sinai (1973-79), former Yugoslavia (1992-95), Rwanda (1993-94), Haiti (multiple deployments), East Timor (1999-2000), and Afghanistan (2003-14, transitioning to combat). Canadian peacekeeping participation peaked in 1993 at ~3,300 deployed and has declined sharply — as of 2024, ~33 Canadian peacekeepers are deployed across UN missions, the lowest in Canadian history. The 2017 Vancouver Principles on peacekeeping training (especially on prevention of child-soldier recruitment) reflect Canadian engagement at the doctrinal level even as troop contributions have declined.

The contemporary debate over Canada's defence posture is sharp. Critics argue Canada free-rides on US security; supporters argue Canadian contributions to NATO, NORAD, UN peacekeeping, the Five Eyes intelligence alliance, and recent contributions (Operation Reassurance, training Ukrainians, Indo-Pacific deployments) reflect genuine commitment within Canada's fiscal capacity. The 2025 question is whether Canada will sustainably reach 2% of GDP on defence, what role Canada will play in the evolving Indo-Pacific strategic environment, and how Canadian Arctic capabilities will be modernised in the context of climate-driven Arctic accessibility and Russian Northern Fleet activity.

  • NATO Article 2 ("Canadian Article") — economic and cultural cooperation commitment; Pearson's contribution.
  • NATO Article 5 — collective defence (one attack on one is attack on all); invoked once, post-9/11.
  • NORAD (1958–) — US-Canada binational continental air defence command.
  • UN Emergency Force (UNEF, 1956) — Pearson's invention; first armed UN peacekeeping mission.
  • Strong, Secure, Engaged (2017) — Canada's defence policy framework; 2024 update extended commitments.

Foreign-Policy Analysis — Canada's Afghanistan Mission (2001-14)

Canada deployed to Afghanistan from October 2001 (initial Special Operations Joint Task Force 2 contribution) through 2014 (formal end of combat mission). Over 40,000 Canadian Forces members rotated through; 158 were killed and over 2,000 wounded. Canada commanded the multinational Kandahar Brigade 2006-11 and led the Provincial Reconstruction Team in Kandahar. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: the longest Canadian combat operation since the Korean War and the largest Canadian military deployment outside North America since WWII. Reshaped the CAF (purchase of leopard tanks, M777 howitzers, C-17 transport aircraft; reorganisation of equipment and doctrine); reshaped Canadian society (the Highway of Heroes commemorations 2002-2014; substantial post-deployment mental-health policy debate; the 2017 federal apology for veterans' issues). Objectives and results: stated objectives — defeat al-Qaeda and Taliban, build stable Afghan state, support international order; results — Taliban returned to power August 2021; al-Qaeda significantly degraded; humanitarian costs profound; institutional learning substantial. Stability and change: confirmed Canada's NATO commitment but raised questions about the limits of military intervention in state-building. Political perspective: military and security scholars assess achievements (CAF capability, NATO interoperability, regional intelligence) and limits; humanitarian critics emphasise civilian costs; Indigenous-veteran scholars (the 7% of CAF Afghanistan deployment that was Indigenous) note disproportionate burden. A Level-4 analysis names all four concepts and recognises that the mission was both substantively serious and ultimately limited.

  • Treating Canadian foreign policy as a single tradition — it has shifted between Pearsonian middle-power multilateralism, Cold-War alignment with the US, and post-2001 expeditionary combat operations.
  • Forgetting that Canadian peacekeeping declined sharply after 1995 — the Pearsonian image persists in public memory but not in current contribution.
  • Confusing NATO and the UN — they are distinct institutions with different membership, mandates, and authority.
  • Treating the 2% GDP defence target as objective — it is an alliance political commitment, not a substantive military analysis.
  • Underestimating the binational character of NORAD — Canadian sovereignty over Canadian airspace is jointly defended.
