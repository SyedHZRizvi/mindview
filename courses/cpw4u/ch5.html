🇺🇳 5.1 The United Nations System
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The United Nations was founded on 24 October 1945 by 51 original members upon the entry into force of the Charter signed in San Francisco on 26 June 1945. As of 2024, it has 193 member states. The UN was designed to address the failures of the League of Nations (1920–46) — the absence of major powers, the lack of enforcement, the inability to prevent the Second World War. The Charter's Preamble — "We the peoples of the United Nations determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war" — is the founding moral claim. The structure is set out in the 111 articles of the Charter.

The UN's six principal organs are: the General Assembly (every member has one vote; passes non-binding resolutions on most matters and binding decisions on UN administration and budget); the Security Council (15 members — 5 permanent with veto power: China, France, Russia, UK, USA — and 10 elected non-permanent for 2-year terms; binding decisions under Chapter VII can authorise sanctions or use of force); the Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) (54 members; coordinates economic, social, and related work); the Trusteeship Council (suspended 1994 after Palau's independence); the International Court of Justice (ICJ) (the principal judicial organ; in The Hague); and the Secretariat (the international civil service led by the Secretary-General, currently António Guterres since 2017).

The Security Council is the most consequential and most contested organ. Chapter VII (Articles 39-51) gives it primary responsibility for international peace and security, with authority to impose binding sanctions and authorise the use of force. The five permanent members' veto power (Article 27) was the price of Soviet-Western agreement in 1945; it ensures that no UN coercive action can proceed over the active opposition of any P5 member. As of 2024, vetoes have been cast 320+ times, with the Russian and US vetoes dominating in recent decades (Russia 32 times since 2011 on Syria/Ukraine; US frequently on Israel-Palestine). Reform proposals (the G4 — Brazil, Germany, India, Japan — and African Union's Ezulwini Consensus seeking permanent African seats) have been blocked by the P5 since they would have to authorise their own dilution.

The UN's specialised agencies address particular global issues. The World Health Organization (WHO, 1948), the UN Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO, 1945), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO, 1945), the International Labour Organization (ILO, 1919 — predates UN), the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA, 1957), the UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR, 1950), the UN Children's Fund (UNICEF, 1946), and the UN Development Programme (UNDP, 1965). These agencies have substantial mandates and budgets; their effectiveness varies with funding, leadership, and political support. Canada is a substantial contributor — currently ranked 8th in absolute UN system contributions (~$2.4 billion annually) and 18th per capita.

  • UN Charter (1945) — founding treaty; 111 articles; signed San Francisco; entered force October 1945.
  • Six principal organs — General Assembly, Security Council, ECOSOC, Trusteeship Council (suspended), ICJ, Secretariat.
  • P5 (Permanent Five) — China, France, Russia, UK, USA; each with veto under Article 27.
  • Chapter VII — Security Council powers regarding threats to peace and acts of aggression.
  • Specialised agencies — WHO, UNESCO, FAO, ILO, IAEA, UNHCR, UNICEF, UNDP, et al.

Institutional Analysis — UN Security Council Reform Debate

Apply concepts of political thinking to the question of UNSC reform. Political significance: the UNSC is the only body in international law with binding authority to authorise coercive measures. Its current composition reflects 1945 geopolitics, not 21st-century power distribution (Germany, Japan, India, Brazil — none on UNSC permanently; whole African continent has no permanent seat). The continued exclusion of these states delegitimises the UNSC for substantial portions of the world's population. Objectives and results: reform initiatives have been intensive (G4 since 2004; the "Uniting for Consensus" group of Italy, Pakistan, South Korea, Mexico, Argentina; the African Union's Ezulwini Consensus 2005) but have not produced charter amendment. Charter amendment requires 2/3 of member states plus all P5 — making reform very hard. Stability and change: the current structure has produced both remarkable continuity (UNSC still exists, still functions) and major failures (Rwanda 1994, Syria 2011–24, Ukraine 2022–). Political perspective: G4 advocates see reform as completing post-colonial transition; P5 (especially China and Russia) see reform as threatening their privileged position; small states see reform as preserving a degraded multilateralism that nonetheless protects them. A Level-4 analysis identifies all four positions and recognises that reform is not just procedural but constitutive of legitimate global governance.

  • Treating UN General Assembly resolutions as binding — they are mostly recommendations except on UN administration.
  • Confusing the UN with global government — the UN is a treaty-based organisation of states, not a supranational government.
  • Forgetting the P5 veto — even unanimous global opinion cannot override a single P5 veto on UNSC matters.
  • Treating the UN as monolithic — its many bodies have different mandates and effectiveness.
  • Underestimating Canadian engagement — Canada is consistently among the top contributors despite low public visibility.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.1 — "analyse the structure and functioning of the United Nations system (General Assembly, Security Council, ICJ, ECOSOC, specialised agencies including WHO and UNHCR)."

⚖️ 5.2 International Courts and Trade Institutions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The International Court of Justice (ICJ), the principal judicial organ of the UN, sits in the Peace Palace in The Hague. It has 15 judges elected for nine-year terms by the UN General Assembly and Security Council (concurrent majorities required). Its jurisdiction has two branches: contentious cases between states that have consented to its jurisdiction (either by treaty clause or by ad-hoc consent) and advisory opinions requested by UN organs and specialised agencies. ICJ judgments are binding on parties (Article 94 UN Charter), but enforcement depends on the UNSC — a meaningful constraint given the P5 veto.

The ICJ has produced foundational judgments in international law: Corfu Channel (1949 — state responsibility for territory); Reservations to the Convention on Genocide (1951 — treaty law); Nicaragua v. United States (1986 — prohibition on use of force, US-Contra support); Bosnia v. Serbia (2007 — Genocide Convention obligations); the Wall Advisory Opinion (2004 — Israel-Palestine); and most recently South Africa v. Israel (provisional measures order, January 2024 — Genocide Convention application; advisory opinion on Israeli policies in Palestinian territory, July 2024). The ICJ's normative impact is real but its enforcement is conditional on political will of states and the Security Council. Canada has been a party to ICJ cases including the 1984 Gulf of Maine boundary case with the US.

The International Criminal Court (ICC) is separate from the UN. Created by the Rome Statute (signed 1998, entered force 2002), it sits in The Hague and prosecutes individuals (not states) for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression. As of 2024, 124 states are party (Canada signed 1998, ratified 2000); the US, China, Russia, India, and Israel are not parties. The ICC has indicted 53+ individuals; convictions include Thomas Lubanga (2012), Germain Katanga (2014), Jean-Pierre Bemba (2016, overturned 2018), Bosco Ntaganda (2019), and Dominic Ongwen (2021). Recent high-profile actions include the March 2023 Putin arrest warrant (Ukraine child deportations) and the November 2024 warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, former Defence Minister Yoav Gallant, and Hamas leaders. The ICC's authority is contested — Trump-era US sanctions on the ICC (2020-21, lifted 2021, partially reimposed 2024-25), Russian rejection of the Putin warrant, Israeli rejection of the Netanyahu warrant.

The World Trade Organization (WTO) grew out of the 1947 General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) and was established by the 1994 Marrakesh Agreement. With 164 members as of 2024, it administers global trade rules covering goods, services, and intellectual property. The WTO's most distinctive feature was its dispute settlement system — binding, time-bound, with appeals — which produced 600+ disputes between 1995 and 2024. The WTO has been substantially degraded since 2017, when the US under the Trump administration blocked appointments to the Appellate Body, leaving it without a quorum to hear appeals. The Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA, 2020) preserves limited appeal capacity for 51 members including Canada. Canada has been an active participant — both as complainant (e.g., the long softwood-lumber disputes with the US) and as respondent. The 2024 status quo is "WTO in stasis," with the Doha Development Round (2001–) effectively suspended and US-China trade frictions managed bilaterally rather than multilaterally.

  • ICJ — UN principal judicial organ; binds states; in The Hague.
  • ICC — independent court for individuals; created by 1998 Rome Statute.
  • WTO — established 1994 from GATT; trade rules; degraded dispute settlement since 2017.
  • Universal jurisdiction — concept that some crimes (genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, torture) can be prosecuted by any state.
  • Most-Favoured-Nation (MFN) principle — WTO requirement that trade benefits granted to one member be extended to all.

Institutional Analysis — The November 2024 ICC Warrants for Netanyahu and Gallant

On 21 November 2024, the ICC Pre-Trial Chamber issued arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defence Minister Yoav Gallant on charges of war crimes and crimes against humanity in Gaza (denial of humanitarian relief, intentional attacks on civilians), along with warrants for Hamas leaders Yahya Sinwar (later confirmed killed) and others. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: first ICC warrants against sitting Western-allied leaders; tests whether ICC obligations apply to senior officials of states with close Western ties. Objectives and results: stated objective is individual criminal accountability; results include political mobilisation (some EU members affirming they would arrest Netanyahu on their territory; the US sanctioning ICC officials in 2025); practical execution depends on Netanyahu's travel patterns and state cooperation. Stability and change: continues to test the post-1998 ICC project; may weaken or strengthen ICC authority depending on state responses. Political perspective: international-law scholars largely support the warrants on legal grounds; Israeli government and many Israeli scholars reject them as politicised; US under both administrations has criticised them; civil-society organisations support accountability; Palestinian and Arab states demand enforcement. Canada has not yet stated its position clearly. A Level-4 analysis names all four positions and recognises that ICC authority is being tested by exactly the kinds of cases the Rome Statute was designed to address.

  • Confusing the ICJ (states) with the ICC (individuals) — they are separate courts with different jurisdiction.
  • Treating ICJ/ICC judgments as automatically enforced — enforcement depends on state and UNSC action.
  • Forgetting the WTO Appellate Body's effective shutdown since 2017 — fundamentally changed the global trade regime.
  • Treating universal jurisdiction as well-established — it is recognised but politically constrained.
  • Underestimating Canada's role — Canada has been a substantial supporter of all three institutions (ICJ, ICC, WTO).

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.2 — "analyse the structure and functioning of regional and economic institutions (G7, G20, WTO, IMF, World Bank, NATO, OECD)."

🌡️ 5.3 Climate Governance — From Kyoto to Paris and Beyond
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Global climate governance rests on the 1992 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), signed at the Rio Earth Summit and ratified by 198 parties (effectively universal). The Convention establishes the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities" — all states must contribute, but developed states bear greater responsibility due to historical emissions. The Conference of the Parties (COP) meets annually (COP29 was held in Baku, Azerbaijan in November 2024; COP30 is scheduled for Belém, Brazil in November 2025).

The Kyoto Protocol (signed 1997, entered force 2005) was the first major binding climate treaty. It required developed (Annex I) countries to reduce emissions by an average of 5.2% below 1990 levels in the 2008-12 commitment period. The US (under both Clinton and Bush) signed but never ratified; Canada ratified in 2002 but withdrew in 2011 (the only state to do so) when it became clear that targets would be missed. The Protocol's "top-down" structure — internationally negotiated emission reduction targets — proved politically unsustainable in many countries, particularly when developing-country emissions (especially China and India) rose dramatically after 2000.

The Paris Agreement (signed December 2015, entered force November 2016) replaced the Kyoto structure with a "bottom-up" model based on nationally determined contributions (NDCs). Each state determines its own emission-reduction commitments; submits them every five years; commitments are required to represent progression; there is no enforcement mechanism. The Agreement's headline target is to "hold the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels." The 1.5°C target became increasingly difficult: 2024 was the first calendar year on record to exceed 1.5°C of warming relative to pre-industrial (per Copernicus Climate Change Service, January 2025). The Agreement's gap is therefore widening between aspiration and trajectory.

Canada's climate trajectory has been politically contested. Canada is a top-15 global emitter (~545 Mt CO2eq in 2023, ~14% reduction from 2005 peak) with substantial fossil-fuel exports (the oil sands). The federal Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act (2018) established a carbon-pricing backstop, upheld by the SCC in 2021 (Reference re GGPPA) as a valid POGG matter. The consumer-facing carbon levy was dismantled in 2025; the industrial carbon price (large emitters trading system, OBPS) remains. Canada's NDC commits to 40–45% below 2005 by 2030; the 2024 modelling suggests achievement is uncertain. The 2024 Federal Carbon Capture and Storage Investment Tax Credit and the LNG Canada Phase 1 (operational late 2024) illustrate the policy contradiction at the heart of Canadian climate policy: simultaneous expansion of fossil-fuel infrastructure and decarbonisation commitments. Indigenous-led climate governance (Indigenous-led conservation areas under the Pathway to Canada Target 1) and the 2024 Indigenous Climate Hub are emerging dimensions.

  • UNFCCC (1992) — foundational climate treaty; common but differentiated responsibilities.
  • Kyoto Protocol (1997, in force 2005) — first binding climate treaty; "top-down" targets; Canada withdrew 2011.
  • Paris Agreement (2015, in force 2016) — "bottom-up" NDCs; 1.5°C / well-below 2°C target.
  • Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) — state-by-state climate commitments under Paris.
  • Loss and Damage Fund (COP27 2022) — new financing mechanism for developing-country climate harm.

Climate Policy Analysis — Canada's Carbon Pricing Reversal (2024-25)

Canada's consumer-facing carbon levy was initially imposed in 2019 under the federal backstop, applied to provinces (Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, New Brunswick) without provincial pricing; expanded to all non-pricing provinces; survived the SCC Reference re GGPPA (2021); but became politically toxic by 2024 under the post-2022 Pierre Poilievre Conservative "Axe the Tax" campaign. In March 2025, the new Liberal government under Mark Carney suspended consumer carbon pricing. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: dismantles the most prominent piece of Canadian climate policy at the consumer level; represents political defeat of a flagship policy. Objectives and results: stated objective of climate pricing was demand-side decarbonisation through cost signals; observed result is reduced policy intensity and increased reliance on industrial carbon pricing (OBPS) and regulatory measures. Stability and change: shows how climate policy can be reversed within a political cycle even after constitutional vindication. Political perspective: climate-policy scholars (Mark Jaccard, Kathryn Harrison) emphasise the loss of an efficient instrument; affordability advocates emphasise relief for consumers; Conservative-aligned analysts emphasise democratic responsiveness. A Level-4 analysis recognises that climate policy reflects the intersection of climate science, economic theory, and political economy — and that political economy can override both science and theory.

  • Treating the Paris Agreement as enforceable — it has no enforcement mechanism; NDCs are politically binding only.
  • Confusing the UNFCCC with the IPCC — UNFCCC is the treaty body; IPCC (1988) is the scientific assessment body.
  • Forgetting that climate policy is constrained by federalism — provinces have substantial jurisdiction over energy and resource development.
  • Treating carbon pricing as the only climate policy tool — regulation, infrastructure investment, and electrification matter as much or more.
  • Underestimating Indigenous climate governance — increasingly central to Canadian climate policy through conservation, monitoring, and consent-based development.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.1 — "analyse global climate governance, including the UNFCCC, the Kyoto Protocol (1997), and the Paris Agreement (2015) with its NDC mechanism."

🏥 5.4 Global Health Governance and Pandemic Preparedness
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The World Health Organization (WHO), established 1948, is the UN specialised agency for international public health. Headquartered in Geneva with 194 member states, it coordinates international responses to disease outbreaks, sets global health norms (the International Health Regulations 2005), provides technical assistance, runs major eradication and immunisation campaigns, and serves as the standard-setting body for pharmaceuticals (the Essential Medicines List), antimicrobial resistance, and tobacco control (Framework Convention on Tobacco Control 2003).

The WHO's mandate is constrained by its dependence on member-state cooperation and funding. The 2005 International Health Regulations (IHR) require states to report potential public health emergencies of international concern (PHEIC). The WHO Director-General can declare a PHEIC and issue temporary recommendations, but compliance is voluntary. PHEICs declared include H1N1 (2009), polio resurgence (2014, ongoing), Ebola West Africa (2014-16), Zika (2016), Ebola DRC (2019), COVID-19 (declared 30 January 2020, ended 5 May 2023), and mpox (declared and ended multiple times 2022-24). The IHR is undergoing 2024-25 reform with proposed amendments and a parallel Pandemic Treaty negotiation that aims to strengthen capacity, equity, and compliance.

The COVID-19 pandemic (2020-23) was the most consequential global health event since the 1918-20 influenza pandemic. Estimated excess mortality 17-30+ million globally (WHO estimates ~14.9 million); economic costs $10+ trillion; profound impacts on supply chains, education, mental health, and political institutions. Canada's response included the 2020-22 CERB / EI overhaul ($82+ billion direct CERB plus CRB and other programs); the 2020-21 vaccine procurement (the most diverse portfolio internationally, securing 414 million doses for 38 million people); the 2021-23 vaccine donations to COVAX (~120 million doses); the National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) recommendations; and the 2023 Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) restructuring. The pandemic also exposed Canadian preparedness gaps — PPE stockpiling collapse, federal-provincial coordination challenges, and Indigenous community vulnerabilities.

The post-COVID global health architecture is being substantially restructured. The 2021 G7 announced the Pandemic Preparedness Partnership and the 100 Days Mission for vaccine and diagnostic development. The 2022 World Health Assembly initiated negotiations on a Pandemic Accord (under WHO Constitution Article 19), with goals to address access, surveillance, and accountability — negotiations remain ongoing through 2025 with substantial North-South tensions. The 2024 Pandemic Fund (hosted by World Bank, with WHO technical lead) has mobilised $2 billion+ for pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response. Canada has been a substantial contributor and convenor; the 2024 federal Pandemic Preparedness investment ($573 million over five years) addresses surveillance, vaccine manufacturing, and Indigenous health.

  • World Health Organization (WHO) — UN specialised agency since 1948; 194 member states.
  • International Health Regulations (IHR, 2005) — binding treaty requiring outbreak reporting and PHEIC mechanism.
  • Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) — WHO Director-General declaration triggering coordinated response.
  • COVAX — vaccine-equity facility 2020-23; partnered with Gavi and WHO; delivered ~2 billion doses.
  • Pandemic Accord — proposed binding treaty under WHO Article 19; negotiations 2022–25.

Health Policy Analysis — Vaccine Equity and TRIPS Waiver

During COVID-19, the global vaccine rollout was profoundly unequal. As of mid-2021, high-income countries had administered 2-3× more doses than low-income countries combined; by late 2022, ~70% of low-income population remained unvaccinated against COVID-19. South Africa and India proposed a TRIPS Agreement waiver at the WTO in October 2020 to allow generic production of patented vaccines and treatments. The proposal was supported by 100+ countries, including the US (after May 2021), but blocked by Germany, the UK, and Switzerland (the pharmaceutical-producing countries). A limited Ministerial Decision was adopted at WTO MC12 (June 2022) but covered only patents (not data exclusivity, trade secrets, or manufacturing know-how) and excluded therapeutics. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: tested whether the post-1995 WTO IP regime could accommodate emergency public-health needs; the limited outcome suggests intellectual-property protections are politically very durable. Objectives and results: stated objective was vaccine access; observed result was modest because manufacturing capacity, not patents alone, was the binding constraint. Stability and change: confirmed the IP regime but generated significant North-South political tension. Political perspective: pharmaceutical industry argued IP protection enables R&D investment; global-health advocates argued for emergency access; developing-country governments emphasised sovereignty over public health. Canada took a middle position, supporting expanded access but not full waiver. A Level-4 analysis names all four positions and recognises that "health emergency exceptions" to IP are politically conditional.

  • Treating WHO as having binding authority over states — it depends on voluntary compliance and political support.
  • Confusing the WHO Constitution with the IHR — both are binding instruments but with different scope and mechanisms.
  • Forgetting that vaccines depend on manufacturing capacity, not only patents — Africa CDC's 2022-25 vaccine-manufacturing investment addresses this.
  • Underestimating Indigenous health governance — the First Nations Health Authority (BC, 2013) and similar arrangements operate alongside provincial systems.
  • Treating pandemic preparedness as one-off — it requires sustained investment between crises.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.2 — "analyse global health governance, including the WHO, the IHR (2005), and the international response to COVID-19 (2020–23)."

🛂 5.5 Migration, Trade, and the Limits of Cooperation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Global migration is one of the most consequential and least-governed international issues. As of 2024, ~281 million people live outside their country of birth (~3.6% of world population). ~120+ million are forcibly displaced (refugees, asylum seekers, internally displaced persons) — the highest number ever recorded. The international migration regime is fragmented: refugees are governed by the 1951 Convention; the broader migration governance is partly under the 2018 Global Compact for Migration (non-binding) and the 2018 Global Compact on Refugees, partly through bilateral and regional agreements (the EU's Common European Asylum System; the post-2024 Pacific Migration regime).

Canada's distinctive approach combines selection (the points-based skilled-worker program, the 2024 Express Entry system, the Provincial Nominee Programs), family reunification (~30% of permanent residents), humanitarian admission (refugees, ~10-15%), and economic-immigrant streams (~60%). Canada admitted ~485,000 permanent residents in 2024 and a record ~1.2 million temporary residents (international students plus temporary workers). The 2023-24 Immigration Levels Plan revision reduced 2025-27 targets in response to concerns over housing pressure, healthcare strain, and labour-market integration. The 2024 federal cap on international study permits (~360,000, down from ~1 million in 2023) and tightened temporary-foreign-worker rules reflect the post-2023 policy turn.

Global trade governance has been substantially reshaped since 2017. The WTO remained nominally central but its Appellate Body was effectively shut down (US block on appointments); the Doha Round was abandoned; bilateral and regional deals proliferated. Major regional agreements include: USMCA (2020, replacing NAFTA 1994); CPTPP (2018, the post-US Pacific deal); CETA (Canada-EU Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement, 2017 provisional, 2024 fully ratified); Mercosur-EU (proposed, contested 2024-25); RCEP (Asia-Pacific 2022, includes China but not US or Canada); ACFTA (African Continental Free Trade Area, 2018, operational 2021). Canadian trade policy is increasingly trilateral (USMCA), pluri-lateral (CPTPP, CETA, AfCFTA observership), and bilateral (the 2024 Canada-Ukraine FTA modernisation, the proposed Canada-Indonesia CEPA). The 2024-25 Trump-administration "tariff politics" (10-25% tariffs threatened or imposed on Canadian and Mexican imports) test the durability of USMCA.

The limits of cooperation are most starkly visible in the post-2017 fracturing of the multilateral order. Climate cooperation has weakened (US withdrawal from Paris 2017-20, returned 2021; political contestation in many states). Trade cooperation has fragmented (WTO Appellate Body, US-China decoupling, tariff politics). Health cooperation has been tested (COVID-19 nationalism, vaccine inequity, slow Pandemic Accord). Refugee cooperation has been contested (EU Mediterranean policy, US-Mexico border policy, Australian offshore processing). Through all of this, Canada has maintained substantial multilateral engagement — UNHCR contributions, ICRC support, NATO commitments, CIDA (now Global Affairs Canada) development assistance ~$8 billion annually — but operates in a less hospitable global environment than the early 2010s.

  • Global Compact for Migration (2018) — non-binding UN framework; 152 countries endorsed.
  • Express Entry — Canada's electronic skilled-worker selection system; 2024 update introduced category-based draws.
  • USMCA (2020) — Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement; replaced NAFTA.
  • CPTPP (2018) — Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership; 11 members.
  • CETA (2017/2024) — Canada-EU Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement; provisional 2017, full 2024.

Trade Policy Analysis — Canada in a Fragmenting Trade System

Canada has signed or is party to 15 trade agreements covering 51 countries representing 1.5 billion consumers and ~$33 trillion combined GDP. The 2024-25 Trump-administration tariff threats (10-25% on Canadian goods, with effects on autos, lumber, dairy, and steel) test the durability of USMCA. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: Canada-US trade represents 65-75% of Canadian exports; the relationship is structurally asymmetric (Canada needs the US far more than vice versa). Tariff threats produce political and economic uncertainty even when not imposed. Objectives and results: Canadian objectives have been preservation of duty-free access, diversification (CETA, CPTPP, Indo-Pacific Strategy 2022), and rules-based dispute settlement; observed results include continued access but increased bilateral pressure and reduced multilateral protection. Stability and change: the post-2017 trade environment is dramatically less stable than the post-1995 WTO golden age; Canada's response combines diversification, internal-trade liberalisation (the post-2017 Canadian Free Trade Agreement), and tactical engagement with US administrations. Political perspective: trade economists emphasise efficiency losses from fragmentation; political economists emphasise distributional effects (winners and losers); national-security analysts emphasise strategic considerations (de-risking from China). A Level-4 analysis recognises all three lenses and notes that Canadian trade policy is increasingly shaped by both economic and security considerations.

  • Treating the WTO as still functioning normally — Appellate Body shutdown has fundamentally changed dispute settlement.
  • Confusing immigration (selection) with refugee protection (Convention obligations) — Canada has both streams but they operate differently.
  • Forgetting the asymmetry of Canada-US trade — Canada has ~10% of US GDP; the dependence is structural.
  • Underestimating diversification — CETA and CPTPP have given Canadian exporters access far beyond NAFTA but volume remains modest.
  • Treating "the rules-based order" as a stable feature — it is contested and evolving.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.3 — "analyse global migration patterns and Canadian immigration and refugee policy (Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, 2002; private-sponsorship model; 2015–16 Syrian resettlement)."

🔮 5.6 The Future of Multilateralism — Canada in a Changing World
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The post-1945 multilateral order — the UN system, Bretton Woods institutions, the GATT/WTO, NATO, NAFTA/USMCA, the EU, and the dense network of treaties governing rights, trade, environment, and security — is under unprecedented stress. The post-2017 shift in US foreign policy, the post-2008 economic crisis and its political aftershocks, the 2020-23 COVID pandemic, the rise of China as a comprehensive challenger, the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, the post-2023 Gaza crisis, and the AI-disinformation transformation of public discourse all challenge the system's stability. Whether the result is renewal, reform, or replacement is the defining political question of the 2020s and 2030s.

Three competing visions are visible. The liberal-institutionalist vision (Joseph Nye, Anne-Marie Slaughter, Brookings Institution) sees the multilateral order as imperfect but renewable: requires updating to address climate, AI, pandemics, and inequality; the basic architecture should be preserved and strengthened. The realist vision (John Mearsheimer, Stephen Walt, the Quincy Institute) sees the post-1945 order as a transient unipolar moment that is ending; multipolar great-power politics is reasserting; multilateralism will continue but with weaker authority. The civilisational/multipolar vision (Russian, Chinese, Indian, Brazilian, Iranian sources) sees the post-1945 order as a Western-dominated arrangement that should be replaced by a more pluralistic system reflecting BRICS+ growth and post-Western legitimacy.

Canada's position in this transition is consequential. As a middle power (~10% of US GDP; ~1.5% of world GDP; ~10% of world peacekeeping in the 1990s; ~1% in the 2020s), Canada lacks the heft to reshape the system unilaterally but can influence direction. Recent Canadian initiatives — the 2017 Strong, Secure, Engaged; the 2022 Indo-Pacific Strategy ($2.3 billion over 5 years); the 2023 Africa Strategy; the 2024 Defence Policy Update; the renewed engagement with the African Union — try to navigate the new environment. The 2024-25 federal government changes (Trudeau's resignation, Carney's leadership, Poilievre's electoral prospects) and the 2024-25 Trump administration test Canadian foreign-policy continuity.

The contemporary issues most demanding multilateral cooperation are climate (no state can decarbonise unilaterally), pandemics (no state can isolate from global biology), AI governance (frontier capabilities require coordinated rules; the 2023 Bletchley Declaration, 2024 Seoul Statement, 2024 OECD AI Principles update, 2025 EU AI Act phased entry into force), critical minerals (the energy transition requires globally distributed inputs), space (the 2024 Artemis Accords, growing space-debris and traffic-management challenges), and the cyber-conflict regime. None of these issues will be addressed without multilateral cooperation; none will be addressed in the absence of substantial state political will. The Grade-12 university-prep student should leave this course understanding that they are entering a world where multilateral cooperation is harder than at any time since 1945, but no less necessary — and that they will be among the cohort whose civic and professional choices will shape what comes next.

  • Liberal-institutionalist vision — post-1945 order should be reformed and renewed (Nye, Slaughter, Brookings).
  • Realist vision — multipolar great-power politics is reasserting (Mearsheimer, Walt, Quincy Institute).
  • Civilisational/multipolar vision — Western-dominated order should be replaced (BRICS+ governments and analysts).
  • Middle power — a state with substantial but not dominant capabilities; uses diplomatic and institutional influence (Pearson-era Canadian self-conception).
  • Bletchley Declaration (2023) — first major AI-safety multilateral document; signed by 29 states including UK, US, EU, China, Canada.

Future Analysis — Canada's Indo-Pacific Strategy (2022) in a Multipolar World

Canada's 2022 Indo-Pacific Strategy committed $2.3 billion over 5 years to substantially expanded engagement with the Indo-Pacific region (the 40+ states from India to the Pacific Islands). Apply concepts of political thinking to evaluate its prospects. Political significance: the strategy represents the first comprehensive Canadian foreign-policy reorientation since the end of the Cold War; reflects recognition that the centre of economic and strategic gravity has shifted to the Indo-Pacific. Objectives and results: stated objectives — diversify trade, strengthen partnerships, contribute to regional security; observed results — increased CAF deployment (HMCS Ottawa Indo-Pacific 2023-24; expanded joint exercises), CPTPP membership, India and ASEAN engagement, but limited tangible gains in the 2024 India-Canada diplomatic crisis (over Hardeep Singh Nijjar killing). Stability and change: represents substantial change in Canadian foreign-policy orientation but its sustainment requires sustained funding, political will, and senior leadership attention. Political perspective: foreign-policy realists support the strategy on geopolitical grounds; trade-policy advocates support diversification; some Asian-Canadian communities support stronger engagement with their countries of origin; critics emphasise the difficulty of executing comprehensive strategy with mid-sized resources. A Level-4 analysis names all four positions and recognises that Canadian foreign-policy capacity is constrained but real — and that the political choices made now will shape Canadian standing for decades.

  • Treating multilateralism as either fully functioning or fully broken — the reality is partial and uneven across issue areas.
  • Confusing the rules-based order with the post-1945 status quo — the rules-based order requires evolution to remain legitimate.
  • Forgetting that middle powers can shape outcomes through institutions even when they cannot determine them.
  • Treating Canadian foreign policy as nonpartisan — the 2024-25 transition between Trudeau and Carney administrations, and the prospective transition to a possible Poilievre government, will shape direction substantially.
  • Missing that future Canadian citizens will inherit the responsibility of choosing what multilateralism to support and what to reform.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.1 — "analyse Canada's diplomatic tools (multilateralism, 'middle-power' doctrine, sanctions regimes, development assistance)."
