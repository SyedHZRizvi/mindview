📚 2.1 The Concept of Political Ideology
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A political ideology is a coherent set of ideas about human nature, society, the state, and political change that provides both an interpretation of the political world and a programme for action within it. Michael Freeden's Ideologies and Political Theory (1996) treats ideologies as constellations of "core, adjacent, and peripheral concepts" — so liberalism's core might be liberty, individuality, rationality, and the limited state; its adjacent concepts vary by historical moment (constitutionalism, the market, equality of opportunity); its peripheral concepts are debated within the tradition. Every ideology is internally diverse: there is no single "liberalism" or "conservatism," only families of related positions.

The term "ideology" was coined by Antoine Destutt de Tracy in 1796 to name the scientific study of ideas, but its modern meaning was reshaped by Marx and Engels in The German Ideology (1846) — for them, ideology was the systematic distortion that ruling classes impose on their societies to legitimise their dominance. The 20th-century use is more neutral: ideologies are simply the frameworks through which political actors understand and pursue politics. The contemporary literature (Freeden; Andrew Heywood's Political Ideologies; the Cambridge History of Twentieth-Century Political Thought) treats ideologies analytically rather than as Marxist false-consciousness.

The conventional Left-Right spectrum dates to the French Revolution: in the 1789 Estates-General and subsequent Assemblies, supporters of the king sat on the right of the presiding officer, supporters of revolutionary change on the left. The spectrum has been used since to describe attitudes toward inequality, change, authority, and the role of the state. By the late 20th century, scholars increasingly distinguished an "economic Left-Right" (state vs. market) from a "social/cultural Left-Right" (cosmopolitan vs. communitarian, progressive vs. traditional, libertarian vs. authoritarian). The Political Compass (Jonathan Goldfarb & Daniel Goldfarb, 2001) and the V-Dem "GAL/TAN" (green-alternative-libertarian vs. traditional-authoritarian-nationalist) typology operationalise the two-axis view.

Ideologies do not map neatly onto parties. A Canadian Liberal MP, a U.S. Democratic congresswoman, and a U.K. Labour MP can all be called "liberal" or "left-of-centre" but disagree sharply on health-care funding, gun control, monarchy, or trade unions. Conversely, the post-2016 populist realignment has produced parties (Trump's Republicans, France's Rassemblement National, Italy's Fratelli d'Italia, the U.K.'s Reform Party) that mix economic protectionism (traditionally Left) with social traditionalism and immigration restriction (traditionally Right). A Grade-12 university-prep student should therefore use ideological vocabulary precisely — identifying which concept is being invoked and which others are being silently rearranged.

  • Ideology — a coherent set of ideas about human nature, society, the state, and political change.
  • Left-Right spectrum — the conventional axis from change/equality (Left) to tradition/hierarchy (Right); originated in the 1789 French National Assembly.
  • Economic vs. social axis — state-vs-market and cosmopolitan-vs-communitarian dimensions can vary independently.
  • Freeden's morphological approach — ideologies as constellations of core, adjacent, and peripheral concepts.
  • Populism — a thin-centred ideology pitting "the people" against "the corrupt elite" (Mudde & Rovira Kaltwasser, 2017).

Conceptual Application — Placing 2025 Canadian Parties on Two Axes

Using the 2025 federal landscape, place the major parties on both axes. Economic axis (state vs. market): NDP and Greens favour a larger state role (pharmacare, dental care, public housing, higher corporate taxation); Liberals occupy a centre-state position (Canada Health Transfer increases, modest carbon pricing, mixed economy); Conservatives generally favour a smaller state (lower personal income tax, reduced regulation, pipelines); the Bloc Québécois maps onto the Liberal-NDP space on economics. Social/cultural axis: NDP, Greens, and Liberals are progressive on cultural issues (climate, LGBTQ rights, immigration); Conservatives are mixed (the post-2022 Poilievre direction is more populist and immigration-sceptical than the Harper-era platform); the People's Party (Maxime Bernier) is explicitly populist-restrictionist; the Bloc takes Quebec-nationalist positions (Bill 21 support) that mix progressive economic with traditionalist cultural. A Level-4 analysis notes that single-axis placement is misleading and uses both axes to capture realignment.

  • Treating "liberal" as a synonym for "left-wing" — in the U.S., yes; in Canada and globally, the Liberal Party is centrist and "classical liberalism" refers to small-state pro-market positions.
  • Assuming ideologies are static — they evolve substantially across decades (Disraeli's Conservatism vs. Thatcher's Conservatism vs. May/Sunak Conservatism).
  • Confusing ideology with party — parties are coalitions that contain multiple ideological currents.
  • Using "extremist" as an analytical category — it is a normative judgment, not a descriptive classification.
  • Forgetting that the post-Cold-War "end of ideology" claim (Fukuyama 1989, "The End of History?") has been falsified by post-2008 events.

🗽 2.2 Liberalism, Conservatism, and Socialism
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The "big three" of modern political ideology emerged in the 17th–19th centuries in response to the same questions: what is the proper relationship between the individual and the state? what is the legitimate basis of authority? what is the proper distribution of wealth and power? Each gives a different answer.

Liberalism originates with John Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689). Locke argued that individuals possess natural rights (life, liberty, property) prior to and independent of the state; legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed; the state's purpose is to protect rights, not to define or pursue collective virtue. Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) added the economic argument: markets, not states, are the efficient mechanism for organising production. John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859) extended individual liberty into the social realm, with the "harm principle": individuals should be free to act as they wish provided their actions do not harm others. 20th-century "social liberalism" (T.H. Green, John Rawls's A Theory of Justice, 1971) emphasised that genuine liberty requires substantive equality of opportunity and a baseline of welfare; "classical" and "social" liberalism are still in tension within the liberal tradition.

Conservatism begins with Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790). Burke opposed the French revolutionaries' abstract rationalism: societies, he argued, are organic wholes built up over centuries; institutions embody accumulated practical wisdom; abrupt change in the name of theory destroys the social fabric. Conservatism is therefore (i) sceptical of abstract reason in politics, (ii) committed to gradual rather than revolutionary change, (iii) attached to tradition, religion, family, and authority. 20th-century conservatism diversified: traditional/Burkean (Roger Scruton, George Grant in Canada), libertarian/neoliberal (Hayek, Friedman, Thatcher, Mulroney's free-market wing), neo-conservative (Irving Kristol, the Bush-era foreign-policy school), and national-conservative/post-liberal (the post-2016 Patrick Deneen / Yoram Hazony school). George Grant's Lament for a Nation (1965) is the foundational text of Canadian Red Toryism — a conservatism committed to nation, tradition, and a substantive state.

Socialism emerged as a critique of industrial capitalism. Early "utopian socialists" (Saint-Simon, Fourier, Owen) imagined cooperative communities; Marx and Engels in the Communist Manifesto (1848) and Capital (1867) developed the materialist analysis: history is a sequence of class struggles; capitalism produces wealth but concentrates ownership; the proletariat will eventually overturn capitalism. By the early 20th century, socialism split into revolutionary (Lenin's Bolsheviks, who took power in October 1917) and democratic/reformist (the German SPD under Bernstein, the British Labour Party founded 1900, the Canadian CCF 1932). Postwar social democracy (Tommy Douglas in Saskatchewan, Olof Palme in Sweden, Willy Brandt in Germany) accepted markets but used the state to redistribute, regulate, and guarantee basic welfare. The 1980s neoliberal turn (Thatcher, Reagan, Mulroney) put social democracy on the defensive; the post-2008 financial crisis revived interest in democratic-socialist programmes (Sanders, Corbyn, Singh's NDP).

  • Liberalism — Lockean rights, Smithian markets, Millian liberty; classical and social variants in tension.
  • Conservatism — Burkean caution, organic society, gradual change; traditional, libertarian, and post-liberal variants.
  • Socialism — critique of capitalism, collective ownership/redistribution; revolutionary and democratic variants.
  • Red Tory — Canadian conservative tradition (George Grant) combining tradition with substantial state.
  • Neoliberalism — the post-1970s revival of classical-liberal economics (Hayek, Friedman, Mont Pèlerin Society).

Ideological Analysis — The 2025 Canadian Pharmacare Debate

Apply concepts of political thinking to the federal pharmacare expansion (Bill C-64, 2024). Liberal perspective: a single-payer pharmacare programme would extend the universalist tradition of Canadian healthcare, reduce inequality of access, and modestly redistribute risk; the federal Liberal government framed it as completing Tommy Douglas's vision. Conservative perspective: pharmacare expansion increases federal spending, encroaches on provincial jurisdiction over health, displaces private-employer drug plans (creating crowd-out), and reduces individual choice; Pierre Poilievre's Conservatives oppose universal pharmacare in favour of targeted assistance. Socialist/NDP perspective: pharmacare is a basic right and should be expanded; the NDP-Liberal supply-and-confidence agreement (2022–24) was conditioned on its progress. Indigenous perspective: First Nations and Inuit communities have specific drug-coverage arrangements (NIHB) and may benefit or be displaced depending on programme design. A Level-4 political analysis identifies all four positions, names the ideological assumptions of each, and assesses how the eventual policy resolves tensions among them.

  • Treating "liberal," "conservative," and "socialist" as fixed positions — each is an internally diverse tradition with multiple competing strands.
  • Equating Canadian "Liberals" with U.S. "liberals" — they map differently to the international left-right spectrum.
  • Forgetting that Canada has a real social-democratic tradition (CCF/NDP) absent from the U.S. mainstream.
  • Confusing socialism with communism — democratic socialism accepts liberal-democratic institutions; Marxist-Leninist communism rejects them.
  • Assuming ideologies are economic only — each has cultural, religious, and moral dimensions.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "describe the key principles of major political ideologies (liberalism, conservatism, socialism)."

⚠️ 2.3 Fascism, Populism, and Indigenous Political Thought
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Fascism emerged in interwar Europe, most importantly in Mussolini's Italy (in power 1922–43) and Hitler's Germany (1933–45). Roger Griffin's influential 1991 definition isolates "palingenetic ultra-nationalism" — the myth of national rebirth from decadence through cleansing violence — as fascism's mythic core. Robert Paxton's The Anatomy of Fascism (2004) lists nine "mobilising passions" including a sense of overwhelming crisis, the primacy of the group, the belief that one's group is a victim, dread of the group's decline, the need for closer integration of a purer community, the authority of leaders, the beauty of violence, the right of the chosen people to dominate others, and a populist anti-elitism. Fascism is not synonymous with right-wing authoritarianism or with conservatism; it is a specific, historically situated combination. Its definitional features are mobilising mass nationalism, violence as cleansing, leader cult, and rejection of liberal democracy.

Populism is contested. The most-cited definition is Cas Mudde & Cristóbal Rovira Kaltwasser's Populism: A Very Short Introduction (2017): "a thin-centred ideology that considers society to be ultimately separated into two homogeneous and antagonistic camps, 'the pure people' versus 'the corrupt elite,' and which argues that politics should be an expression of the volonté générale (general will) of the people." Populism is "thin-centred" because it attaches itself to thicker ideologies — there is left-wing populism (Hugo Chávez, Evo Morales, Jean-Luc Mélenchon, the Sanders movement) and right-wing populism (Trump, Le Pen, Meloni, Orbán, Bolsonaro, Modi). The post-2016 surge has produced a substantial scholarly literature on causes (cultural backlash, economic stagnation, the digital information environment, the 2008 financial crisis), consequences (democratic erosion, polarisation, foreign-policy realignment), and policy responses.

The Canadian populist tradition is older than 2016. The 1920s Progressive movement (United Farmers of Ontario, the Progressive Party of Canada), Social Credit (William Aberhart in Alberta 1935–43; Réal Caouette's Quebec wing), the early Reform Party (Preston Manning's 1987 founding), Doug Ford's "for the people" 2018 PC campaign, and Maxime Bernier's People's Party (2018–) all draw on populist tropes. The post-2022 Poilievre Conservatives have adopted populist rhetorical strategies (anti-"gatekeepers," distrust of mainstream media, framing "common people vs. elites") while remaining institutionally embedded in the parliamentary system. Distinguishing populism from democracy is essential: populism rejects pluralism (the legitimacy of competing political identities), claiming exclusive representation of "the people" — this is what makes it dangerous when it gains executive power.

Indigenous political thought is a substantial tradition often omitted from Ontario political-science treatments. Vine Deloria Jr.'s Custer Died for Your Sins (1969) and Red Earth, White Lies (1995), Taiaiake Alfred's Peace, Power, Righteousness (1999), Glen Coulthard's Red Skin, White Masks (2014), Audra Simpson's Mohawk Interruptus (2014), and Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's As We Have Always Done (2017) develop political theory from Indigenous traditions (Haudenosaunee Confederacy's Great Law of Peace, Anishinaabe clan governance, Inuit elder-council systems) rather than as a footnote to Western ideologies. Key concepts include: nationhood as relationship to land and ancestors rather than as Westphalian state; sovereignty as ongoing self-determination rather than territorial supremacy; governance as distributed authority across clan, family, and community; reconciliation not as a final settlement but as an ongoing political relationship requiring "the recognition of and respect for the inherent right of Indigenous peoples to self-determination" (UNDRIP, Article 3). The TRC (2008–15) and UNDRIP Act (2021) have raised Indigenous political thought to a recognised place in Canadian constitutional politics.

  • Fascism (Paxton/Griffin) — palingenetic ultra-nationalism, leader cult, cleansing violence, anti-pluralism.
  • Populism (Mudde & Rovira Kaltwasser) — thin-centred ideology pitting "pure people" against "corrupt elite."
  • Left vs. right populism — different thicker ideological hosts attached to the same thin-centred core.
  • UNDRIP — UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), adopted by Canada 2010, given federal legislative status 2021.
  • Indigenous nationhood — political identity as relationship to land, ancestors, and ongoing community, distinct from Westphalian sovereignty.

Comparative Analysis — Three Populisms

Compare three populist movements: the Trump 2016–24 movement (USA), the Five Star Movement (Italy, 2009–25), and the Saskatchewan Party (Canada). Common features: all three claim to represent "the real people" against "elites" (Washington/Brussels-Berlin/Ottawa-Toronto); all three use the digital media environment to bypass traditional party gatekeepers; all three are charismatic-leader-centred. Differences: Trump's movement is right-wing populist (anti-immigration, cultural-traditionalist, nationalist); Five Star originated as a left-libertarian "horizontalist" movement before drifting; the Saskatchewan Party is right-of-centre conservative-populist, defending provincial interests against federal carbon pricing. Concepts of political thinking: political significance — each has reshaped its political system; objectives and results — Trump's stated objective (drain the swamp) vs. observed results (continued lobbying, court packing); stability and change — populism destabilises party systems and can either be absorbed (FDP Germany incorporated AfD voter concerns) or normalised (Hungary's Fidesz, now in the European People's Party question). Political perspective — populists see themselves as democratic insurgents; their critics see them as a threat to pluralism. A Level-4 analysis distinguishes these three carefully.

  • Treating "fascism" as a synonym for any authoritarian right-wing politics — it has a specific historical and conceptual meaning.
  • Treating populism as merely a style — it has substantive content (anti-pluralism, monistic conception of "the people") that distinguishes it from democratic competition.
  • Confusing democratic socialism with totalitarian Marxism-Leninism — Tommy Douglas was a democratic socialist, not a communist.
  • Treating Indigenous political thought as merely cultural or spiritual — it is a substantive political-theoretical tradition with implications for sovereignty, governance, and constitutionalism.
  • Forgetting Canada's own populist tradition (Progressives, Social Credit, Reform, PPC) — populism is not a foreign import.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "describe the key principles of major political ideologies (fascism, populism, Indigenous political thought)."

🇨🇦 2.4 Citizenship: Rights, Duties, and the Charter
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Citizenship is the formal legal-political relationship between an individual and a state. T.H. Marshall's classic 1950 essay Citizenship and Social Class distinguished three historical layers: civil citizenship (rights to legal equality, due process, property — won in the 17th–18th centuries); political citizenship (the right to vote and run for office — won in the 19th–early 20th centuries); and social citizenship (rights to a minimum standard of welfare — built in the 20th century through the welfare state). Each layer is a political achievement, not a natural condition. Each can be contested or eroded.

Canadian citizenship is constituted by the Citizenship Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-29 (revised most recently in 2017). Canadian citizens are either citizens by birth (the jus soli principle — born on Canadian territory, with limited exceptions for foreign diplomats), citizens by descent (born outside Canada to a Canadian citizen parent — limited to one generation since 2009 amendments, with 2024 case law expanding the rule), citizens by grant (naturalisation after permanent residence and residency requirements), or citizens by adoption. As of 2024, Canada had approximately 26 million citizens (2021 census base) within a total population of ~40 million; roughly 23% of citizens are foreign-born — among the highest in the G7.

Section 3 of the Charter protects democratic rights: "Every citizen of Canada has the right to vote in an election of members of the House of Commons or of a legislative assembly and to be qualified for membership therein." Section 6 protects mobility rights. Section 23 protects minority-language education for citizens. The 1985 Operation Dismantle decision (cruise missile testing) confirmed that the Charter applies to all government action, including foreign-policy decisions affecting citizens. Sauvé v. Canada (2002, 5–4) struck down the prohibition on prisoner voting, holding that the right to vote is a fundamental civic right that cannot be removed by Parliament except by the section 33 override. Frank v. Canada (2019) struck down the five-year rule excluding long-term non-residents from voting.

Citizenship duties in Canadian law are remarkably minimal: paying taxes, obeying law, jury duty when called, military service (only when conscripted, last in 1944), and "obeying Canada's laws" as one of the citizenship-oath obligations. The contemporary citizenship-rights debate focuses on three pressure points: (i) the 2017 Bill C-6 reversal of the Harper-era citizenship-revocation powers for dual nationals convicted of terrorism (the right to citizenship cannot be unequal); (ii) the 2024 case Pillai v. Canada on second-generation citizenship abroad, opening descent rules; (iii) the digital-citizenship debate over what platform algorithms and AI-generated content do to political participation. A Grade-12 university-prep treatment of citizenship therefore needs both the legal-structural account and the lived-political account: what does citizenship actually do, day to day, for the various Canadians who hold it?

  • Marshall's three layers — civil, political, and social citizenship developed in successive centuries.
  • Jus soli vs. jus sanguinis — citizenship by birthplace vs. citizenship by descent; Canada uses primarily jus soli for first generation.
  • Naturalisation — acquiring citizenship through permanent residence, residency, language tests, and oath.
  • Section 3 of the Charter — democratic rights of citizens (voting, eligibility for office).
  • Sauvé v. Canada (2002) — leading case affirming prisoner voting rights.

Citizenship Analysis — Voting Rights for Non-Residents and Prisoners

Two SCC cases test citizenship rights for marginal citizens. Sauvé v. Canada (No. 2), 2002: prisoners serving 2+ years had been barred from voting under the Canada Elections Act. The SCC (5–4) struck down the prohibition, with McLachlin C.J. holding that the right to vote is the fundamental democratic right and cannot be removed except by section 33. Frank v. Canada, 2019: Canadian citizens absent from Canada for more than five years had been prevented from voting in federal elections. The SCC (5–2) struck down this restriction, holding that the citizen-voter relationship does not depend on continuous physical residence. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: both decisions extended the franchise to marginal citizens (prisoners; long-term non-residents — about 4 million Canadians abroad). Objectives and results: Parliament had restricted voting to preserve "civic responsibility" criteria; the Court held the criteria were illegitimate. Stability and change: the trajectory from 1885 (when Manitoba women won the provincial vote) through 1960 (Status Indian federal vote) through 2002–19 has been a continuous expansion. Political perspective: civil-libertarians see this as fulfilment of citizenship; traditional conservatives have argued for limits to enfranchisement. A Level-4 analysis notes that these are unusual cases — most Canadian citizenship rights are uncontested.

  • Treating citizenship as merely a legal category — it is also a political, social, and identity construct.
  • Confusing citizenship rights with human rights — citizenship rights apply only to citizens; human rights apply to everyone.
  • Forgetting that Canadian Indigenous peoples were not federal citizens until 1956–60 (Status Indians).
  • Assuming the right to vote is unconstrained — Charter section 3 is robust, but section 33 could theoretically override it (though section 3 is one of the protected sections).
  • Missing the contemporary debate over algorithmic citizenship — what does political participation mean when platforms shape information flow?

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "analyse the rights and responsibilities of citizenship in Canada."

🗳️ 2.5 Political Parties and the Canadian Party System
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A political party is a voluntary organisation that seeks to win elected office, develop public policy, and aggregate the political demands of citizens. Parties developed in the late 18th and 19th centuries as electorates expanded and representative institutions grew more complex; modern political competition is essentially impossible without them. The classic functional account (V.O. Key, Politics, Parties, and Pressure Groups, 1942) sees parties as performing three roles: party-as-organisation (the formal membership, executive, and bureaucracy); party-in-government (elected legislators and ministers); and party-in-the-electorate (voter identification and loyalty).

The Canadian party system has undergone five distinguishable "party systems" (Carty, Cross, and Young, Rebuilding Canadian Party Politics, 2000). First system 1867–1917: Liberals (Laurier) vs. Conservatives (Macdonald, Borden) — patronage-driven, regionally cross-cutting. Second system 1921–57: addition of Progressives, then CCF and Social Credit; the Liberals dominant nationally under King. Third system 1963–93: Liberals vs. Progressive Conservatives vs. NDP; the era of "brokerage politics." Fourth system 1993–2011: post-1993 fragmentation — Liberals, Reform/Canadian Alliance, Bloc Québécois, NDP, Progressive Conservatives; the eventual 2003 merger of the Canadian Alliance and PC into the modern Conservative Party of Canada. Fifth system 2011–: Conservatives, Liberals, NDP, Bloc, Greens, and (since 2018) the People's Party; punctuated by minority parliaments (2019, 2021), supply-and-confidence agreements, and the post-2024 Liberal-NDP rupture.

Canadian electoral law shapes party behaviour. The federal first-past-the-post (FPTP) electoral system rewards regionally concentrated support and punishes diffuse national support — the Bloc Québécois consistently wins seats with ~7% of the national vote because its vote is concentrated in Quebec; the Greens have historically struggled with ~6% nationally because their vote is dispersed. Reference re Provincial Electoral Boundaries (1991) established the principle of "effective representation" rather than strict per-capita equality — riding boundaries can vary somewhat to recognise communities of interest. Election spending limits (Bill C-2, the Federal Accountability Act 2006) cap donations to ~$1,725/year per party in 2024 — Canada's "small donor" model contrasts sharply with the U.S. high-dollar fundraising environment.

The 2025 federal landscape illustrates contemporary tensions. The Liberals (Justin Trudeau resigned January 2025; Mark Carney's leadership) hold government but face declining polling; the Conservatives (Pierre Poilievre) lead in most polls but have not yet been elected to government; the NDP (Jagmeet Singh) has lost ground after the supply-and-confidence agreement collapsed in September 2024; the Bloc remains regionally strong; the Greens (Elizabeth May, Jonathan Pedneault co-leaders) remain in the margins; the People's Party (Maxime Bernier) has yet to win a seat. The future of the party system is contested: realignment around a populist Conservative-Liberal cleavage, or restoration of three- or four-party brokerage politics? A Grade-12 university-prep treatment recognises that the answer depends in part on the next federal election and on how each party manages its internal coalitions.

  • Party-as-organisation / party-in-government / party-in-electorate — Key's three functional dimensions.
  • Five Canadian party systems — successive eras of party competition since 1867 (Carty, Cross & Young).
  • First-past-the-post (FPTP) — single-member plurality electoral system; favours regionally concentrated parties.
  • Brokerage politics — pre-1993 Canadian model of catch-all parties seeking cross-regional and cross-linguistic coalitions.
  • Federal Accountability Act 2006 — comprehensive election-finance regime with strict donation limits.

Party-System Analysis — The Bloc Québécois in Canadian Politics

The Bloc Québécois was founded in 1991 by Lucien Bouchard and other dissident MPs in protest of the failed Meech Lake Accord. It contests federal elections only in Quebec, advocating Quebec sovereignty and Quebec interests at the federal level. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: the Bloc has won between 10 and 54 seats in every federal election from 1993 to 2025 except 2011; it formed the Official Opposition 1993–97 — the only time a regional party has done so. Objectives and results: stated objective is Quebec independence and protecting Quebec's political weight; observed results include defeat of the 1995 sovereignty referendum (49.42% Yes) and 30 years of federal-Quebec policy bargaining without independence. Stability and change: the Bloc's persistence is itself remarkable — predictions of its demise in 2008, 2011 (when it fell to 4 seats and lost official-party status), and 2015 have all been wrong. Political perspective: federalists see the Bloc as institutionalising Quebec separatism within Parliament; Quebec nationalists see it as essential federal-level representation. The Bloc's continued existence illustrates Canadian federalism's tolerance of regional dissent in a way no other G7 country matches. A Level-4 analysis treats the Bloc as a case study in the durability of regional political identity within a federal system.

  • Treating Canadian parties as ideologically frozen — they shift substantially across decades.
  • Confusing the Conservative Party of Canada (post-2003) with the historic Progressive Conservatives (1942–2003) — they are legally and politically distinct.
  • Forgetting that Canada has had five federal party systems and may be entering a sixth.
  • Underestimating the role of leadership conventions and party constitutions in shaping policy direction.
  • Missing the institutional shaping effect of FPTP — different systems would produce different party landscapes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "analyse the relationship between political ideologies and political parties in Canada and internationally."

✊ 2.6 Civil Society and Social Movements
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Civil society is the realm of voluntary association between the family and the state — labour unions, religious institutions, advocacy organisations, professional bodies, community groups, and the press. The concept comes from the 18th-century Scottish Enlightenment (Adam Ferguson's Essay on the History of Civil Society, 1767) and was developed by Hegel and Tocqueville. Tocqueville's Democracy in America (1835/40) argued that the proliferation of "voluntary associations" in the United States was the secret of its democratic vitality — citizens learned to govern themselves by participating in non-state organisations. Robert Putnam's Bowling Alone (2000) used "social capital" — the trust and norms of reciprocity produced by associational life — to argue that civil society had declined in the late 20th century, with consequences for democratic health.

Social movements are sustained collective challenges to political authority by people with common purposes, organised outside conventional party politics (Sidney Tarrow, Power in Movement, 1994). They typically combine some mix of contentious tactics (protests, strikes, boycotts, occupations, civil disobedience) and conventional pressure (lobbying, electoral mobilisation, litigation). The "political opportunity structure" framework (Doug McAdam) emphasises that movements succeed when political openings appear — splits in elites, expanding political access, weakening repression, available allies. The "resource mobilisation" framework emphasises that movements need organisational infrastructure, money, leadership, and networks. The "framing" framework (Robert Benford, David Snow) emphasises how movements interpret grievances and propose solutions in ways that resonate with their audiences.

The major Canadian social movements of the past half-century include: the 1960s–70s second-wave feminist movement (Royal Commission on the Status of Women 1967–70; the 1970 Abortion Caravan; the 1981 ad hoc Conference on Women and the Constitution that secured sections 15 and 28 of the Charter); the labour movement (CLC reorganisation 1956; the public-sector unionisation wave 1965–75; the 2024 federal strike actions); the environmental movement (Greenpeace founded Vancouver 1971; Clayoquot Sound 1993; the climate movement 2018– including Fridays For Future and 350.org); the Indigenous rights movement (the 1990 Oka Crisis; the 2012–13 Idle No More movement; the 2020 Wet'suwet'en solidarity actions; the 2021 residential-school burial-site activism); the LGBTQ rights movement (Toronto bathhouse raids 1981 and resulting Pride movement; Vriend 1998; same-sex marriage 2003–05; the 2017 federal apology; the 2024 conversion-therapy ban); and the post-2020 Black Lives Matter and anti-racism mobilisations.

The contemporary digital environment has reshaped collective action. Social-media organising lowered coordination costs and enabled the Arab Spring (2011), Occupy (2011), Idle No More (2012), Black Lives Matter (2013–), Fridays For Future (2018–), and the Convoy protests (2022). It has also fragmented attention, enabled disinformation, and produced "movements without organisations" that struggle to translate mobilisation into durable institutional change. Zeynep Tufekci's Twitter and Tear Gas (2017) is the leading study of digital-era movements. A Grade-12 university-prep analysis recognises that civil society and social movements are both essential complements to liberal democracy (creating pluralism, holding government accountable, generating policy innovation) and sites of political contestation (movements have ideologies, partisans, and rivals).

  • Civil society — the realm of voluntary association between family and state.
  • Social capital (Putnam) — trust and norms of reciprocity produced by associational life.
  • Political opportunity structure — the macro-political conditions that allow or constrain movements.
  • Framing — how movements interpret grievances and propose solutions to mobilise support.
  • Networked movements — post-2010 digitally-coordinated movements with low formal organisation.

Movement Analysis — Idle No More (2012–13)

Idle No More began in November 2012 with four Saskatchewan women — Nina Wilson, Sheelah McLean, Sylvia McAdam, and Jessica Gordon — organising teach-ins against the Harper government's Bill C-45 (which weakened the Navigable Waters Protection Act and reduced consultation on resource development). It became a continent-wide Indigenous-led movement combining round-dance flash mobs, blockades, hunger strikes (Chief Theresa Spence's six-week fast on Victoria Island, December 2012–January 2013), and 12 January 2013 Day of Action. Apply concepts of political thinking. Political significance: the movement reframed Indigenous politics as an ongoing assertion of nationhood, not a request for legislative concession; it preceded and helped enable the 2015 TRC release. Objectives and results: stated objectives — withdrawal of Bill C-45, treaty respect, nation-to-nation relationship — were not directly achieved; but the movement shifted public discourse and created the political conditions for the TRC and 2021 UNDRIP Act. Stability and change: produced a generation of Indigenous activist leadership and shifted the political grammar of Indigenous-state relations from "rights" to "nationhood." Political perspective: settler-Canadian audiences interpreted the movement variously as legitimate protest, as legally questionable (re-blockades), or as a wake-up call; Indigenous audiences emphasised continuity with centuries of resistance. A Level-4 analysis uses all four concepts.

  • Treating civil society as politically neutral — it is itself contested terrain with progressive, conservative, and reactionary currents.
  • Confusing protest with social movement — sustained collective action with shared identity is movements; one-off protests are not.
  • Assuming digital organising is necessarily democratising — it can also fragment attention, enable disinformation, and produce surveillance vulnerabilities.
  • Forgetting that successful movements typically combine extra-parliamentary action with parliamentary allies (e.g., the long Canadian feminist alliance with NDP, Liberal, and PC women MPs).
  • Missing the role of civil-society organisations (United Way, religious networks, professional bodies) that do everyday political work without ever being called "movements."

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "describe several ways in which citizens can participate in political life beyond voting (civil society, social movements, public consultation, direct action)."
