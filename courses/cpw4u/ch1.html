🌍 1.1 The Modern State and Sovereignty
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The "modern state" is the central unit of contemporary political analysis. Max Weber's 1919 lecture Politics as a Vocation gives the classical definition: the state is "a human community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory." Four elements compress into that sentence: a defined territory, a settled population, an internal government, and the recognised capacity to enter relations with other states. The 1933 Montevideo Convention codified these as the legal criteria of statehood, and they remain the standard test today — invoked, for instance, in debates over Kosovo (declared 2008), Palestine (UN observer state since 2012), and Taiwan (de facto state but recognised by only ~12 states).

The state is a historical product, not a permanent feature of human political life. Before 1648, European political authority was layered and overlapping — Holy Roman Emperor, Pope, king, duke, free city, and bishop each claimed authority over the same territory. The Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended the Thirty Years' War, is conventionally treated as the founding moment of the modern state system. Westphalia introduced two principles still operative today: cuius regio, eius religio (rulers determine the religion of their state) and non-intervention (states should not interfere in one another's internal affairs). The latter principle is the legal basis of the UN Charter's Article 2(7).

Sovereignty has two faces. Internal sovereignty is the state's supreme authority over its territory — the capacity to make and enforce law without superior. External sovereignty is its independence from other states — the capacity to make foreign policy and conclude treaties as a free actor. Both are ideal types; in practice every state is constrained. Canada is internally sovereign but constrained by the Charter, federalism, Indigenous treaty rights, and international human-rights obligations. Externally sovereign but constrained by membership in NATO, NORAD, the UN, the WTO, USMCA, and ~3,000 bilateral and multilateral treaties.

The "failed state" concept (popularised in the 1990s through Somalia, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Sudan) refers to states that no longer effectively control their territory, lack a functioning government, or cannot provide basic public goods. The Fragile States Index ranks ~178 states annually on twelve dimensions; the 2024 list places Somalia, Yemen, South Sudan, and Syria at the top of fragility. The category is contested — critics argue "failed state" is a Western label that conceals colonial legacies, externally imposed sanctions regimes, and great-power interference. A Grade-12 university-prep analysis should hold both moves: states fail, and the label hides as much as it reveals.

  • Sovereignty — supreme authority over a territory and freedom from external superior.
  • Westphalian system (1648) — the territorial-state system based on non-intervention.
  • Montevideo Convention (1933) — codified the legal criteria of statehood.
  • De jure vs. de facto state — recognised in law (e.g., the State of Palestine in many bilateral relations) vs. operating in fact (e.g., Somaliland since 1991).
  • Failed/fragile state — state lacking effective control, government, or capacity for public goods.

Concept Application — Is Taiwan a State?

Apply the Montevideo criteria to Taiwan (Republic of China). (1) Defined territory: the island and its outlying islets, roughly 36,000 km², are continuously administered from Taipei. (2) Population: ~23.4 million as of 2024. (3) Government: a fully functioning democratic government — Taiwan ranked 8th globally on the 2024 Economist Democracy Index, ahead of every other Asian state. (4) Capacity for international relations: Taiwan maintains diplomatic relations with ~12 states (a number that has fallen as the PRC has used economic leverage to shift recognitions), has trade and de facto consular ties with most of the rest of the world, is a member of the WTO under "Chinese Taipei," and signs significant international agreements. By Montevideo standards, Taiwan is a state. Politically, however, the PRC's "One China" principle and the threat of military force have kept Taiwan outside the UN system since 1971 (when UN seat 758 transferred to the PRC). A Level-4 analysis distinguishes the legal question (yes, by criteria) from the political question (no, by recognition) and identifies which concept of political thinking applies — primarily political perspective, because the answer depends on whose framework one uses.

  • Treating sovereignty as absolute — every state is constrained by treaties, federalism, and rights frameworks.
  • Confusing the state with the nation — the state is a legal/political construct; the nation is a cultural/identity construct. Canada is a state with multiple nations (English, French, ~70 Indigenous nations).
  • Reading Westphalia as a complete account of the modern state — colonial empires, decolonisation, and the post-1945 multilateral order have modified the system.
  • Treating "failed state" as a neutral category — it carries normative weight that requires critical handling.
  • Forgetting that recognition is political, not just legal — Kosovo is recognised by ~100 states, not recognised by ~90.

🗳️ 1.2 Democratic, Authoritarian, and Totalitarian Systems
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A political system is the set of institutions, rules, and practices through which a society makes collective binding decisions. Comparative political science classifies systems along several dimensions, but the most useful Grade-12 spectrum runs from democracy through authoritarianism to totalitarianism. Democracy, on Robert Dahl's influential 1971 definition (Polyarchy: Participation and Opposition), requires: free and fair elections, universal adult suffrage, the right to run for office, freedom of expression, alternative sources of information, freedom of association, and the responsiveness of elected officials. Dahl's seven criteria are still the operational test used by Freedom House and the Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index.

"Liberal democracy" is the dominant Western form: democracy plus the rule of law, the protection of individual rights, an independent judiciary, and constraints on executive power. By contrast, "illiberal democracy" (Fareed Zakaria, 1997) describes regimes that hold competitive elections but erode rights protections, judicial independence, or media freedom — Viktor Orbán's Hungary, Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's Türkiye, and Narendra Modi's India have all been categorised this way at various points. The democracy-quality literature shows a clear trend: in the 2024 V-Dem Institute report, ~71% of the world's population lives in autocracies, the highest share since the early 1990s, and 42 countries are undergoing "autocratisation" while only 18 are democratising.

Authoritarian systems concentrate political power in a single leader, party, or small elite, restrict political competition, and suppress or co-opt opposition. They permit some private life and limited civil society but punish open challenge. Modern examples include the People's Republic of China (CCP single-party rule since 1949), the Russian Federation since the 2000s (managed elections, eliminated independent media, jailed and killed opposition figures), Saudi Arabia (absolute monarchy), and Iran (theocratic-electoral hybrid). Juan Linz's classic distinction: authoritarianism tolerates a limited, depoliticised private sphere; totalitarianism does not.

Totalitarianism is the most demanding category. Hannah Arendt's The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951) identified three features: a single ruling ideology that claims to explain all of history, a single mass party fused with the state, and the use of terror — not just to punish but to transform the population. Arendt's paradigmatic cases are Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR. Contemporary candidates are contested: North Korea under the Kim dynasty is the clearest current example; China under Xi Jinping (the "social-credit system," the Uyghur surveillance and detention regime in Xinjiang) shows totalitarian tendencies in specific domains but is not yet fully totalitarian. The category remains a powerful analytical tool when used carefully — and a slogan when used loosely.

  • Polyarchy (Dahl) — Dahl's seven institutional criteria of democracy.
  • Liberal vs. illiberal democracy — elections plus/minus rights protections, judicial independence, and media freedom.
  • Authoritarianism — concentrated power, suppressed competition, tolerated private life.
  • Totalitarianism (Arendt) — single ideology + single party + terror; aims to transform the population.
  • Autocratisation — the contemporary backsliding from democracy toward authoritarianism (V-Dem 2024).

Comparative Analysis — Classifying Hungary and Türkiye Today

Are contemporary Hungary and Türkiye democracies, illiberal democracies, or competitive authoritarian regimes? Apply Dahl's seven criteria. Hungary holds elections, but: (i) the 2011 constitution and 2010 electoral law were redesigned to favour Fidesz; (ii) state media is openly partisan; (iii) the Constitutional Court was packed; (iv) press freedom has declined to ~76th out of 180 (RSF 2024); (v) the EU has activated Article 7 proceedings (2018–) over rule-of-law concerns. Türkiye holds elections (Erdoğan won 2014, 2018, 2023 presidentials), but: (i) thousands of journalists, judges, and academics were dismissed or jailed after the 2016 coup attempt; (ii) the press freedom score is ~165/180; (iii) the 2017 constitutional referendum transferred legislative power to a strengthened executive presidency. Both meet some Dahl criteria but fail others. The contemporary literature classifies both as "competitive authoritarian" — regimes that hold real elections that opposition can theoretically win but where the playing field is severely tilted. A Level-4 response uses both political-significance and political-perspective concepts: significant because the category bears on whether external actors (EU, NATO, the UN) should treat the regime as a democracy; perspective-dependent because Fidesz supporters and Erdoğan voters reject the "competitive authoritarian" label as Western interference.

  • Using "totalitarian" as a synonym for "authoritarian" or "dictatorship" — Arendt's category is narrower and more demanding.
  • Assuming democracy requires a presidential or two-party system — Westminster parliamentary democracies (Canada, the UK, Australia, India) are democracies too.
  • Treating regime classification as a single moment — regimes drift; democracy can erode (Hungary 2010–) or expand (Tunisia 2011, briefly).
  • Confusing electoral authoritarianism with democracy — Russia holds elections but has no real competition.
  • Forgetting that democratic backsliding usually comes through legal channels (constitutional amendments, court-packing) not coups.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "analyse the political characteristics of democratic, authoritarian, and totalitarian systems."

🍁 1.3 Canadian Federalism and the Division of Powers
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada is a federal state. The federal principle divides sovereignty between two orders of government — a central government and provincial governments — each of which acts directly on citizens within its sphere of jurisdiction. The federal-provincial division of powers is set out in sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867 (originally the British North America Act). Section 91 enumerates federal powers: trade and commerce, defence, criminal law, money and banking, immigration, "peace, order, and good government" (POGG), and 27 other heads. Section 92 enumerates provincial powers: property and civil rights (which has been interpreted broadly to include most contract, employment, and consumer-protection law), municipalities, hospitals, education (section 93), the administration of justice, and 13 other heads. Section 95 makes immigration and agriculture concurrent (both levels can legislate, with federal paramountcy).

The text is only the starting point. Federalism in practice is shaped by judicial interpretation. From 1867 to 1949 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council (JCPC) in London interpreted sections 91 and 92 narrowly in favour of provincial powers. The Supreme Court of Canada, which became the final court of appeal in 1949, has been less consistent but has generally given POGG broader reach in modern issues — most notably in Reference re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act (2021), a 6–3 majority upheld the federal carbon-pricing law as a valid POGG matter of "national concern" because climate change has effects beyond any single province. Federalism is therefore not a static text but a continuing political-legal negotiation.

The federal-provincial relationship is also shaped by fiscal arrangements. The Constitution gives provinces extensive jurisdiction (especially health, education, and welfare) but limited revenue tools relative to those obligations — the "fiscal imbalance" debate. Federal transfers fill the gap: equalisation payments (constitutionalised in section 36 of the Constitution Act, 1982; ~$24 billion in 2024–25 to seven provinces), the Canada Health Transfer (~$50 billion), and the Canada Social Transfer (~$16 billion). Quebec, Alberta, and Ontario have at various times pressed for tax-room transfers in place of cash transfers — a recurring constitutional-political fight.

Canadian federalism is also shaped by what the constitutional text does not say. Indigenous self-government is not part of section 91/92 division, but section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, and the Calder (1973), Sparrow (1990), Delgamuukw (1997), and Haida (2004) decisions have built up an extensive Indigenous-rights framework that operates alongside federalism. UNDRIP (2007), adopted by Canada in 2010 and given federal legislative status in 2021 (UNDRIP Act, S.C. 2021, c. 14), now requires federal law to be brought into harmony with the Declaration over time — a third "order" of governance is becoming explicit.

  • Federalism — division of sovereignty between two orders of government, each acting directly on citizens within its sphere.
  • Sections 91 & 92 — the federal-provincial division of legislative powers under the Constitution Act, 1867.
  • POGG ("Peace, Order, and Good Government") — the federal residual and "national concern" power.
  • Equalisation — federal transfers (s. 36, Constitution Act, 1982) to ensure reasonably comparable public services across provinces.
  • Section 35 — recognition and affirmation of Aboriginal and treaty rights (1982).

Constitutional Reasoning — Federal Carbon Pricing

Apply concepts of political thinking to Reference re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act (SCC 2021). Political significance: 6–3 ruling settled (for now) that climate change is sufficiently a "national concern" for federal POGG jurisdiction. The dissenting judges (Brown, Côté, Rowe) argued this expansion threatens federalism's balance and provincial regulatory autonomy. Objectives and results: federal government's stated objective was a minimum national carbon price ($20/t in 2019 rising to $170/t by 2030); the result has been a working backstop in provinces that did not enact their own price, plus political backlash (the "Axe the Tax" 2024 campaign, the 2025 dismantling of the consumer carbon levy). Stability and change: the decision is a major shift in federal-provincial balance on environmental matters; whether it endures depends on future political choices, including whether Parliament repeals the underlying act. Political perspective: federalist legal scholars (Sébastien Grammond, Hugo Cyr) generally support the decision; provincial-rights scholars (Dwight Newman, Asher Honickman) generally oppose it; environmental policy scholars assess it on outcome-effectiveness rather than constitutional purity. A Level-4 analysis surfaces all four perspectives explicitly.

  • Treating the section 91/92 list as exhaustive — courts have created doctrines (POGG, double aspect, paramountcy, interjurisdictional immunity) that modify it.
  • Treating federalism as static — the balance shifts with politics, fiscal arrangements, and Supreme Court appointments.
  • Forgetting Indigenous self-government — section 35 and UNDRIP increasingly require federalism to be understood as multi-order, not two-order.
  • Confusing equalisation with health/social transfers — they have different purposes and formulas.
  • Treating the JCPC era as merely historical — its narrow reading of POGG still informs how some doctrines are framed today.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "explain the structure and functioning of the Canadian political system, including federalism, the Westminster parliamentary model, and the division of powers."

🏛️ 1.4 Westminster Parliamentary vs. Presidential Systems
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The two dominant institutional designs in modern democracies are the Westminster parliamentary system (Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, India, ~30 others) and the presidential system (the United States, most of Latin America, the Philippines, South Korea, the post-1958 French semi-presidential variant). The core difference is the relationship between the legislative and executive branches: in Westminster, the executive (Cabinet) is drawn from and accountable to the legislature; in presidential systems, the executive (President) is elected separately and is constitutionally independent of the legislature.

The Westminster model rests on responsible government — the doctrine that the Cabinet must hold the confidence of the elected lower house. If a government loses a confidence vote (either an explicit motion of non-confidence or a defeat on a money bill or Speech from the Throne), it must resign or seek a dissolution. The principle was won in British politics in the 17th–18th centuries and exported to Canada in 1848 (Joseph Howe's Nova Scotia, Baldwin–Lafontaine in the Province of Canada). It produces fused executive-legislative authority: the Prime Minister is leader of the majority party (or leader of the largest party in a minority Parliament) and sits in the legislature. The PM is not elected as PM by the public; the public elects MPs, and the party leader who can command the confidence of the House forms government. This was tested in Canada's 2008 prorogation crisis (Harper sought prorogation from Governor General Michaëlle Jean to avoid a confidence vote; she granted it after a delay) and the 2015–25 period of multiple minority and majority transitions.

The presidential model rests on separation of powers — the legislative branch (Congress) and the executive branch (President) are elected separately, serve fixed terms, and check each other. The U.S. Constitution's Article I (Congress) and Article II (President) were designed by the framers to prevent the concentration of power. The trade-off is gridlock: when the President and Congress are from different parties (divided government, as in 2023–24 with President Biden and a Republican House), legislation can stall. Westminster systems trade flexibility for executive dominance; presidential systems trade gridlock for separation. Linz's classic argument ("The Perils of Presidentialism," 1990) is that presidential systems are vulnerable to constitutional crisis when the two branches have conflicting mandates — a worry borne out in Latin American breakdowns and, more recently, in U.S. anxieties about executive overreach.

A third variant matters for Canadian comparative analysis: the French semi-presidential system (Fifth Republic since 1958). France has both a directly elected President with substantial powers and a Prime Minister responsible to the National Assembly. When the President and PM are from the same party, the President dominates; when they are from different parties ("cohabitation," as 1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002), power is genuinely shared. This hybrid has been adopted by ~50 countries (Russia, Ukraine, Romania, Portugal, Senegal). It illustrates that the parliamentary-presidential dichotomy is a spectrum, not a binary.

  • Responsible government — the doctrine that the executive must hold the confidence of the legislature.
  • Separation of powers — the institutional independence of legislative, executive, and judicial branches.
  • Confidence convention — the unwritten rule that a government losing a confidence vote must resign or seek dissolution.
  • Semi-presidential system — hybrid model with a directly elected President and a PM responsible to the legislature.
  • Cohabitation — French term for a President and PM from opposing parties.

Comparative Case — The 2008 Canadian Prorogation Crisis

Prime Minister Stephen Harper's minority Conservative government faced a confidence vote that it expected to lose to a Liberal-NDP-Bloc coalition (with formal agreement signed 1 December 2008). On 4 December Harper asked Governor General Michaëlle Jean to prorogue Parliament, effectively shutting it down for ~7 weeks to avoid the vote. Jean granted the request after a two-hour delay. Apply political-thinking concepts. Significance: this was the first time in Canadian history that prorogation was used to escape a confidence vote; it sparked academic debate (Andrew Heard, Peter Russell, Hugo Cyr) about whether the Governor General should have refused. Stability and change: the convention that the GG acts on PM advice was tested and (controversially) upheld; the convention also evolved — subsequent academic and judicial commentary suggests the GG's discretion in such cases is real, not merely formal. Political perspective: Conservative supporters saw the request as a legitimate use of prerogative; coalition supporters saw it as a constitutional perversion. A comparable U.S. scenario is essentially impossible — the President cannot shut down Congress. This illustrates how parliamentary executive flexibility cuts both ways: it allows responsive government but also allows manoeuvres unavailable in fixed-term separated systems.

  • Saying "Canadians elect the Prime Minister" — they do not; they elect MPs, who then determine confidence.
  • Confusing the separation of powers (constitutional) with the separation of party and state (a different concept).
  • Treating the U.S. system as a "neutral" baseline; it is one design among several with its own pathologies (gridlock, presidential overreach, mid-term swing politics).
  • Forgetting that the Senate, in both Canada and the U.S., is also an institutional check on the lower house.
  • Ignoring the role of unwritten constitutional conventions in Westminster systems — most of the rules are not in the written text.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "explain the structure and functioning of the Canadian political system, including the Westminster parliamentary model."

⚖️ 1.5 The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms is Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982. It came into force on 17 April 1982 with patriation, replacing the merely statutory 1960 Canadian Bill of Rights (which applied only to federal law). The Charter binds all government action — federal, provincial, and municipal — and is constitutionally entrenched, meaning ordinary legislation that conflicts with it is "of no force or effect" (section 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982) unless the legislature invokes the section 33 "notwithstanding clause." It is the single most consequential constitutional document in modern Canadian political life.

The Charter protects: fundamental freedoms (section 2 — conscience and religion; thought, belief, opinion, and expression; peaceful assembly; association); democratic rights (sections 3–5 — vote, election every five years, annual session); mobility rights (section 6); legal rights (sections 7–14 — life, liberty, and security of the person; freedom from unreasonable search; right to be informed of charges; right to counsel; right to trial within reasonable time; presumption of innocence; freedom from cruel and unusual punishment); equality rights (section 15); language rights (sections 16–22); minority-language education rights (section 23); enforcement (sections 24, 52); and limitation clauses (sections 1, 33).

Two sections do most of the constitutional work. Section 1 permits "reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society." The test for section 1 was set in R. v. Oakes (1986): a Charter limit is justified if (1) the law's objective is pressing and substantial, and (2) the means are proportional — rationally connected, minimally impairing, and proportionate in effect. Almost every major Charter case turns on whether section 1 saves a Charter-infringing law. Section 33 — the "notwithstanding clause" — allows Parliament or any provincial legislature to declare that a law operates notwithstanding sections 2 and 7–15 of the Charter, for a renewable five-year period. Section 33 is the negotiated price of the 1982 deal with provinces (the "Kitchen Accord," November 1981); it allows the elected branch to override courts on certain matters.

Section 33 was rarely used in the first three decades after patriation — invoked by Quebec retroactively for all statutes 1982–85, by Saskatchewan once in 1986 (back-to-work legislation, with the SCC eventually deciding the Charter wasn't even infringed), and by Alberta once (overridden marriage definition, 2000, before same-sex marriage reference). The use has accelerated in the 2010s and 2020s: Quebec's 2019 Act respecting the laicity of the State (Bill 21) and 2021 language reforms (Bill 96); Ontario's 2021 back-to-work legislation (later withdrawn); New Brunswick and Saskatchewan threats. The contemporary debate is fierce: does section 33 protect parliamentary sovereignty (a Westminster value) or threaten constitutional rights (a liberal-democratic value)? The 2024 Supreme Court reference on Yukon Citizenship and the eventual Bill 21 reference, when it reaches the SCC, will be defining moments.

  • Charter of Rights and Freedoms — Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982; entrenched bill of rights.
  • Section 1 (Oakes test) — limits-clause: rights can be limited by laws that are pressing/substantial and proportional.
  • Section 33 (notwithstanding clause) — legislatures can override sections 2 and 7–15 for renewable five-year terms.
  • Section 15 (equality) — protection against discrimination including the listed and analogous grounds.
  • Section 35 (technically outside the Charter, in Part II of the Constitution Act, 1982) — recognition and affirmation of Aboriginal and treaty rights.

Charter Analysis — Quebec's Bill 21 (2019)

Bill 21 (Act respecting the laicity of the State) prohibits certain public-sector employees (teachers, police officers, judges) in positions of authority from wearing religious symbols. It explicitly invokes section 33 to operate notwithstanding sections 2 and 7–15. Apply the four concepts of political thinking. Political significance: this is the most extensive use of section 33 in Canadian history and tests whether the override can be used pre-emptively (before a Charter challenge is even heard) for a permanent policy goal. Objectives and results: stated objective is state secularism; observed results include the resignation or relocation of hijab-wearing teachers, English-school-board litigation (the English Montreal School Board challenge), and substantial public debate. Stability and change: section 33 is being normalised as a routine policy tool — a major change from the 1982–2010 pattern of rare and reluctant use. Political perspective: Quebec nationalist scholars (Mathieu Bock-Côté, Guillaume Rousseau) defend Bill 21 as a democratic expression of Quebec's distinct society and a Westminster-tradition use of legislative sovereignty; civil-liberties scholars (Sébastien Grammond, the Canadian Civil Liberties Association) argue it violates equality rights and disproportionately targets Muslim women. A Level-4 analysis surfaces both sides and identifies that the eventual SCC decision (likely 2026–27) will define the section 33 future.

  • Believing the Charter applies to private actors — it does not; it binds only government action (section 32). Private discrimination is regulated by provincial human-rights codes.
  • Confusing section 33 (notwithstanding) with section 1 (Oakes-test limits) — both limit rights but in different ways.
  • Treating the 1960 Bill of Rights as equivalent to the Charter — the 1960 Bill is a federal statute, applies only to federal law, and was rarely successfully invoked (only R. v. Drybones 1970 used it to strike down a statute).
  • Forgetting that section 33 cannot override sections 3–6, 16–23, or 28 — democratic, mobility, language, and gender-equality rights are immune.
  • Treating Charter jurisprudence as politically neutral — appointments to the SCC are made by the PM and shape doctrine over decades.

⚖️ 1.6 Judicial Review and the Separation of Powers
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Judicial review is the power of courts to review legislation and executive action for compliance with the constitution, and to declare unconstitutional measures invalid or of no force or effect. In Canada, this power flows from section 52(1) of the Constitution Act, 1982: "The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law of Canada, and any law that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect." Section 24(1) gives every court of "competent jurisdiction" the power to grant Charter remedies. Before 1982, judicial review of federal-provincial division of powers existed (since Hodge v. The Queen, 1883), but rights review was narrow. The 1982 entrenchment transformed Canadian courts — especially the SCC — into a major political actor.

The leading cases establish a recognisable shape for modern Canadian judicial review. R. v. Oakes (1986) gave the section 1 framework. R. v. Morgentaler (1988) struck down the federal abortion law as a section 7 (life, liberty, and security) violation. Vriend v. Alberta (1998) read sexual orientation into the Alberta human-rights act under section 15 by "reading in." Reference re Same-Sex Marriage (2004) and Halpern v. Canada (Ontario CA 2003) led to Civil Marriage Act (2005). Carter v. Canada (2015) struck down the prohibition on physician-assisted dying. R. v. Jordan (2016) imposed presumptive ceilings on criminal trial delay. These decisions are foundational; they show the Court doing what some critics call "judicial activism" and others call "constitutional fidelity."

The "dialogue theory" of judicial review (Hogg and Bushell, 1997) describes the Canadian system as an iterative conversation between Court and Parliament. The Court strikes a law; Parliament reformulates; the Court considers the reformulation. Examples: after Morgentaler, Parliament tried (and failed) to pass a replacement abortion law (1989–91 Bill C-43); after Carter, Parliament passed the Medical Assistance in Dying Act (2016), then expanded it in 2021 (Bill C-7), with further amendments pending; after Jordan, federal and provincial governments invested in court capacity. The dialogue is imperfect — sometimes Parliament cannot or will not act — but it distinguishes the Canadian model from the U.S. model, where Supreme Court decisions are typically treated as the final word for decades.

The separation of powers in Canadian constitutional theory is softer than in the U.S. model. The Westminster fusion of executive and legislative branches means there is no strict separation between Cabinet and Parliament. But judicial independence is robustly protected. Section 99 of the Constitution Act, 1867 grants superior-court judges security of tenure to age 75; remuneration is constitutionally protected (PEI Judges Reference, 1997); judicial appointments are made by the federal Cabinet with various advisory bodies (the Judicial Advisory Committees for trial courts; the 2016 reformed process for SCC appointments). The Supreme Court is the apex court for all law, civil and criminal, and its decisions bind all lower courts. The 2014 Reference re Senate Reform and Nadon Reference demonstrated that the Court is willing to assert its own structural independence as an unwritten constitutional principle.

  • Judicial review — the power of courts to assess government action for constitutional compliance.
  • Section 52(1) — the supremacy clause of the Constitution Act, 1982.
  • Dialogue theory (Hogg & Bushell) — the iterative Court-Parliament exchange in Canadian constitutional politics.
  • Reading in / reading down — Charter remedies that modify a statute rather than strike it down entirely.
  • Judicial independence — security of tenure, financial security, and administrative independence; an unwritten constitutional principle (PEI Judges Reference).

Dialogue Theory in Practice — Medical Assistance in Dying

In Carter v. Canada (2015), the SCC unanimously struck down sections 14 and 241(b) of the Criminal Code insofar as they prohibited physician-assisted dying for competent adults with a "grievous and irremediable medical condition." The Court suspended its declaration for 12 (then 16) months to give Parliament time to legislate. Parliament responded with Bill C-14 (2016) creating a federal framework but limiting eligibility to those whose "natural death has become reasonably foreseeable." Truchon v. Canada (Quebec Superior Court 2019) struck down this restriction as further unconstitutional. Parliament passed Bill C-7 (2021), removing the foreseeable-death requirement, with expansion to mental-illness-only cases scheduled (and successively delayed, most recently to 2027). This is dialogue theory: Court speaks, Parliament refines, lower court intervenes again, Parliament adjusts. Political significance: Canada now has one of the most permissive MAID regimes globally. Objectives and results: stated objective of reasonable access for the suffering; actual results include rapidly rising MAID numbers (~4.7% of Canadian deaths in 2023) and intense debate over disability-rights and economic-coercion concerns. Stability and change: a decade of recurring institutional adjustment with no settled equilibrium. Political perspective: rights advocates see the trajectory as progress; disability scholars (Catherine Frazee, Heidi Janz) see it as endangering vulnerable populations. A Level-4 analysis holds all of this together.

  • Treating judicial review as inherently illegitimate ("unelected judges") — it is constitutionally mandated by section 52 and accepted across the Canadian political spectrum.
  • Confusing judicial activism with judicial review — every Charter decision is technically judicial review; "activism" is a contested political label.
  • Forgetting the role of provincial superior courts and Courts of Appeal — most Charter cases never reach the SCC.
  • Treating SCC decisions as fixed forever — they can be modified by constitutional amendment (very hard in Canada), by section 33 invocation (in limited domains), or by the Court itself revisiting earlier doctrine.
  • Missing the comparative point that the U.S. Supreme Court is far more political (lifetime appointments, partisan confirmation hearings, much wider doctrinal swings) than the SCC.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "analyse the role of judicial review and the separation of powers in Canadian government."
