1.1 — The Six Classes of Nutrients
Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, vitamins, minerals, water — overview, sources, primary functions, and the macronutrient/micronutrient distinction.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A nutrient is any chemical compound in food that the body uses to grow, repair, regulate, or generate energy. Although thousands of compounds appear in human diets, nutrition science groups them into six classes — carbohydrates, lipids (fats and oils), proteins, vitamins, minerals, and water. The first three are called macronutrients because we need them in large quantities (grams to hundreds of grams per day) and because they are the only nutrients that supply energy. Vitamins and minerals are micronutrients — required in milligram or microgram amounts; they enable enzymes to function but do not, on their own, deliver kilocalories. Water is in a category of its own: it provides no calories but accounts for roughly 60% of adult body mass and is essential for every biochemical reaction.

The energy yield of the macronutrients is fixed and worth memorising. Carbohydrate provides \(4 \text{ kcal/g}\), protein \(4 \text{ kcal/g}\), and fat \(9 \text{ kcal/g}\). Alcohol, though not a nutrient, provides \(7 \text{ kcal/g}\). These four numbers (4-4-9-7) let you estimate the energy in any food from its macronutrient grams listed on the Nutrition Facts table. Health Canada calls these energy yielding nutrients; together they make up the AMDR (Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range): 45–65% of total energy from carbohydrate, 20–35% from fat, and 10–35% from protein for adults.

A balanced diet contains all six classes in the right proportions. Most Canadian deficiencies today are not gross macronutrient deficits but rather inadequate intake of fibre, calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, and potassium — alongside excesses of sodium, added sugars, and saturated fat. The 2019 Canada's Food Guide replaces older "servings" with proportional plate guidance: half the plate vegetables and fruit, one quarter whole grains, one quarter protein foods. Hydration is recommended from water as the beverage of choice.

  • Nutrient — any compound in food the body uses for growth, repair, regulation, or energy.
  • Macronutrient — needed in gram amounts (carbohydrate, lipid, protein).
  • Micronutrient — needed in mg or µg amounts (vitamins, minerals).
  • Kilocalorie (kcal) — energy to raise 1 kg of water by 1 °C; the "Calorie" on food labels.
  • AMDR — Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range: 45–65% C, 20–35% F, 10–35% P.
  • Essential nutrient — one the body cannot synthesise; must come from food.

Worked example — Calculating kilocalories from a food label

A granola bar lists 30 g carbohydrate, 6 g protein, and 10 g fat per serving. How many kilocalories does it contain, and what percentage of energy comes from fat?

Carbohydrate energy: \(30 \times 4 = 120\) kcal. Protein energy: \(6 \times 4 = 24\) kcal. Fat energy: \(10 \times 9 = 90\) kcal. Total: \(120 + 24 + 90 = 234\) kcal per serving. Fraction from fat: \(\frac{90}{234} \approx 0.385 = 38.5\%\). This exceeds the upper end of the AMDR (35%), so the bar is fat-dense compared with the recommendation.

  • Forgetting that vitamins and minerals provide zero kilocalories — they enable energy release but do not contain it.
  • Calling water a "macronutrient" — it is essential but is not energy-yielding, so it sits in its own class.
  • Saying carbohydrate provides "9 kcal/g" — that is fat. Carbs and protein are both 4 kcal/g.
  • Treating "Calorie" on a label as a small-c calorie. One food "Calorie" = 1 000 calories = 1 kilocalorie.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "identify the six classes of nutrients (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, vitamins, minerals, water) and describe the primary function of each in the body."

1.2 — Carbohydrates: Simple, Complex, and Fibre
Monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides, soluble vs insoluble fibre, glycemic index, and the role of added sugars in chronic disease.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Carbohydrates are the body's preferred energy source. They are organic compounds containing carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in the general ratio \((CH_2O)_n\), and they are classified by the number of sugar units they contain. Monosaccharides are single-sugar units — glucose (the form blood transports), fructose (the sugar in fruit and honey), and galactose (one half of lactose). Disaccharides are two monosaccharides joined: sucrose (glucose + fructose, table sugar), lactose (glucose + galactose, milk sugar), and maltose (glucose + glucose, in malted grains and beer). Mono- and disaccharides are collectively called simple sugars. Polysaccharides are long chains of many monosaccharides: starch (the storage carbohydrate of plants — potatoes, rice, bread, pasta), glycogen (the storage form in animal liver and muscle), and the fibres cellulose and pectin.

Dietary fibre is the portion of plant food that humans cannot digest. Soluble fibre (oats, beans, apples, psyllium) dissolves in water, forms a gel, lowers LDL cholesterol, and slows glucose absorption. Insoluble fibre (whole-wheat bran, vegetables, nuts) adds bulk, accelerates transit time, and reduces constipation and colorectal-cancer risk. Health Canada's adequate intake is 25 g/day for women and 38 g/day for men — most Canadians consume only about half that.

The glycemic index (GI) ranks foods by how rapidly they raise blood glucose. Pure glucose has GI = 100. White bread, mashed potatoes, and sugary cereals are high-GI (≥70); whole-grain oats, lentils, and most non-starchy vegetables are low-GI (≤55). Low-GI diets are associated with better appetite control and reduced risk of type-2 diabetes. Added sugars — sugars added during processing or preparation, including sucrose, high-fructose corn syrup, honey, and concentrated fruit juice — should provide less than 10% of total energy (WHO recommendation), or less than 5% for the maximum health benefit.

  • Monosaccharide — single sugar (glucose, fructose, galactose).
  • Disaccharide — two sugars joined (sucrose, lactose, maltose).
  • Polysaccharide — long chain (starch, glycogen, cellulose).
  • Dietary fibre — plant carbohydrate humans cannot digest; soluble or insoluble.
  • Glycemic index — relative rate of blood-glucose rise after a food, glucose = 100.
  • Added sugars — sugars added during processing, excluding naturally occurring sugars in fruit and milk.

Worked example — Spotting added sugar on a Nutrition Facts table

A 30 g serving of breakfast cereal lists Total Sugars 14 g. The ingredient list reads: "Whole-grain wheat, sugar, corn syrup, raisins, salt, malt extract." Estimate the added sugar content and the daily-value (DV) percentage given that the Canadian DV for total sugars is 100 g.

Two of the added-sugar sources (sugar and corn syrup) appear in the top three ingredients, indicating they dominate. Raisins are a natural source, but their contribution is small at the 30 g serving size — perhaps 1–2 g of natural fructose. Therefore approximately \(14 - 2 = 12\) g per serving is added sugar. As %DV: \(\frac{12}{100} \times 100 = 12\%\) of the daily limit in a single 30 g portion. Eating one cup (~50 g) would push the added-sugar contribution toward 20% DV — substantial.

  • Calling all carbohydrate "bad". Fibre and resistant starch are protective; the problem is added sugars and refined starches.
  • Equating "Total Sugars" with "Added Sugars" — the former includes natural sugars in milk and fruit.
  • Treating "low-fat" yogurt as low-sugar. Many low-fat yogurts add sugar to replace lost flavour.
  • Believing fruit juice is equivalent to whole fruit — juice lacks fibre and has a higher glycemic load.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "describe the structures, sources, and functions of carbohydrates (monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides, and dietary fibre) and explain the effect of these carbohydrates on human health."

1.3 — Lipids: Saturated, Unsaturated, Trans, and Essential Fatty Acids
Triglycerides, fatty-acid structure, sources of saturated vs unsaturated fats, the 2018 Canadian trans-fat ban, omega-3 and omega-6 essentials.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Lipids — commonly called fats and oils — are the most energy-dense macronutrient at \(9 \text{ kcal/g}\). The dietary lipids are mostly triglycerides: a glycerol "backbone" linked to three fatty acid tails by ester bonds. Fatty acids are long hydrocarbon chains classified by the number of C=C double bonds they contain. Saturated fatty acids have no C=C bonds; their straight tails pack tightly, so they are solid at room temperature (butter, beef tallow, coconut oil). Unsaturated fatty acids have one (monounsaturated, like olive oil's oleic acid) or more (polyunsaturated, like sunflower oil's linoleic acid) C=C bonds; the resulting kinks prevent close packing, so they are liquid at room temperature.

Trans fats are produced industrially by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils. Unlike the kinked cis-double bond that occurs in nature, the trans-configuration leaves the chain straight, mimicking saturated fat's packing behaviour but raising LDL ("bad") cholesterol while lowering HDL ("good") cholesterol — a uniquely damaging combination. Effective September 17, 2018, Health Canada banned partially hydrogenated oils in food sold in Canada, eliminating most artificial trans fat from the food supply. Small amounts of naturally occurring ruminant trans fats remain in beef and dairy.

Two fatty acids are essential — the body cannot synthesise them and must obtain them from food. Linoleic acid (omega-6) is abundant in vegetable oils. Alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3) is found in flaxseed, chia, walnuts, canola oil, and especially fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines) where it occurs alongside the long-chain omega-3s EPA and DHA. EPA and DHA are precursors of anti-inflammatory eicosanoids and are critical for brain and retinal development. Health Canada and the Dietitians of Canada recommend at least two servings of fatty fish per week (≈ 150 g cooked) for cardiovascular and developmental benefit.

  • Triglyceride — one glycerol + three fatty acids; the storage form of dietary lipid.
  • Saturated fatty acid — no C=C double bonds, solid at room temperature.
  • Monounsaturated / polyunsaturated — one / two or more C=C double bonds.
  • Trans fat — straight-chain unsaturated fat produced by industrial hydrogenation; banned in Canada since 2018.
  • Essential fatty acid — must come from food: omega-3 (alpha-linolenic) and omega-6 (linoleic).
  • HDL vs LDL — "good" cholesterol carries cholesterol away from arteries; "bad" deposits it in artery walls.

Worked example — Comparing the fat profile of two oils

A 14 g (1 Tbsp) serving of butter contains 7 g saturated, 3 g monounsaturated, 0.5 g polyunsaturated, and 0.5 g trans (ruminant) fat. A 14 g serving of olive oil contains 2 g saturated, 10 g monounsaturated, 1 g polyunsaturated, and 0 g trans. Which is the better cardiovascular choice and why?

Olive oil delivers far more monounsaturated fat (≈10 g vs 3 g) and less saturated fat (2 g vs 7 g) per equivalent serving, and contains no trans fat. Replacing saturated fat with monounsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol while preserving HDL, reducing cardiovascular-disease risk. The Mediterranean dietary pattern emphasises this substitution, and meta-analyses (e.g., PREDIMED, 2013) show ≈30% relative risk reduction for major cardiovascular events.

  • Avoiding all dietary fat. Essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) require some dietary fat for absorption.
  • Treating "cholesterol-free" labels as healthier. Many cholesterol-free foods (palm oil, coconut oil) are still high in saturated fat.
  • Believing trans fats no longer matter — small amounts in ruminant products remain, and the 2018 ban applies to manufactured Canadian foods only.
  • Mistaking omega-3 for omega-6 — the ratio in Western diets is heavily skewed toward omega-6, so omega-3 sources should be prioritised.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "describe the structures, sources, and functions of lipids (saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated, and trans fats; essential fatty acids) and explain the relationship between dietary fat and health (cardiovascular disease)."

1.4 — Proteins, Amino Acids, and Protein Complementation
Essential vs non-essential amino acids, complete vs incomplete proteins, sources, and how plant-based diets combine grains and legumes.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Proteins are polymers of amino acids. Twenty amino acids are used to build human proteins; of these, nine are essential — the body cannot synthesise them and must obtain them from food. The essential amino acids are histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine (mnemonic: "Pvt Tim Hall" → P, V, T, T, I, M, H, A, L for phenylalanine, valine, tryptophan, threonine, isoleucine, methionine, histidine, arginine[*], leucine, lysine — note that arginine is only conditionally essential in infants). Proteins serve every role imaginable: structural (collagen in skin and bones, keratin in hair), enzymatic (every digestive enzyme), transport (haemoglobin), immune (antibodies), and signalling (insulin, growth hormone). They also yield \(4 \text{ kcal/g}\) when used for energy, although the body prefers to spare protein for biosynthesis.

A complete protein contains all nine essential amino acids in adequate amounts. Most animal sources — meat, fish, poultry, eggs, dairy — are complete. Among plant foods, soy (tofu, tempeh, edamame), quinoa, buckwheat, hemp seed, and amaranth are also complete. An incomplete protein is low in one or more essential amino acids — that one is called the limiting amino acid. Grains are typically limited in lysine; legumes (beans, lentils, peas, peanuts) are typically limited in methionine. Protein complementation combines two incomplete sources whose limiting amino acids differ, producing a complete amino-acid profile. Classic complementary combinations include rice + beans, hummus (chickpeas) + pita, peanut butter + bread, and lentil + naan. Older nutrition texts insisted that complementary proteins must be eaten at the same meal; current evidence (Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016) shows that variety over the day is sufficient.

RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) for protein in healthy adults is \(0.8 \text{ g}\) protein per kilogram body weight per day. Adolescents (growth), pregnant women, breast-feeding women, and serious endurance/strength athletes may require 1.0–1.6 g/kg.

  • Amino acid — building block of protein; 20 amino acids form human proteins.
  • Essential amino acid — body cannot synthesise; must come from food (9 in adults).
  • Complete protein — contains all 9 essential amino acids in adequate amounts.
  • Incomplete protein — limited in one or more essential amino acids.
  • Limiting amino acid — the essential amino acid in shortest supply in a given source.
  • Protein complementation — combining two incomplete sources with different limiting amino acids.
  • RDA — 0.8 g protein/kg body weight/day for healthy adults.

Worked example — Daily protein requirement and meal planning

An active 55 kg adolescent female on a vegetarian diet wants to know her protein RDA and whether her usual lunch — a bowl of brown rice with black beans — provides complete protein. Use the 0.8 g/kg standard plus 25% for active adolescent growth.

RDA: \(55 \text{ kg} \times 0.8 \text{ g/kg} = 44\) g/day baseline. Adolescent growth allowance (~+25%) raises target to \(44 \times 1.25 = 55\) g/day. Her lunch: 1 cup brown rice (≈5 g protein, limited in lysine) + 1 cup black beans (≈15 g protein, limited in methionine and tryptophan). Together they total ≈20 g protein with a complete amino-acid profile because rice provides methionine and beans provide lysine. This lunch covers ≈36% of her daily target — she should still consume two more protein-containing meals or snacks (yogurt, tofu, eggs, hemp seeds) to reach 55 g.

  • Believing "vegetarians cannot get complete protein". A varied plant diet readily provides all essential amino acids.
  • Eating very high-protein diets (>2 g/kg) believing it builds extra muscle — surplus protein is converted to fat or used for energy.
  • Confusing essential amino acids with essential fatty acids — both are essential, but they are different molecules.
  • Ignoring lysine adequacy in vegan diets dominated by grains; combine grains with legumes to compensate.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "describe the structures, sources, and functions of proteins; distinguish between essential and non-essential amino acids; explain protein complementation in plant-based diets."

1.5 — Vitamins: Fat-Soluble and Water-Soluble
Fat-soluble A, D, E, K (storage, toxicity risk) vs water-soluble B-complex and C (daily intake needed, urinary excretion), key sources and deficiency syndromes.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts to enable normal metabolism, growth, and immunity. There are 13 vitamins divided into two solubility classes. The four fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) dissolve in dietary fat and bile, are stored mainly in liver and adipose tissue, and can reach toxic levels (hypervitaminosis) if taken as megadose supplements over time. The nine water-soluble vitamins — the eight B-complex vitamins (\(B_1\) thiamin, \(B_2\) riboflavin, \(B_3\) niacin, \(B_5\) pantothenic acid, \(B_6\) pyridoxine, \(B_7\) biotin, \(B_9\) folate, \(B_{12}\) cobalamin) plus vitamin C — dissolve in body water, are stored only briefly, and excess is generally excreted in urine, so toxicity from food is rare but daily intake is necessary.

The Canadian deficiency landscape today is dominated by three vitamins. Vitamin D: synthesised in skin from UV-B exposure, but Canadian latitude restricts production from October through April. Health Canada recommends 600 IU/day for ages 1–70 and 800 IU/day above 70; supplementation is widely advised, especially for breast-fed infants (400 IU/day routinely) and people with darker skin. Folate (B9): white flour has been mandatorily fortified with folic acid since 1998 to reduce neural-tube defects in pregnancy (Spina bifida and anencephaly fell ≈50% within five years). Vitamin B12: found almost exclusively in animal foods; vegans and many older adults (gastric atrophy reduces absorption) must rely on fortified plant milks, nutritional yeast, or supplements.

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) deserves separate mention: it is required for collagen synthesis, immune function, and enhances non-heme iron absorption. Adult RDA is 75 mg/day for women and 90 mg/day for men, with an additional 35 mg/day for smokers. Severe deficiency causes scurvy — bleeding gums, easy bruising, poor wound healing — once endemic among sailors. James Lind's 1747 controlled trial on HMS Salisbury demonstrated that citrus fruit prevented scurvy; this is regarded as one of the first clinical trials in nutrition history.

  • Vitamin — organic micronutrient required in small amounts for normal metabolism.
  • Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) — stored in liver/adipose; toxicity from megadoses possible.
  • Water-soluble (B-complex, C) — limited storage; daily intake required; excess urinated.
  • Hypervitaminosis — toxic accumulation of a vitamin.
  • Fortification — adding a nutrient to a food to address a population deficiency (e.g., folic acid in flour).
  • Deficiency syndromes — rickets (D), beri-beri (B1), pellagra (B3), pernicious anaemia (B12), scurvy (C).

Worked example — Designing a vitamin-D plan for a Canadian winter

A 16-year-old in Toronto (43° N) takes no supplements, drinks two cups (500 mL) of vitamin-D-fortified milk per day (100 IU per 250 mL serving = 200 IU/day total), and rarely eats fish. Her dermatologist instructs her to avoid sun exposure. Calculate whether her intake meets Health Canada's recommendation of 600 IU/day from November to March.

Dietary intake: \(2 \times 100 = 200\) IU/day from milk; salmon and egg yolks would add the rest, but she rarely eats fish. Endogenous synthesis: ≈0 IU because UV-B at 43° N is too weak from November to March and she avoids sun. Total: ≈200 IU/day — well below the 600 IU recommendation. She should take a 400 IU/day vitamin-D3 supplement to close the gap, bringing intake to ≈600 IU/day. (Health Canada's tolerable upper intake level (UL) is 4 000 IU/day for ages 9+, so a small supplement carries negligible toxicity risk.)

  • Assuming a "balanced diet" automatically provides enough vitamin D in Canada — winter UV-B is insufficient regardless of food intake.
  • Megadosing fat-soluble vitamins A or D from supplements — toxicity (liver damage, hypercalcaemia) is real.
  • Believing vitamin C "prevents colds" in doses above 200 mg/day — Cochrane reviews show only a small symptom-duration effect.
  • Forgetting that vegan diets must include a reliable B12 source (fortified beverages, nutritional yeast, or supplements).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.5 — "describe the functions, food sources, and recommended intakes of major vitamins (fat-soluble A, D, E, K; water-soluble B-complex and C) and explain deficiency syndromes relevant in Canada."

1.6 — Minerals and Water
Calcium, iron, sodium, potassium, iodine, zinc; the role of water; daily fluid needs; electrolyte balance.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Minerals are inorganic micronutrients — elements rather than organic molecules. They are subdivided by daily need. Major minerals (need >100 mg/day) include calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, potassium, chloride, and sulfur. Trace minerals (need <100 mg/day) include iron, zinc, copper, iodine, selenium, fluoride, manganese, chromium, and molybdenum. Unlike vitamins, minerals are not destroyed by cooking heat, but they can be leached into cooking water or removed during processing (e.g., milling refined white flour removes magnesium and zinc).

Three minerals dominate Canadian public-health concerns. Calcium: critical for bones, teeth, muscle contraction, and nerve signalling. Adolescents need 1 300 mg/day; sources include milk, fortified plant milks, cheese, yogurt, canned salmon with bones, kale, and tofu set with calcium sulfate. Iron: required for haemoglobin (oxygen transport) and many enzymes. Adolescent menstruators need 15 mg/day; sources include red meat (heme iron, well absorbed), legumes, dark green vegetables, and iron-fortified cereals (non-heme iron, less well absorbed but enhanced by vitamin C taken at the same meal). Sodium: needed in only ~1 500 mg/day, but most Canadians consume 3 000–4 000 mg/day, almost all from processed foods. Health Canada's tolerable upper intake level is 2 300 mg/day; excess sodium drives hypertension and cardiovascular disease.

Water accounts for ≈ 60% of adult body mass and is the medium for every biochemical reaction. Health Canada's adequate intake (AI) from all beverages and food is 2.7 L/day for women and 3.7 L/day for men (food provides ~20%, so beverage targets are ≈ 2.2 L and 3.0 L respectively). Thirst is a reliable indicator for most healthy adults, but older adults, children, and people exercising in heat should drink before thirst arrives. Sport drinks (with sodium and carbohydrate) make sense for exercise lasting longer than 60 minutes; for ordinary activity, water is sufficient. Electrolytes — primarily \(Na^+\), \(K^+\), \(Cl^-\), \(Ca^{2+}\), and \(Mg^{2+}\) — maintain fluid balance, blood pressure, and neuromuscular function.

  • Major mineral — needed >100 mg/day (Ca, P, Mg, Na, K, Cl, S).
  • Trace mineral — needed <100 mg/day (Fe, Zn, Cu, I, Se, F, Mn, Cr, Mo).
  • Heme iron — from animal sources, bioavailability ~25%.
  • Non-heme iron — from plant or fortified sources, bioavailability ~5–15%; enhanced by vitamin C.
  • Electrolyte — mineral ion (Na+, K+, Cl−, Ca2+, Mg2+) that maintains fluid and pH balance.
  • Adequate intake (AI) of water — 2.7 L/day women; 3.7 L/day men (all sources).

Worked example — Estimating sodium intake from one meal

A 16-year-old eats one cup of canned vegetable soup (760 mg Na), one slice of frozen pizza (640 mg Na), 30 g of crackers (220 mg Na), and a 355 mL can of regular cola (40 mg Na). What is his total sodium for this meal, and what percentage of Health Canada's 2 300 mg/day upper-intake-level (UL) does it represent?

Total sodium: \(760 + 640 + 220 + 40 = 1\,660\) mg in a single meal. Percentage of UL: \(\frac{1\,660}{2\,300} \times 100 \approx 72\%\). One meal therefore eats up almost three-quarters of the day's safe sodium limit, and he has not yet eaten breakfast or dinner. The driver is the processed foods (soup, frozen pizza, crackers); a meal of an equivalent home-prepared lentil soup with whole-grain toast would contain only ~400 mg of sodium.

  • Believing sea salt is "lower in sodium" than table salt — gram for gram they are equivalent (~390 mg Na per gram of salt).
  • Neglecting calcium during the teenage years — peak bone mass is set by about age 25 and cannot be recovered later.
  • Assuming "drink 8 glasses of water" is a rule. The official AI includes water, tea, coffee, milk, and food.
  • Pairing iron-fortified cereal with milk and tea simultaneously — calcium and tannins reduce non-heme iron absorption; pair with vitamin C (orange juice) instead.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.5 — "describe the functions, food sources, and recommended intakes of major and trace minerals, the role of water in human physiology, and the implications of excess sodium intake for cardiovascular health."
