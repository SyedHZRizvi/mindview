3.1 — Canada's Food Guide (2019): The Plate Model
History of the Food Guide, the 2019 revision, plate proportions (½ vegetables & fruit, ¼ whole grains, ¼ protein foods), water as the beverage of choice.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada has issued national dietary guidance since 1942, when wartime rationing prompted the Official Food Rules. The Guide has been revised in 1949, 1961, 1977, 1982, 1992, 2007, and most recently 2019. Older versions (1992 and 2007) used four "food groups" — Grain Products, Vegetables and Fruit, Milk and Alternatives, Meat and Alternatives — and a per-day serving system (e.g., "6–7 servings of grains, 2 servings of milk"). The 2019 Canada's Food Guide replaces servings with a plate model that emphasises proportions and food categories rather than counted portions.

The 2019 plate calls for half the plate vegetables and fruit, one-quarter whole grain foods, and one-quarter protein foods. The "protein foods" category collapses what used to be Milk and Meat groups into a single grouping that includes plant proteins (legumes, nuts, seeds, tofu), animal proteins (eggs, dairy, fish, lean meats), and emphasises plant-based protein "more often". A separate companion document, Healthy Eating Recommendations, contains three pillars: (1) be mindful of your eating habits (eat regularly, in a positive setting); (2) cook more often, plan, prepare, and share meals; (3) limit highly processed foods. Water is the beverage of choice, replacing the older requirement for two cups of milk per day. Sugar-sweetened beverages should be limited.

The plate model also moved away from species-specific recommendations toward food-pattern recommendations. For example, the 2019 Guide does not mandate dairy; calcium can be obtained from fortified soy beverages, calcium-set tofu, dark leafy greens, or canned salmon with bones. This reflects evolving evidence on dairy and bone health and accommodates lactose intolerance and cultural dietary patterns.

  • Canada's Food Guide — official Canadian dietary guidance; current version 2019.
  • Plate model — ½ vegetables & fruit, ¼ whole grains, ¼ protein foods.
  • Protein foods — plant (legumes, nuts, tofu) and animal (eggs, dairy, fish, meat); "plant-based more often".
  • Beverage of choice — water; sugar-sweetened beverages and excess fruit juice are limited.
  • Three pillars — be mindful; cook more often; limit highly processed foods.
  • Highly processed foods — products with added sugars, sodium, or saturated fat (e.g., ultra-processed snack foods).

Worked example — Evaluating a teenage lunch against the 2019 Food Guide

A student's lunch is: a 30 cm sub on white bread filled with 150 g processed deli meat, mayonnaise, and lettuce; a 500 mL bottle of cola; a chocolate-chip cookie. Critically evaluate this lunch using the plate model and the three healthy-eating pillars.

Plate model: the white-bread sub is a refined-grain food (not whole grain); the lettuce alone does not constitute a "half plate" of vegetables; the deli meat is highly processed and high in sodium and nitrites. The 500 mL of cola is a sugar-sweetened beverage instead of water. Pillars: pillar 1 — eating on the go is acceptable, but a calm setting would aid digestion; pillar 2 — the meal was likely bought and packaged elsewhere rather than prepared at home; pillar 3 — both the sub meat and the cola are highly processed. Improvements: switch to a whole-grain wrap with grilled chicken, hummus, and 1 cup of mixed peppers and carrots; replace the cola with water; replace the cookie with a piece of fruit.

  • Memorising 2007 "servings" — the 2019 Food Guide no longer uses serving counts.
  • Treating "protein foods" as meat only — beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, fish, and dairy all qualify.
  • Believing dairy is mandatory in the 2019 Guide — calcium can be obtained from fortified plant beverages, fish bones, and dark greens.
  • Conflating "low-fat" with "healthy" — many low-fat foods are highly processed and high in added sugar.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "evaluate personal food choices against Canada's Food Guide (2019), including the plate model and the healthy-eating recommendations, and identify changes that promote health."

3.2 — Reading Nutrition Facts Tables and Ingredient Lists
Mandatory format, Daily Value (DV), the 5%–15% rule, core 13 nutrients, ingredient list rules, "front-of-package" symbols.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Since 2003 (and significantly revised in 2016), the Nutrition Facts table has been mandatory on virtually all packaged foods sold in Canada. It has three components: a serving size at the top, the calorie value for that serving, and a list of core nutrients with both absolute amounts and "% Daily Value" (%DV). The current core 13 nutrients are: fat (saturated + trans), cholesterol, sodium, carbohydrate (fibre + sugars), protein, vitamin A and C (formerly), calcium, iron, potassium, and (after 2016) vitamin D. "Sugars" was updated in 2017–2022 to highlight added sugars with a %DV based on a 100 g/day reference.

The %DV is interpreted using the 5%–15% rule: 5% or less is "a little" of that nutrient per serving; 15% or more is "a lot". For nutrients to limit (sodium, saturated fat, added sugars, cholesterol) you want %DV to be low; for nutrients to encourage (fibre, vitamin D, calcium, iron, potassium) you want %DV to be high. The %DV uses a single reference value for everyone aged 4+ rather than age- and sex-specific targets. The Daily Value for sodium is 2 300 mg, for saturated + trans fat 20 g, for fibre 25 g, for total sugars 100 g, and for protein 50 g.

The ingredient list appears below or beside the Nutrition Facts and is listed in descending order by weight. Identifying added sugars requires recognising at least 60 different names — sugar, sucrose, glucose-fructose syrup (Canadian for high-fructose corn syrup), brown sugar, dextrose, maltose, honey, agave, brown-rice syrup, evaporated cane juice, and so on. Beware "sugar splitting": a manufacturer can divide one sweetener into several entries so that none individually reaches the top of the list. Food allergens (peanuts, tree nuts, sesame, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, soy, wheat, sulphites, mustard) must be declared in plain English on the label, separate from any tricky chemical names.

In 2025, Health Canada is implementing a mandatory front-of-package nutrition symbol on foods that exceed thresholds for saturated fat, sodium, or sugars. The black-and-white magnifying-glass symbol warns consumers without requiring them to read the back panel.

  • Nutrition Facts table — mandatory back-of-package nutrient panel.
  • Serving size — listed at top; all nutrient values are per serving.
  • % Daily Value (%DV) — percentage of a reference daily intake.
  • 5%–15% rule — ≤5% = "a little"; ≥15% = "a lot".
  • Ingredient list — descending order by weight; allergens declared plainly.
  • Front-of-package symbol — Health Canada 2025: black-and-white magnifier on foods high in sodium, saturated fat, or sugar.

Worked example — Comparing two cereals

Cereal A: 30 g serving, 150 kcal, fibre 1 g (4% DV), sugar 12 g (12% DV), sodium 220 mg (10% DV). First ingredients: sugar, whole-grain wheat, rice flour. Cereal B: 30 g serving, 110 kcal, fibre 5 g (20% DV), sugar 5 g (5% DV), sodium 65 mg (3% DV). First ingredients: whole-grain oats, whole-grain wheat, raisins. Apply the 5%–15% rule.

Fibre: A 4% (a little — bad), B 20% (a lot — good). Sugar: A 12% (more than "a little" — bad), B 5% (a little — good). Sodium: A 10% (moderate — could be lower), B 3% (a little — good). Ingredients: A's first ingredient is sugar (worrying); B leads with whole-grain oats. Conclusion: Cereal B is the better choice on every Nutrition Facts criterion and is also a more transparent ingredient list. Even though A is only 40 more kcal, the quality of the carbohydrate and fibre tips the comparison decisively to B.

  • Comparing nutrients between two foods at different serving sizes — always check the serving size first.
  • Reading sugar grams as "added sugar" — sugars include natural sugars from fruit and milk.
  • Ignoring sodium because the food doesn't taste salty (e.g., cottage cheese, bread, cereals).
  • Forgetting that "no added sugar" and "low sodium" are regulated claims with specific thresholds, while marketing terms like "natural" are not.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "evaluate the nutritional content of foods using the Nutrition Facts table, Daily Value (DV) percentages, and ingredient lists; apply the 5%–15% rule."

3.3 — Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) and Daily Values
EAR, RDA, AI, UL — what each represents; sex- and age-specific intake; how DVs simplify them for labels.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) are jointly set by Canadian and U.S. expert panels and published by the National Academies. They are the gold-standard nutrient-intake recommendations for North America. The DRI system has four components. Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) is the daily intake that meets the needs of 50% of healthy individuals in a sex/age group — useful for population planning and assessment but not as an individual target. Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) is set at EAR + 2 standard deviations and meets the needs of 97–98% of individuals — the most useful single individual target. Adequate Intake (AI) is used when data are insufficient to set an EAR/RDA (e.g., total fluid, vitamin D for infants); it represents the best estimate of an adequate intake. Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) is the maximum daily intake unlikely to cause harm in healthy people — above which toxicity risk rises (especially for fat-soluble vitamins, sodium, and trace minerals).

DRIs are sex- and age-specific and adjust for pregnancy and lactation. For example, the iron RDA is 8 mg/day for adult men but 18 mg/day for menstruating adult women, and rises to 27 mg/day in pregnancy. The vitamin D AI rises from 600 IU/day (ages 1–70) to 800 IU/day for ≥71. Calcium RDA is 1 300 mg/day for teenagers — higher than the adult 1 000 mg — to support peak bone mass acquisition.

The Daily Value used on Nutrition Facts tables is a regulatory simplification: a single number per nutrient that represents "a reasonable daily target" for everyone aged 4+. Because Daily Values cannot be age- and sex-specific, they aim at the upper end of the population's needs (e.g., DV for iron is 14 mg, intermediate between the men's RDA and the women's RDA), or at the desired ceiling for nutrients to limit (e.g., DV for sodium is 2 300 mg, equal to the UL). The %DV on a label is therefore an approximate, population-level reference; an individual with specific needs (athletes, pregnant women, those with chronic disease) should refer to their DRI directly.

  • DRI — Dietary Reference Intakes — Canada/US gold-standard recommendations.
  • EAR — meets needs of 50% of a sex/age group; for population assessment.
  • RDA — meets needs of 97–98%; the standard individual target.
  • AI — best estimate when EAR/RDA data are insufficient.
  • UL — maximum daily intake without harm; above this, toxicity risk increases.
  • Daily Value (DV) — regulatory label simplification; a single number for everyone 4+.

Worked example — Comparing label %DV with personal DRI

A 14-year-old female athlete reads "Iron 25% DV" on a fortified cereal serving. Calculate the iron grams that delivers, and compare with her actual iron RDA of 15 mg/day.

Iron DV on the label is 14 mg. 25% × 14 mg = 3.5 mg per serving. Her actual RDA is 15 mg/day; this single serving provides \(\frac{3.5}{15} \times 100 \approx 23\%\) of her individual RDA — close to the %DV but slightly lower because she has higher needs than the population reference. Two servings of this cereal plus an iron-rich evening meal (e.g., lentil curry) would readily meet her daily target.

  • Treating %DV as a personal recommendation rather than a population reference — pregnant women, athletes, and chronic-disease patients should compare to their individual DRIs.
  • Confusing EAR (50% of population) with RDA (97–98%) — RDA is the safer individual target.
  • Believing higher is always better up to the UL — for some nutrients (sodium, saturated fat) "high" %DV is undesirable.
  • Forgetting that the UL applies to total intake from food + supplements + fortified foods combined.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "describe the Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) and explain the relationship between %DV on Nutrition Facts tables and individual nutrient needs."

3.4 — Meal Planning Across the Lifespan
Infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, pregnancy, older adults — how nutrient needs change with age and life stage.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Nutrient needs are highest, relative to body size, during periods of rapid growth (infancy, adolescence, pregnancy, breast-feeding) and during recovery from illness. Infancy (0–6 months): Health Canada and the Canadian Paediatric Society recommend exclusive breast-feeding for the first 6 months. Breast milk supplies essentially all nutrients for that period, with a routine vitamin-D supplement of 400 IU/day for breast-fed infants. Iron-fortified infant cereal and iron-rich complementary foods (pureed meat, lentils, tofu) are introduced around 6 months because foetal iron stores deplete.

Childhood (1–8 years): nutrient density is critical — small stomachs but high needs. Children need adequate protein (~1.1 g/kg), calcium (700–1 000 mg), iron (7–10 mg), and vitamin D (600 IU). The "fruit-juice paradox": 100% juice is no substitute for whole fruit and is limited to ≤125 mL/day for ages 1–3 and ≤175 mL/day for ages 4–6 in Canadian guidelines.

Adolescence (9–18 years): this is the second period of rapid growth, with major increases in muscle mass and bone deposition. Daily energy requirement may exceed 2 200–3 000 kcal for active adolescents. Calcium needs peak at 1 300 mg/day, iron at 11 mg/day (males) or 15 mg/day (menstruating females), and protein at ~50–60 g/day. Iron-deficiency anaemia is common in menstruating adolescents; pair iron-rich plant foods with vitamin C to boost absorption.

Pregnancy: energy +0 kcal in trimester 1, +340 in T2, +452 in T3. Protein increases to 1.1 g/kg + an additional 25 g/day in T2 and T3. Folate needs jump to 600 µg/day (from 400 µg) and supplementation with 400 µg/day folic acid begins before conception to prevent neural-tube defects. Iron rises to 27 mg/day. Pregnant women should avoid raw fish (Listeria, parasites), high-mercury fish (swordfish, shark, marlin), unpasteurised dairy, and alcohol.

Older adults (65+): total energy needs fall (lower BMR, less activity), but several micronutrient needs rise: vitamin D to 800 IU, calcium remains at 1 200 mg, and vitamin B12 must come from synthetic sources (fortified foods or supplements) because gastric atrophy reduces absorption of food-bound B12. Adequate protein (≥1.0 g/kg) prevents sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss). Hydration is often inadequate because thirst sensation declines with age.

  • Exclusive breast-feeding — 0–6 months recommended; vitamin-D supplement 400 IU/day for breast-fed infants.
  • Adolescent calcium RDA — 1 300 mg/day; the bone-mass acquisition window.
  • Iron-deficiency anaemia — common in menstruating teens; pair iron with vitamin C.
  • Folic acid in pregnancy — 400 µg/day pre-conception to prevent NTDs.
  • Pregnancy avoidances — raw fish, high-mercury fish, unpasteurised dairy, alcohol.
  • Sarcopenia — age-related muscle loss; mitigated by adequate protein and resistance training.

Worked example — A one-day meal plan for a 17-year-old pregnant student (second trimester)

Energy target ~2 200 + 340 = 2 540 kcal; protein 1.1 × 60 kg + 25 g = ~91 g; folate 600 µg (plus 400 µg folic-acid supplement); iron 27 mg; calcium 1 300 mg. Outline a sample day.

Breakfast: 1 cup fortified oatmeal (8 g protein, 2 mg iron, 100 µg folate from oats + fortification) topped with ½ cup berries and 250 mL fortified soy milk (8 g protein, 300 mg calcium). Snack: a hard-boiled egg + apple (7 g protein, 25 µg folate). Lunch: spinach & lentil salad — 1 cup lentils (18 g protein, 6.5 mg iron, 360 µg folate), 2 cups spinach (4 g protein, 6.4 mg iron, 300 µg folate), ½ cup feta (8 g protein, 200 mg calcium), olive oil + lemon. Snack: 30 g almonds + 200 mL yogurt (10 g protein, 250 mg calcium). Dinner: 100 g baked salmon (25 g protein, omega-3 EPA/DHA), 1 cup brown rice (5 g protein, 0.8 mg iron), 1 cup roasted broccoli (4 g protein, 0.7 mg iron, 60 µg folate). Beverage all day: water + 1 cup fortified milk before bed (8 g protein, 300 mg calcium, 100 IU vitamin D). Plus 400 µg folic-acid supplement. Approximate totals: ~2 500 kcal, ~95 g protein, ~1 200 µg folate (food) + 400 µg (supplement), ~17 mg iron (food) — additional iron supplement may be needed to reach 27 mg, ~1 200 mg calcium.

  • Treating "eat for two" literally — energy needs in pregnancy rise modestly (340–452 kcal/day), not double.
  • Believing all juice is healthy for toddlers — whole fruit is preferred; juice is high in sugar and lacks fibre.
  • Forgetting that the adolescent calcium window cannot be reopened later — bone mass declines after about age 30.
  • Recommending B12-free vegan diets to older adults without supplementation — atrophic gastritis reduces food-bound B12 absorption.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "plan meals and menus that meet the dietary needs of specific groups across the lifespan (infants, children, adolescents, adults, pregnant/lactating women, older adults), including those with specific needs (athletes, vegetarians, food allergies)."

3.5 — Special Diets: Athletes, Vegetarians, Food Allergies, Chronic Disease
Macronutrient timing for athletes, balanced vegetarian/vegan meal patterns, allergy management, DASH and Mediterranean for hypertension and CVD.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Athletes have higher energy needs (1.5–2× sedentary), higher carbohydrate needs (5–10 g/kg/day for endurance athletes, used to refill muscle glycogen), and higher protein needs (1.2–1.6 g/kg for strength; up to 1.6–2.0 g/kg in intense training). Nutrient timing matters: carbohydrate before exercise fuels effort; carbohydrate + 20 g of leucine-rich protein within 30 minutes after exercise enhances muscle-protein synthesis. Hydration: ≥ 500 mL water 2 hours before exercise, sip 150–300 mL every 15–20 min during, and replace 1.5 L per kg of body weight lost after.

Vegetarians avoid meat (lacto-ovo: include dairy and eggs; pescatarian: include fish; vegan: avoid all animal products). Well-planned plant diets fully meet adolescent and adult nutrient needs and are associated with lower rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and ischaemic heart disease (Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016). The nutrients to monitor in vegan diets specifically are vitamin B12 (use fortified plant milks, nutritional yeast, or a supplement), iron (pair plant iron with vitamin C; expect ~2× higher needs than for omnivores), zinc, calcium, vitamin D (especially in Canadian winters), omega-3 (flax, chia, walnuts; consider algae-derived DHA), and iodine (iodised salt, sea vegetables).

Food allergies are IgE-mediated immune reactions to specific proteins. Health Canada has identified 11 priority allergens that must be labelled: peanuts, tree nuts (almonds, cashews, etc.), sesame, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish (crustaceans + molluscs), soy, wheat, sulphites, and mustard. An allergic reaction can range from hives to anaphylactic shock; epinephrine auto-injectors (EpiPen) are first-line treatment. Schools in Ontario fall under Sabrina's Law (2005), which mandates anaphylaxis policies including staff training and accessible epinephrine.

Chronic-disease diets: the DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) emphasises vegetables, fruit, whole grains, low-fat dairy, lean protein, and limits sodium to 1 500–2 300 mg/day; it lowers systolic blood pressure by ~6–11 mmHg. The Mediterranean diet centres olive oil, fish, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and red wine in moderation; the 2013 PREDIMED trial showed ~30% reduction in major cardiovascular events. Diabetes management uses carbohydrate counting: 15 g carbohydrate = "1 carb choice", typically 2–4 choices per meal.

  • Endurance athlete carbohydrate need — 5–10 g/kg/day to fuel glycogen.
  • Vegan supplement priority — B12, vitamin D (winter), iodine, omega-3 DHA.
  • 11 priority allergens — peanuts, tree nuts, sesame, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, soy, wheat, sulphites, mustard.
  • Anaphylaxis — severe allergic reaction; first-line treatment = intramuscular epinephrine.
  • DASH diet — lowers systolic BP ~6–11 mmHg; cap sodium 1 500–2 300 mg.
  • Mediterranean diet — ~30% reduction in major CVD events (PREDIMED 2013).
  • Carb choice — 15 g of carbohydrate; basic unit of diabetes meal planning.

Worked example — Post-workout snack for a hockey player

A 70 kg adolescent hockey player finishes a 90-minute practice with intense sprint intervals. Design a recovery snack delivering ~50 g carbohydrate (for muscle-glycogen replenishment, ~1 g/kg) and ~20 g leucine-rich protein within 30 minutes.

Option 1: 250 mL chocolate milk + a medium banana. Chocolate milk has been validated as a recovery beverage in multiple trials (≈ 25 g carbohydrate + ~8 g whey/casein protein); banana adds ~25 g carbohydrate and potassium for fluid balance. Add ½ cup of plain Greek yogurt (~10 g protein) to reach the 20 g protein target. Drink 500 mL water alongside. Total: ~50 g carbohydrate, ~18 g protein (close to target), plus electrolytes — meets the post-exercise window for maximum glycogen and muscle protein synthesis.

  • Believing that vegetarians cannot get enough protein — well-planned plant diets readily meet protein needs without animal meat.
  • Confusing food allergy (IgE, can be fatal) with food intolerance (e.g., lactose intolerance — uncomfortable but not life-threatening).
  • Recommending high-protein diets indiscriminately for everyone — kidney patients and very-low-carbohydrate "keto" extremes require medical supervision.
  • Treating sports drinks as universally needed — water suffices for exercise <60 minutes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "plan meal patterns that meet the specific needs of athletes, vegetarians, individuals with food allergies, and people managing chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, coeliac disease)."
