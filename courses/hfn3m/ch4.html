4.1 — From Farm to Fork: The Canadian Food Supply Chain
Primary production, processing, distribution, retail, foodservice, consumer; the role of CFIA, Health Canada, and the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The food on a Canadian table has passed through a long supply chain. Primary production is farming, ranching, fishing, hunting, and harvesting. Ontario's primary producers grow wheat, corn, soybeans, fruit (especially in the Niagara region), greenhouse vegetables, dairy, poultry, beef, and pork. Marine and freshwater producers harvest cod and lobster on the East Coast, salmon and halibut on the West Coast, and pickerel and whitefish in the Great Lakes. Processing turns raw commodities into intermediate goods (milling wheat to flour) and finished products (bread, cheese, frozen pizza). Distribution moves products by truck, rail, ship, and air to warehouses. Retail and foodservice (grocery stores, restaurants, school cafeterias) deliver food to consumers, who close the chain by purchasing, preparing, and eating.

Regulation is shared. Health Canada sets food-safety standards and nutrition labelling rules through the Food and Drugs Act. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) enforces compliance under the Safe Food for Canadians Act (2012) and its regulations (2019), inspects federally registered processors, oversees food recalls, and issues import/export licences. Provincial ministries inspect locally registered processors, restaurants, and school cafeterias. The 2008 Maple Leaf Foods listeriosis outbreak — 23 deaths from contaminated cold cuts — drove the shift to the modern integrated regulatory regime, including mandatory Preventive Control Plans (PCPs) for all federally registered businesses.

"Local" foods are typically defined as produced within the same province or within a 50-km radius. Local foods often (but not always) require less transport energy and may be fresher; however, year-round Canadian production of many fruits and vegetables is impossible, so off-season imports are needed. The Foodland Ontario logo identifies Ontario-grown produce. Organic certification (Canada Organic Regime) restricts synthetic pesticides, synthetic fertilisers, GMOs, antibiotics and growth hormones in livestock, and food irradiation; nutritional differences between organic and conventional foods are typically small. Genetically modified (GM) foods — canola, corn, soy, sugar beet — are widely grown in Canada; Health Canada has assessed them for safety but does not require GM-specific labelling.

  • Primary production — farming, ranching, fishing.
  • Processing — milling, baking, freezing, packaging.
  • CFIA — Canadian Food Inspection Agency; enforces SFCA.
  • Safe Food for Canadians Act — 2012 Act, 2019 Regulations.
  • Maple Leaf 2008 — listeriosis outbreak (23 deaths); drove modern food-safety reforms.
  • Foodland Ontario — logo identifying provincially grown produce.
  • Organic / GMO — regulated labels; nutrient differences are typically small.

Worked example — Following a wheat-to-bread chain

Trace the journey of a slice of whole-grain bread sold in a Toronto grocery store from southern Ontario to the consumer's table, identifying the regulatory body at each major step.

Step 1 — Field: a farmer grows hard red winter wheat near London, Ontario. Provincial agriculture standards apply. Step 2 — Elevator: harvest is graded and sold to a grain elevator. Step 3 — Mill: a federally registered mill in Mississauga grinds wheat into whole-grain flour. CFIA enforces SFCR. Step 4 — Bakery: the flour goes to a federally registered bakery that mixes, ferments, and bakes loaves. Health Canada has set the standard for added folic-acid fortification of wheat flour since 1998. Step 5 — Distribution: refrigerated trucks deliver bread to a regional warehouse. Step 6 — Retail: a Toronto grocer (provincially inspected) stocks the loaf with a Nutrition Facts table and ingredient list. Step 7 — Consumer: a buyer takes it home, stores it at room temperature, and toasts a slice. The total carbon footprint of one slice is roughly 60 g CO₂-equivalent.

  • Believing "local" automatically means "low carbon" — transport is a small fraction of total food emissions; production method dominates.
  • Treating organic as automatically more nutritious — well-designed comparisons typically show small nutrient differences.
  • Assuming GMOs are unsafe — Health Canada has approved each Canadian GM crop after individual safety assessment.
  • Forgetting that primary producers, processors, and retailers are regulated by different layers of government.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "describe the path of selected foods from farm to fork in Canada, including the roles of primary producers, processors, distributors, retailers, and the regulatory agencies (Health Canada, CFIA, provincial ministries)."

4.2 — Food Processing: Unprocessed to Ultra-Processed
NOVA classification (groups 1–4), additives, fortification, "natural" claims, ultra-processed foods and health outcomes.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The NOVA classification, developed by Brazilian nutrition researchers (Monteiro et al., 2010), groups foods by the extent and purpose of processing. Group 1 — unprocessed or minimally processed foods: whole grains, fresh meats, vegetables, fruits, eggs, plain milk, nuts. Group 2 — processed culinary ingredients: oils, butter, sugar, salt, flour — used in cooking, not eaten alone. Group 3 — processed foods: simple combinations of group 1 + group 2 ingredients, often for preservation (canned vegetables, bread, cheese, smoked fish). Group 4 — ultra-processed foods (UPF): industrial formulations with substances rarely used in home kitchens (high-fructose corn syrup, hydrogenated oils, modified starches, emulsifiers, flavour enhancers, colorants). Examples: sugar-sweetened soft drinks, packaged snack foods, instant noodles, reconstituted meat products, breakfast cereals heavy in added sugar.

NOVA group 4 (UPF) accounts for roughly 47% of total energy intake in Canadian adults (Statistics Canada, 2015) and over 55% in adolescents. Higher UPF intake is associated with elevated risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and depression (NutriNet-Santé cohort, 2019). UPF tends to be energy-dense (high in saturated fat, sugars, sodium), low in fibre and micronutrients, and engineered for hyperpalatability — encouraging passive over-consumption.

Processing can also be a force for good. Fortification adds nutrients deliberately. Mandatory in Canada: vitamin D in cow's milk and margarine (added since the 1970s to prevent rickets); iodine in salt (since 1949 to prevent goitre); folic acid in white flour (since 1998 to prevent neural-tube defects); riboflavin and thiamin in white flour. Voluntary fortification appears on fortified plant milks (added calcium, B12), breakfast cereals (B vitamins, iron), and orange juice (calcium).

Food additives serve specific roles: preservatives (sorbate, benzoate), antioxidants (ascorbate, BHT), emulsifiers (lecithin), thickeners (xanthan gum), colorants (annatto, beet juice, FD&C dyes), and flavour enhancers (monosodium glutamate). All additives sold in Canada are evaluated by Health Canada for safety and acceptable daily intake (ADI).

  • NOVA Group 1 — unprocessed or minimally processed (whole foods).
  • NOVA Group 2 — culinary ingredients (oils, butter, sugar, salt, flour).
  • NOVA Group 3 — simple processed combinations (canned vegetables, bread, cheese).
  • NOVA Group 4 (UPF) — industrial formulations with non-kitchen ingredients.
  • Fortification — adding nutrients to a food (e.g., vitamin D in milk, folic acid in flour).
  • Food additive — substance added to food for a technological purpose; evaluated by Health Canada.

Worked example — Classifying a grocery basket by NOVA group

Classify each item: bag of brown rice; jar of pasta sauce; bag of frozen pepperoni pizzas; cooking oil; tomatoes; salt; a pack of toaster pastries; plain yogurt; sliced bread; soft drink. Which groups dominate?

Brown rice — Group 1. Pasta sauce — Group 3 (tomatoes + oil + spices, simple). Frozen pepperoni pizzas — Group 4 (UPF). Cooking oil — Group 2. Tomatoes — Group 1. Salt — Group 2. Toaster pastries — Group 4 (UPF). Plain yogurt — Group 1 (or Group 3 if flavoured). Sliced bread — Group 3 (or Group 4 if containing dough conditioners and added emulsifiers, increasingly common). Soft drink — Group 4. Score: three Group 4 items (frozen pizza, toaster pastries, soft drink) likely contribute ~50% of the basket's energy. A more whole-food-oriented basket would minimise Group 4.

  • Equating "processed" with "unhealthy" — frozen vegetables and canned beans are processed but nutritious.
  • Believing "natural flavours" on a label means natural ingredients — the term refers to flavour derivation, not overall product wholesomeness.
  • Avoiding all food additives — many additives have strong safety records and serve essential functions (preservation, vitamin retention).
  • Treating fortified UPF (e.g., sugary cereal with added vitamins) as nutritionally equivalent to whole foods — micronutrient fortification does not offset the harms of excess sugar and refined carbohydrate.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "describe and evaluate the effects of food processing (NOVA groups 1–4, fortification, additives) on nutritional quality, palatability, shelf life, and population health outcomes."

4.3 — Cooking Methods and Nutrient Retention
Dry vs moist heat, Maillard reaction, water-soluble vitamin losses, the effect of cooking on antinutrients and digestibility.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Cooking transforms food by applying heat, which softens textures, deactivates dangerous pathogens, and triggers chemical reactions that develop flavour. Methods are grouped by how heat is delivered. Dry-heat methods use air or fat without water: baking (oven, 150–200 °C), roasting (oven, 180–230 °C), broiling/grilling (radiant heat from above or below), sautéing (small amount of fat, high heat), pan-frying, and deep-frying (immersion in 175–190 °C oil). Dry-heat methods favour the Maillard reaction — the browning of amino acids and reducing sugars above ~140 °C — which generates hundreds of flavour compounds and the savoury "browned" taste. Caramelisation is the parallel browning of sugars alone at ~160 °C.

Moist-heat methods use water or steam: boiling (100 °C), simmering (~85–95 °C), poaching (~70–85 °C), steaming (vapour at 100 °C), and braising/stewing (combination of dry sear then liquid simmer). Moist-heat methods do not brown food and preserve water-soluble nutrients better when the cooking liquid is consumed (e.g., a soup or stew) but leach them out when the liquid is discarded.

Cooking has measurable effects on nutrients. Water-soluble vitamins — especially vitamin C and the B vitamins — are sensitive to heat, leaching, and oxidation. Steaming broccoli for 5 minutes preserves ≈85% of vitamin C; boiling for 10 minutes preserves only ≈45%. Microwaving is comparable to steaming, sometimes better. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and most minerals are heat-stable. Antinutrients — compounds in plants that inhibit nutrient absorption — are often deactivated by cooking: phytate in legumes is reduced by soaking and pressure cooking; trypsin inhibitors in soybeans are inactivated by boiling. Cooked starchy foods (potato, rice) become more digestible because heat gelatinises starch granules, making them easier for amylase to break down.

Some cooking practices increase harmful compounds. Heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons form when meat is charred (grilling, BBQ) above 250 °C, and are classified as probable human carcinogens by IARC. Acrylamide forms when starchy foods are fried or roasted to a dark brown colour (potatoes, bread, coffee). To minimise risk: cook meat at moderate temperature, avoid charring, marinate (which reduces HCA formation), and aim for "golden, not dark brown" when frying or roasting starchy foods.

  • Dry-heat methods — bake, roast, broil, grill, sauté, fry; favour Maillard browning.
  • Moist-heat methods — boil, simmer, poach, steam, braise.
  • Maillard reaction — non-enzymatic browning above ~140 °C; develops flavour.
  • Antinutrient — plant compound (phytate, oxalate) that reduces nutrient absorption; often reduced by cooking or soaking.
  • HCA / PAH / acrylamide — harmful compounds formed during high-temperature cooking; minimise by avoiding charring.
  • Starch gelatinisation — heat + water swells starch granules, increasing digestibility.

Worked example — Designing a low-loss vegetable preparation

You are preparing 200 g of broccoli to retain as much vitamin C as possible. Compare three cooking methods (boiling 10 min, steaming 5 min, microwaving 3 min) and recommend one. Original vitamin C content ≈ 180 mg.

Boiling 10 min: ≈45% retention → 81 mg vitamin C, with 55% lost mostly into the discarded boiling water. Steaming 5 min: ≈85% retention → 153 mg vitamin C. Microwaving 3 min in a small amount of water: ≈80–90% retention → ~155 mg. Recommendation: steaming or microwaving conserves nearly double the vitamin C of boiling. If boiling is necessary (e.g., for a soup), keep and consume the cooking liquid to capture leached nutrients.

  • Believing all raw food is more nutritious than cooked — many nutrients are more bioavailable after cooking (e.g., lycopene in tomatoes, beta-carotene in carrots).
  • Discarding cooking water from vegetables — the water often contains the leached water-soluble vitamins.
  • Charring meat thoroughly — increases HCAs and PAHs.
  • Microwaving food with metal containers or sealed containers — both are safety hazards.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "describe dry-heat and moist-heat cooking methods and explain their effects on flavour development (Maillard reaction), texture, and nutrient retention; evaluate strategies to minimise the formation of harmful cooking by-products."

4.4 — Foodborne Illness and the Pathogens that Cause It
Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, Listeria, Campylobacter, norovirus, Clostridium botulinum — sources, symptoms, vulnerable groups.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Foodborne illness ("food poisoning") affects roughly 1 in 8 Canadians per year — about 4 million cases annually — with ~11 000 hospitalisations and ~240 deaths. The Public Health Agency of Canada tracks the most common pathogens.

Salmonella (especially S. Enteritidis): in eggs (yolk and shell surface), raw or undercooked poultry, raw sprouts. Symptoms: diarrhoea, fever, abdominal cramps, 6–72 hours after exposure, lasting 4–7 days. Vulnerable: infants, older adults, immunocompromised. Prevention: cook eggs and poultry to 74 °C internal; wash hands after handling raw poultry.

Escherichia coli O157:H7: produces Shiga-like toxin causing bloody diarrhoea and (in 5–10% of children) haemolytic-uraemic syndrome with kidney failure. Reservoir: cattle intestines (asymptomatic) → contaminated ground beef, raw leafy greens fertilised with manure, unpasteurised milk and juice. Walkerton, Ontario, May 2000: contaminated municipal water killed 7 people and sickened 2 300. Cook ground beef to 71 °C; never serve "pink" hamburgers.

Listeria monocytogenes: in soft cheeses (Brie, Camembert, Queso fresco) made with unpasteurised milk, deli meats, smoked seafood, raw sprouts. Grows even at refrigerator temperatures (4 °C). Listeriosis is unusually severe in pregnant women (miscarriage, stillbirth) and immunocompromised. Maple Leaf 2008 outbreak: 23 deaths. Pregnant women should avoid pre-cooked deli meats unless reheated to steaming.

Campylobacter jejuni: most common cause of bacterial diarrhoea in Canada; in raw poultry, unpasteurised milk, untreated water. Up to 1 in 1 000 cases develop Guillain–Barré syndrome (autoimmune paralysis).

Norovirus: leading cause of acute gastroenteritis ("stomach flu"); spreads via contaminated food, water, surfaces, or person-to-person. Extremely contagious — <100 virus particles can infect. Common in cruise ships and long-term care facilities.

Clostridium botulinum: anaerobic, spore-forming; produces a paralytic neurotoxin. Found in improperly home-canned low-acid foods (green beans, asparagus) and in honey (avoid honey for infants <12 months). Botulinum toxin is one of the most potent toxins known. Properly heat-treated commercial canning destroys spores.

  • Foodborne illness — 1 in 8 Canadians per year (PHAC estimate).
  • Salmonella — eggs, poultry; symptoms 6–72 h.
  • E. coli O157:H7 — ground beef, leafy greens; can cause HUS.
  • Listeria — deli meats, soft cheeses; grows at refrigerator temperature; dangerous in pregnancy.
  • Campylobacter — poultry; most common bacterial diarrhoea.
  • Norovirus — extremely contagious viral gastroenteritis.
  • Botulinum — improper home canning, honey for infants <12 months.

Worked example — Identifying the likely culprit

Four hours after a backyard BBQ, two guests develop violent vomiting and watery diarrhoea. By 24 hours, six of the twelve guests are similarly affected; the others are fine. The menu was hamburgers (well done), potato salad with mayonnaise, raw spinach salad, chocolate cake, and lemonade. The potato salad sat outside in 30 °C heat for 3 hours before serving. Which pathogen is most likely?

The short onset (4 hours) and dramatic vomiting point to a pre-formed bacterial toxin rather than to a live infection; Staphylococcus aureus is the classic suspect. Staph grows in protein-rich, mayonnaise-based foods (potato salad) left in the temperature danger zone (4–60 °C). The toxin it produces is heat-stable, so even reheating would not have helped — but the salad was eaten cold. The hamburgers (cooked well done) and the chocolate cake (low-water-activity dessert) are less suspicious. Public-health follow-up: confirm with stool culture; prevention is to keep mayonnaise-based salads at <4 °C and never to leave them out longer than 2 hours (1 hour if it is above 32 °C).

  • Believing "if it doesn't smell bad, it's safe" — Salmonella, Listeria, and many pathogens grow without producing off-odours.
  • Thawing frozen meat on the kitchen counter — surface temperature climbs into the danger zone quickly. Thaw in fridge, in cold water (changed every 30 min), or in the microwave.
  • Re-using a marinade — discard any marinade that has been in contact with raw meat.
  • Feeding honey to an infant under 1 year — risk of infant botulism.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "identify common foodborne pathogens (Salmonella, E. coli, Listeria, Campylobacter, norovirus, Clostridium botulinum), the foods they typically contaminate, the symptoms they cause, and the populations most at risk."

4.5 — The Four Cs of Food Safety: Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill
Handwashing, the temperature danger zone (4–60 °C), safe internal temperatures, cross-contamination, the two-hour rule.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canadian public-health messaging summarises home-kitchen food safety as the Four Cs: Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill. Each maps to a major route of contamination.

Clean: hands, surfaces, utensils, fruits, and vegetables. Wash hands with soap and warm water for at least 20 seconds before handling food, after handling raw meat or poultry, after using the washroom, and after touching pets or garbage. Sanitise countertops and cutting boards with a fresh solution of 1 teaspoon (5 mL) household bleach in 750 mL water. Rinse all produce thoroughly under running water, even items that will be peeled.

Separate: avoid cross-contamination. Use separate cutting boards for raw meat/poultry/fish and ready-to-eat foods (vegetables, fruit, bread). Store raw meat on the bottom shelf of the fridge in a sealed container, below ready-to-eat foods, so juices cannot drip onto them. Never reuse a marinade that has touched raw meat without first boiling it.

Cook: to safe internal temperatures, measured with a thermometer inserted into the thickest part of the food. Safe internal temperatures: 71 °C for ground beef, pork, and lamb; 74 °C for chicken and turkey, ground poultry, leftovers and casseroles; 63 °C (with 3-minute rest) for whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, veal; 70 °C for fish; 74 °C for stuffing. Microwaved food should be heated until steaming throughout, with a one-minute rest to allow heat to distribute.

Chill: refrigerate or freeze perishable foods within 2 hours of cooking or purchase — within 1 hour if the ambient temperature is above 32 °C. Refrigerator at ≤ 4 °C; freezer at ≤ −18 °C. The temperature danger zone is 4–60 °C, the temperature range in which pathogenic bacteria multiply most rapidly. Thaw frozen meat in the fridge, in cold water (change every 30 minutes), or in the microwave — never on the counter. Leftovers should be discarded after 3–4 days in the fridge.

  • Clean — wash hands 20 s; sanitise surfaces; rinse produce.
  • Separate — different cutting boards; raw meat on bottom shelf of fridge.
  • Cook — measure with a thermometer; ground poultry/leftovers 74 °C; ground beef 71 °C; whole cuts beef/pork 63 °C + 3 min rest.
  • Chill — refrigerate within 2 h (1 h if >32 °C); fridge ≤ 4 °C; freezer ≤ −18 °C.
  • Temperature danger zone — 4–60 °C; bacteria multiply rapidly.
  • Two-hour rule — perishables must not sit at room temperature longer than 2 hours.

Worked example — Auditing a holiday turkey dinner

A family roasts a 6 kg turkey for Sunday dinner. Apply the Four Cs to identify potential food-safety failures and best practices.

Clean: thaw the turkey in the fridge over 3 days (not on the counter — the surface would warm into the danger zone long before the centre thawed). Wash hands and sanitise the sink after handling the raw bird; do not rinse the turkey in the sink — Health Canada explicitly warns this only splatters Campylobacter across nearby surfaces. Separate: dedicate one cutting board to the raw turkey; carve cooked turkey on a different one. Cook: insert thermometer into thickest part of thigh (not touching bone); pull from oven at 74 °C, cover, rest 15 minutes (carry-over cooking adds another 2–4 °C). Stuffing cooked separately reaches 74 °C more reliably than stuffing cooked inside the bird. Chill: carve leftover turkey into shallow containers within 2 hours of serving; refrigerate at ≤ 4 °C. Use within 3–4 days or freeze for up to 4 months.

  • Rinsing raw poultry in the sink — Health Canada explicitly advises against this because it spreads Campylobacter via splatter.
  • Judging doneness by colour alone — colour is unreliable; use a thermometer.
  • Leaving the cooked turkey on the counter while everyone watches a hockey game — perishables in the 4–60 °C danger zone for >2 hours must be discarded.
  • Cooling a large pot of soup in the fridge while still hot — divide into shallow containers first; large pots cool too slowly to clear the danger zone.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "apply the Four Cs of food safety (Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill); identify the temperature danger zone (4–60 °C) and safe internal cooking temperatures for common foods."

4.6 — Food Preservation: Canning, Freezing, Drying, Fermentation, Irradiation
Each method's mechanism, effect on nutrients, suitable foods, and risks (botulism in improperly canned food).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Food preservation extends shelf life by stopping or slowing the activity of microorganisms and enzymes that spoil food. Each method targets different requirements that microbes need: warmth, water, nutrients, and (often) oxygen.

Canning hermetically seals food in jars or cans and heats it to inactivate microbes and their spores. Pressure canning reaches ≈ 116 °C and is required for low-acid foods (vegetables, meats) to destroy Clostridium botulinum spores. Water-bath canning at 100 °C is sufficient only for high-acid foods (pH < 4.6, e.g., jams, tomato sauce with added acid, pickles). Improper canning of low-acid foods is the leading cause of foodborne botulism in Canada; Health Canada explicitly discourages home canning of low-acid foods without pressure equipment.

Freezing below −18 °C (commercial: −30 °C blast freezing) halts microbial growth and slows enzymatic deterioration. Bacteria are not killed by freezing — only paused — so they resume growth once thawed. Quick freezing produces small ice crystals and minimal cell-wall damage; slow freezing produces large crystals that rupture cell walls and worsen texture upon thawing. Frozen vegetables are typically blanched (briefly boiled) first to inactivate enzymes that would otherwise cause flavour loss during storage. Nutrient retention in frozen vegetables is generally excellent — often higher than for "fresh" vegetables that have travelled for days.

Drying removes water to a level (aw < 0.6) at which microbes cannot grow. Methods include sun drying (raisins, apricots), air drying (jerky, herbs), oven dehydrating, and freeze-drying (which preserves structure and flavour). Drying concentrates sugars and salts but typically destroys vitamin C and reduces B vitamins.

Fermentation uses beneficial microbes to outcompete spoilage organisms and to produce preserving acids or alcohol. Lactic-acid fermentation (lactobacilli) creates yogurt, cheese, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, and sourdough; ethanol fermentation (yeast) creates wine and beer. Fermented foods often have improved digestibility (lactose reduced in yogurt; phytate reduced in sourdough) and may contribute probiotic microbes.

Irradiation exposes food to ionising radiation (gamma rays from Co-60, X-rays, or electron beams) to destroy microbes and insects. Health Canada permits irradiation of potatoes, onions, wheat flour, spices, and (since 2017) fresh and frozen ground beef. Irradiated foods must carry the international "Radura" symbol and the words "Treated with radiation". Irradiation does not make foods radioactive; nutrient losses are similar to canning.

  • Pressure canning — 116 °C; required for low-acid foods to destroy botulism spores.
  • Water-bath canning — 100 °C; only for high-acid foods (pH < 4.6).
  • Freezing — halts growth but does not kill microbes; ideal at −18 °C.
  • Drying — reduces water activity to aw < 0.6.
  • Fermentation — beneficial microbes produce acids or alcohol; aids preservation and digestibility.
  • Irradiation — gamma/X-ray/e-beam; "Radura" symbol required in Canada.

Worked example — Comparing fresh, frozen, and canned green beans

A family has three options for green beans in February in Ontario: "fresh" imported from Mexico (shipped over 5 days), frozen (blast-frozen at harvest), and canned. Compare nutrient retention, cost per gram, and food-safety considerations.

"Fresh" imported: respiration losses during 5 days of transport reduce vitamin C by 30–50%; cost typically high; food safety good but environmental footprint highest. Frozen: blanched and frozen within hours of harvest; vitamin C retention often ≥ 80%, comparable to fresh-picked; cost moderate; very safe. Canned: ≈ 60% vitamin C retention because of the cooking heat plus leaching into the canning liquid, but minerals and protein largely intact; cost lowest; safe if processed correctly. Verdict: frozen often wins on nutrition and cost; canned is acceptable and shelf-stable; "fresh" imported is often the worst nutritionally in winter because of long transport.

  • Believing freezing "kills" bacteria — freezing only halts growth.
  • Water-bath canning low-acid vegetables — risk of botulism unless pressure-canned.
  • Equating irradiated food with "radioactive" food — irradiation does not leave any residual radioactivity.
  • Treating fermented foods as universally probiotic — heat-treated fermented foods (pasteurised sauerkraut, baked sourdough) typically lack live microbes by the time consumed.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.3 — "describe the principles, advantages, and limitations of common food-preservation methods (canning, freezing, drying, fermentation, irradiation) and the effect of each on nutrient retention and safety."
