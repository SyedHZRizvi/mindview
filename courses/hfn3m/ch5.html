5.1 — Food Security and Food Insecurity in Canada
Definitions, prevalence, drivers, food banks, school nutrition programs, federal/provincial responses, Indigenous and Northern food insecurity.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Food security exists when "all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life" (FAO, 1996). It has four pillars: availability (sufficient quantity of food); access (physical and economic means to obtain it); utilisation (safe water and storage to use food well); and stability (continuity over time). Food insecurity is the absence of any of these — typically operationalised as worry about running out of food, compromising on food quality, or missing meals because of lack of money.

In Canada, food insecurity is measured by the Household Food Security Survey Module (HFSSM) included in Statistics Canada's Canadian Community Health Survey. As of 2022 (most recent published data), ~ 18.4% of Canadian households experienced some level of food insecurity in the previous 12 months — about one in six. Rates are dramatically higher in single-parent households (≈ 40%), Indigenous off-reserve households (≈ 33%), and Black-identifying households (≈ 36%). In the territories — especially Nunavut — the rate exceeds 50% in some communities; remote-Northern food costs are 2–3 times Southern levels due to fly-in supply chains.

The drivers of food insecurity are economic, not agricultural. Canada produces a food surplus, but low income (social assistance below cost of a Nutritious Food Basket), high housing costs, the rising cost of living, and inadequate wage floors leave many households unable to afford an adequate diet. Charitable responses — Food Banks Canada coordinates over 4 800 food banks and community programs, serving ~ 1.9 million visits per month (2024). Public-policy responses include the federal Canada Child Benefit (which has demonstrably reduced child food insecurity), provincial social-assistance programs, and the federal Nutrition North Canada subsidy in remote Northern communities. School nutrition programs: Ontario's Student Nutrition Program reaches about 1 in 4 school children; in April 2024 the federal government launched a National School Food Program with $1 B over 5 years.

  • Food security — availability + access + utilisation + stability (FAO 1996).
  • Food insecurity — worry about running out of food, compromising quality, or skipping meals.
  • Canadian prevalence — ≈ 18.4% of households (Statistics Canada, 2022).
  • HFSSM — Household Food Security Survey Module; tool for measuring food insecurity.
  • Food banks — Food Banks Canada coordinates 4 800+ programs; ~1.9 million visits per month (2024).
  • Nutrition North Canada — federal subsidy reducing food costs in remote Northern communities.
  • National School Food Program — federal initiative announced April 2024.

Worked example — Estimating a Nutritious Food Basket gap

A single mother on Ontario Works receives roughly $733/month for housing and basic needs combined, of which ≈ $470 goes to housing. The 2024 Toronto Nutritious Food Basket (Toronto Public Health, monthly cost) for one adult woman + one teenage girl is approximately $560/month. Calculate the food-budget shortfall.

Available for food after housing: $733 − $470 = $263/month. Cost of an adequate diet for the family: $560/month. Shortfall: $560 − $263 = $297/month, more than half of the food budget required. The family will therefore experience food insecurity unless they reduce other essentials (transportation, hygiene, school supplies, utilities) or rely on food banks and school food programs. This calculation is widely used by anti-poverty advocates to argue that current social-assistance rates are below the cost of healthy living.

  • Treating food banks as a "solution" — they reduce hunger in a crisis but do not address the underlying income inadequacy.
  • Believing food insecurity is rare in Canada — almost one in six households experienced it in 2022.
  • Forgetting that Indigenous, Northern, single-parent, and racialised households experience disproportionately high rates.
  • Confusing food security with food sovereignty — sovereignty also covers the right of peoples to define their own food systems.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "analyse the prevalence and causes of food insecurity in Canada and globally; describe the role of food banks, school-nutrition programs, and federal/provincial policy responses."

5.2 — Sustainable Food Systems and the Planetary-Health Diet
Greenhouse-gas emissions from food, water and land use, the 2019 EAT–Lancet planetary-health diet, food waste, sustainable seafood.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The global food system is responsible for roughly 26% of all greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions (Poore & Nemecek, Science, 2018), 70% of freshwater withdrawals, and 38% of ice-free land use. Animal-source foods generate disproportionately more emissions than plant foods: per gram of protein, beef emits about 30 times more CO₂-equivalent than legumes, and dairy emits about 5 times more. Cattle are particularly impactful because of enteric methane from rumen fermentation — methane has 28–80 times the warming potency of CO₂ over varying timeframes.

The 2019 EAT–Lancet "planetary-health diet" proposed dietary targets compatible with both human health and planetary boundaries: roughly 2 500 kcal/day, dominated by vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds, with modest amounts of fish, poultry, eggs, and dairy, and very small amounts of red meat (≤ 14 g/day) and added sugar. The Commission concluded that global adoption would prevent ~11 million premature deaths per year while halving the food system's GHG emissions.

Food waste represents a major leverage point. Approximately 30–40% of food in Canada is wasted along the supply chain (Value Chain Management International, 2019). Household waste typically comprises overripe fruit, leftovers thrown out, and uneaten meals — much of it preventable by better meal planning, freezing, and using "best before" dates as quality (not safety) indicators. Wasted food represents wasted water, land, energy, and packaging, and decomposing food in landfills produces additional methane.

Sustainable seafood: roughly 90% of wild fish stocks are fully exploited, overexploited, or depleted (FAO). Canada's Ocean Wise program (Vancouver Aquarium) rates seafood as "recommended" or "not recommended" based on stock status, bycatch, and habitat impact. Eco-certifications include the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC, wild) and the Aquaculture Stewardship Council (ASC, farmed). Indigenous food sovereignty and small-boat fisheries are increasingly recognised as part of sustainable food systems.

  • Food system GHG share — ~26% of global emissions (Poore & Nemecek 2018).
  • Beef vs legume protein — beef emits ~30× more CO₂-eq per gram protein.
  • EAT–Lancet planetary-health diet — ~2 500 kcal; mostly plants; very limited red meat.
  • Food waste in Canada — 30–40% of food (VCMI 2019).
  • Best before date — quality indicator, not a safety expiry; many foods are fine after this date if stored properly.
  • Ocean Wise / MSC / ASC — sustainable-seafood certifications.

Worked example — Comparing the carbon footprint of two dinners

Dinner A: 200 g beef sirloin steak, ½ cup white rice, ½ cup steamed broccoli. Dinner B: 1 cup lentil curry with brown rice, mixed vegetables. Estimate the CO₂-equivalent emissions for each (per Poore & Nemecek averages: 60 kg CO₂-eq/kg beef; 2.7 kg/kg cooked rice; 0.5 kg/kg broccoli; 0.9 kg/kg cooked lentils; 1.6 kg/kg brown rice; 0.5 kg/kg vegetables).

Dinner A: \(0.2 \times 60 = 12\) (beef) + \(0.125 \times 2.7 = 0.34\) (rice) + \(0.08 \times 0.5 = 0.04\) (broccoli) = ≈ 12.4 kg CO₂-eq. Dinner B: \(0.25 \times 0.9 = 0.23\) (lentils) + \(0.15 \times 1.6 = 0.24\) (brown rice) + \(0.15 \times 0.5 = 0.075\) (vegetables) = ≈ 0.55 kg CO₂-eq. Dinner A produces roughly 22 times more emissions than Dinner B for a comparable nutritional payload — illustrating why replacing red meat with legumes is the single most impactful dietary change for climate footprint.

  • Treating "local" as automatically lowest-carbon — for most foods, production method dominates over transport distance.
  • Believing all aquaculture is unsustainable — well-managed mussel and seaweed farms can be net-positive, while open-net salmon farms can be problematic.
  • Confusing "best before" with "expiry" — best-before is a quality date; foods are usually safe well past it if stored properly.
  • Forgetting that food waste in the home is one of the largest leverage points an individual can act on.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "evaluate the environmental sustainability of food systems (greenhouse-gas emissions, water and land use, food waste, sustainable seafood) and apply the principles of the EAT–Lancet planetary-health diet to personal food choices."

5.3 — Dietary Patterns and Food Trends
Mediterranean, vegan, vegetarian, paleo, keto, intermittent fasting, "clean eating", functional foods — what evidence supports them?
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Dietary patterns have stronger evidence than individual nutrients because food eaten in combinations behaves differently than isolated nutrients. Patterns with the strongest evidence include the Mediterranean diet (PREDIMED trial 2013: ~30% reduction in major CVD events with olive oil, fish, vegetables, nuts, legumes, moderate wine); the DASH diet (lowers systolic BP 6–11 mm Hg); and well-planned vegetarian/vegan diets (Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016: appropriate for all life stages, associated with reduced risk of obesity, T2D, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease).

Some popular patterns have weaker or mixed evidence. The paleolithic ("paleo") diet avoids grains, legumes, and dairy, claiming evolutionary alignment; short-term trials show metabolic improvements similar to other low-processed-food patterns, but the historical premise is debated and long-term cardiovascular evidence is limited. The ketogenic ("keto") diet restricts carbohydrate to < 50 g/day, forcing the body to use ketones (β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate) for fuel; it is well-established for refractory paediatric epilepsy and shows short-term weight-loss efficacy, but long-term cardiovascular outcomes are uncertain and high-fat versions may raise LDL cholesterol. Intermittent fasting (16:8, 5:2, alternate-day) shows modest weight-loss and metabolic benefits in trials, with the principal effect being reduced energy intake rather than fasting per se. "Clean eating" is not a defined scientific term and can verge on disordered eating when it morphs into orthorexia (obsessive food purity).

Functional foods are foods or food components claimed to provide health benefits beyond basic nutrition: probiotics, prebiotics, omega-3-enriched eggs, plant sterols (which lower LDL ~ 10%), and oat β-glucan. Health Canada permits specific health claims when supported by peer-reviewed evidence — for example, plant sterols at ≥ 2 g/day, or β-glucan from oats at ≥ 3 g/day, may carry "lowers cholesterol" claims. Many other "superfood" marketing claims (e.g., goji berries cure cancer) are unregulated and unsupported.

  • Mediterranean diet — strongest evidence for CVD prevention; PREDIMED 2013.
  • DASH — lowers BP 6–11 mm Hg.
  • Vegan / vegetarian — appropriate for all life stages when well planned.
  • Paleo / keto — short-term metabolic effects; long-term evidence uncertain.
  • Intermittent fasting — modest benefits; main mechanism is reduced intake.
  • Orthorexia — obsessive pursuit of "pure" eating; not formally classified but can be disabling.
  • Functional food — additional health benefit beyond basic nutrition; e.g., plant sterols, β-glucan.

Worked example — Evaluating a popular trend

A celebrity recommends an "alkaline diet" claiming that eating alkaline foods (cucumber, leafy greens) "raises blood pH and prevents cancer". Using A1.4 inquiry skills (evaluating evidence), critique this claim.

(1) Mechanism check: blood pH is tightly regulated at 7.35–7.45 by buffers (bicarbonate), respiration (CO₂ exchange), and the kidneys. Food cannot meaningfully shift blood pH; the kidneys excrete excess acid or base. The notion that food "alkalises blood" is physiologically untrue. (2) Outcome check: cancer prevention requires randomised trials or large prospective cohorts. No high-quality trials support the alkaline diet for cancer. (3) Source check: the recommendation comes from a celebrity, not from peer-reviewed literature, and the proponent often sells branded supplements (a conflict of interest). (4) Plausible kernel: the diet's emphasis on vegetables and fruit may improve health for general reasons, but not via "alkalisation". Conclusion: the underlying mechanism is wrong, the outcome claim is unsupported, and the source has a conflict. Recommend the patient eat plenty of vegetables for evidence-based reasons (fibre, micronutrients, plant compounds), not for pH effects.

  • Treating "before-and-after" testimonials as evidence — they are anecdotes, not controlled trials.
  • Confusing weight-loss efficacy in the short term with long-term health benefits — many diets produce short-term loss; few produce durable health changes.
  • Believing detox diets remove "toxins" — the liver and kidneys remove toxins continuously; no diet "cleanses" them.
  • Following dietary advice that promises a "single secret" — the human body has no single switch that overrides everything else.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "evaluate current dietary patterns and food trends (Mediterranean, DASH, vegan/vegetarian, paleo, keto, intermittent fasting, clean eating, functional foods) for nutritional adequacy and quality of supporting evidence."

5.4 — Indigenous Foodways and Food Sovereignty
Pre-contact diets, the impact of colonisation, residential-school food, modern Indigenous food sovereignty movements, traditional foods.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Indigenous peoples have lived in what is now Canada for thousands of years, with rich and diverse food cultures. Pre-contact diets were locally appropriate and nutritionally robust: Inuit diets centred on seal, caribou, char, whale, and seasonal berries — high in omega-3 fats, vitamin A from organ meats, and vitamin C from raw meat and skin. First Nations diets in the eastern woodlands included corn, beans, and squash (the "Three Sisters"), fish, deer, wild rice, maple sap, and berries; West Coast diets centred on salmon, shellfish, oolichan, root vegetables, and seaweeds. Métis diets blended Indigenous and European foods. These diets demonstrated impressive nutritional sufficiency without external supplements, with rates of obesity, diabetes, and heart disease essentially zero before European contact.

Colonisation profoundly disrupted Indigenous food systems. The residential-school system (1831–1996) fed Indigenous children deliberately substandard diets that were the subject of unethical nutrition experiments (Mosby, 2013, documented experiments at six residential schools 1942–52 that withheld nutrients to study deficiency). The destruction of buffalo herds, expansion of agriculture onto traditional lands, and policy displacement to reserves disrupted hunting, fishing, and gathering. Forced settlement and government rations replaced traditional foods with refined flour, lard, sugar, and canned goods — driving the "nutrition transition" and contributing to today's elevated rates of diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease in many Indigenous communities (~3–5× higher than the Canadian average for type 2 diabetes).

Food sovereignty — the right of peoples to define their own food systems, including production, distribution, and cultural practices — is a central goal of contemporary Indigenous food movements. The Indigenous Food Sovereignty (IFS) framework (Settee & Shukla, 2020) emphasises four pillars: sacred (food has spiritual significance); self-determination (community control of food choices); participatory (active engagement in food production); and policy reform (re-aligning settler policies with Indigenous food rights). Initiatives include reviving traditional harvesting, community freezers and traditional-food programs, the National Indian Brotherhood of Canada's nutrition advocacy, and Country Food sharing networks in Inuit Nunangat.

  • Three Sisters — corn, beans, squash; traditional companion planting in eastern woodlands.
  • Country food — locally harvested traditional food (seal, caribou, fish, berries) of Inuit and First Nations.
  • Residential schools — 1831–1996; Mosby (2013) documented unethical nutrition experiments.
  • Nutrition transition — shift from traditional to industrialised diets; drove diabetes, obesity epidemics.
  • Indigenous food sovereignty — the right to define one's own food system.
  • Four pillars (Settee & Shukla 2020) — sacred / self-determination / participatory / policy reform.

Worked example — Comparing traditional Inuit diet with a typical Southern Canadian diet

Compare a pre-contact Inuit hunter-fisher daily intake with a modern processed-food intake in a Northern community, focusing on omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, vitamin C, fibre, and sodium.

Traditional: 300 g of seal meat, 150 g of arctic char, 100 g of caribou liver, a small handful of berries. Omega-3: very high from seal and char (~3 g EPA/DHA). Vitamin D: high (~30 µg) from oily fish and seal. Vitamin C: ~50 mg from raw whale skin (muktuk) and berries — surprisingly adequate. Fibre: low (most calories from meat). Sodium: low (~1 g/day, mostly endogenous). Modern Northern: instant noodles, white bread with margarine, sugary drinks, canned meat. Omega-3: very low. Vitamin D: low (no oily fish; UV-B inadequate). Vitamin C: low. Fibre: low. Sodium: high (~ 4 g/day from processed foods). Health consequence: shift to modern diet increases diabetes, cardiovascular, and obesity risk substantially in Inuit communities; revival of country-food consumption is a public-health priority for Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami.

  • Treating Indigenous diets as a single monolith — diets varied widely by region, season, and culture.
  • Romanticising "traditional" diets without acknowledging the modern barriers (cost, climate change, contaminants in country food).
  • Forgetting that Indigenous food insecurity is a political, not nutritional, problem at root.
  • Treating "country food" as automatically healthier — it is, on average, but environmental contaminants (mercury in fish, persistent organic pollutants in marine mammals) require monitoring.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.3 — "identify the role of Indigenous foodways and food sovereignty in Canada, including the impact of colonisation on Indigenous food systems and contemporary efforts to restore traditional food access."

5.5 — Food Advertising, Body Image, and Eating Disorders
Marketing to children, social media's impact on body image, anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, ARFID — diagnostic criteria and treatment.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Food advertising in Canada is regulated by the Canadian Code of Advertising Standards and, for children, by the Canadian Children's Food and Beverage Advertising Initiative (industry self-regulation). Children see thousands of food ads per year, ~80% of which promote foods high in sugar, salt, or saturated fat. Strong evidence links exposure to ads to increased preference, requests, and consumption of advertised foods. Bill S-228 (Child Health Protection Act), passed by the Senate in 2017, would have restricted advertising of unhealthy foods to children under 13; it died in the Commons but the policy debate continues. Quebec has had a strong restriction since 1980 (Loi sur la protection du consommateur), which is associated with lower fast-food consumption among children.

Body image — how a person perceives, thinks about, and feels about their body — is shaped by family, peers, social media, and advertising. Adolescents are particularly susceptible. Meta-analyses of social-media use show small but consistent associations with body dissatisfaction, especially with image-heavy platforms (Instagram, TikTok). Influencer marketing of unrealistic body ideals and "what I eat in a day" videos can promote disordered eating. The body-positivity movement and body-neutrality counter-movement seek to reduce harm.

Eating disorders are serious mental-health conditions with significant morbidity and mortality. The DSM-5 lists: Anorexia nervosa — restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight + intense fear of gaining weight + body-image disturbance; mortality ~5–10%, the highest of any psychiatric disorder. Bulimia nervosa — recurrent binge eating followed by compensatory behaviours (vomiting, laxative misuse, fasting, excessive exercise); often normal weight; medical complications include hypokalaemia and dental erosion. Binge-eating disorder (BED) — recurrent episodes of binge eating without compensatory behaviour; the most common eating disorder. Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) — restriction of food types based on sensory characteristics, fear of consequences, or lack of interest — without body-image disturbance.

Treatment involves multidisciplinary care: family-based therapy (the Maudsley method) is first-line for adolescent anorexia. Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT-E) is well-supported for bulimia and BED. Medical stabilisation, nutritional rehabilitation, and management of comorbid conditions (depression, anxiety, OCD) are essential. Canadian resources include the National Eating Disorder Information Centre (NEDIC) and provincial eating-disorder programs (e.g., Sheena's Place in Toronto).

  • Bill S-228 — proposed restriction on unhealthy-food advertising to children <13 (passed Senate 2017, lapsed in Commons).
  • Quebec restriction — banned ads to children <13 since 1980; associated with lower fast-food consumption.
  • Body dissatisfaction — strongly linked to social-media exposure in adolescents.
  • Anorexia nervosa — low body weight + fear of weight gain + body-image disturbance; mortality 5–10%.
  • Bulimia nervosa — binge + compensatory behaviours.
  • Binge-eating disorder — binge eating without compensation; most common ED.
  • ARFID — restriction without body-image disturbance.
  • Treatment — multidisciplinary; family-based therapy first-line for adolescent anorexia.

Worked example — Recognising warning signs in a peer

A 16-year-old runner has lost 8 kg over four months, refuses meals with her family, says she "only eats clean", exercises 90 minutes twice a day, and stops menstruating. She also denies that she is too thin and is anxious whenever anyone discusses food. Identify warning signs of anorexia nervosa and suggest steps friends and family can take.

Warning signs map cleanly to DSM-5: (1) significantly low weight despite weight loss; (2) restriction of intake (refuses meals); (3) intense fear of weight gain implied by excessive exercise and dietary control; (4) body-image disturbance (denies being too thin); (5) amenorrhoea (formerly a DSM criterion, still a clinical marker). Compulsive exercise and "clean eating" rigidity suggest orthorexia and exercise dependence. Steps: (a) approach privately with concern, not accusation; (b) avoid commenting on appearance; (c) connect with NEDIC (1-866-633-4220) and the family physician for assessment; (d) understand that the illness is not a choice and that early treatment dramatically improves outcomes; (e) if she shows medical instability (fainting, bradycardia, chest pain), seek urgent care.

  • Telling someone they "look great" after they have lost dangerous weight — reinforces the illness.
  • Assuming eating disorders only affect girls — boys and young men are also affected, especially in sports with weight restrictions.
  • Treating "clean eating" as harmless — it can mask or progress to orthorexia and other restrictive eating disorders.
  • Believing eating disorders are about willpower or vanity — they are serious psychiatric illnesses with biological, social, and psychological causes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "identify and describe eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, ARFID) using DSM-5 criteria; analyse the influence of food advertising and social media on body image; identify Canadian resources for prevention and treatment."
