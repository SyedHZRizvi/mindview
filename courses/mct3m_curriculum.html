This course enables students to extend their mathematical knowledge and problem-solving skills as preparation for college technology programs. Students will solve problems involving exponential expressions and trigonometric functions; represent exponential functions; investigate the graphs and equations of polynomial functions; solve problems involving trigonometry of right and oblique triangles; solve problems involving radian measure; solve measurement problems involving optimisation; solve problems involving the geometry of two-dimensional and three-dimensional figures; and reason mathematically as they solve multi-step problems and communicate their thinking. MCT3M is designed primarily for students intending to enrol in technology-related programs at college (e.g., automotive technology, building construction, civil engineering technology, electrical engineering technology, mechanical engineering technology, networking, and biotechnology).

3. Strands & Expectations

Strand A — Exponential Functions

Overall Expectations: solve problems involving exponential equations graphically, including problems arising from real-world applications; solve problems involving exponential equations algebraically using common bases and logarithms, including problems arising from real-world applications.

Key Specific Expectations

  • A1.1 Determine, through investigation with technology, and describe the impact of changing the base and changing the sign of the exponent on the graph of an exponential function.
  • A1.2 Solve simple exponential equations numerically and graphically, with technology (e.g., using table-of-values, graph intersections); recognise that an equation like \( 2^x = 16 \) has \( x = 4 \) as a solution.
  • A1.3 Determine, through investigation using graphing technology, the point of intersection of the graphs of two exponential functions (e.g., \( y = 4^{-x} \) and \( y = 8^{x+3} \)) and recognise the \(x\)-coordinate as the solution to the corresponding equation.
  • A1.4 Pose problems based on real-world applications (e.g., compound interest, population growth) that can be modelled with exponential equations, and solve these and other such problems by using a given graph or a graph generated with technology from a table-of-values or its equation.
  • A2.1 Simplify algebraic expressions containing integer and rational exponents using the laws of exponents \( a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \), \( a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n} \), \( (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \), \( a^{-n} = 1/a^n \), \( a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} \).
  • A2.2 Solve exponential equations in one variable by determining a common base (e.g., solve \( 4^{x+1} = 32 \) by writing both sides as a power of 2).
  • A2.3 Solve simple exponential equations using logarithms in base 10 with a calculator (e.g., \( 1.05^x = 2 \) ⇒ \( x = \log 2 / \log 1.05 \approx 14.2 \)).

Strand B — Polynomial Functions

Overall Expectations: make connections between the numeric, graphical, and algebraic representations of quadratic functions; solve problems involving quadratic equations in a variety of ways; demonstrate an understanding of the relationship between the graphical and algebraic representations of polynomial functions of degree no higher than three.

Key Specific Expectations

  • B1.1 Pose and solve problems involving quadratic equations arising from real-world applications, by interpreting a given graph or a graph generated with technology from its equation (e.g., projectile motion, profit, area maximisation).
  • B1.2 Sketch the graph of a quadratic in any algebraic form (standard \(ax^2+bx+c\), factored \(a(x-r)(x-s)\), vertex \(a(x-h)^2+k\)) and identify key features (vertex, zeros, axis of symmetry, max/min, direction of opening).
  • B1.3 Express a quadratic in vertex form \( y = a(x-h)^2 + k \) by completing the square.
  • B2.1 Solve quadratic equations by factoring, by graphing, by completing the square, and by using the quadratic formula \( x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \).
  • B2.2 Solve problems arising from real-world applications by determining the roots of a quadratic equation; interpret the discriminant \( D = b^2 - 4ac \).
  • B3.1 Recognise, through investigation, that the graph of a polynomial function of degree three is a curve with no sharp corners; identify the role of the leading coefficient on end behaviour.
  • B3.2 Compare, through investigation, the graphs of polynomial functions of degree 1, 2, and 3 (e.g., \( y = x \), \( y = x^2 \), \( y = x^3 \)); identify the maximum number of zeros, turning points, and the symmetry of each.
  • B3.3 Factor a polynomial of degree three using common factoring, grouping, the sum and difference of cubes formulas (\(a^3 \pm b^3 = (a \pm b)(a^2 \mp ab + b^2)\)), or the factor theorem.

Strand C — Trigonometric Functions

Overall Expectations: determine the values of the trigonometric ratios for angles less than 360°; solve problems involving oblique triangles using the sine law and the cosine law, including problems arising from real-world applications; demonstrate an understanding that periodic phenomena can be modelled using trigonometric functions, and make connections between the graphs of these functions and real-world applications.

Key Specific Expectations

  • C1.1 Determine the exact values of the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) for the special angles 30°, 45°, 60° from special right triangles.
  • C1.2 Determine, through investigation, the values of the primary trigonometric ratios for angles from 0° to 360°, using the unit circle and the CAST rule.
  • C1.3 Solve right-triangle problems involving angles of elevation and depression by computing primary trigonometric ratios (e.g., heights of buildings, lengths of guy wires).
  • C2.1 Solve oblique triangle problems requiring the sine law \( \frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c} \), including the ambiguous case (SSA), and the cosine law \( c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C \), arising from surveying, navigation, and structural engineering applications.
  • C3.1 Define the radian as the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius; convert between degree and radian measure using \( \pi \) rad = 180°.
  • C3.2 Sketch graphs of \( y = \sin x \) and \( y = \cos x \) over at least one period (using both degree and radian scales); determine amplitude, period, midline, intercepts, max/min.
  • C3.3 Determine, through investigation, the roles of \(a\), \(k\), \(d\), \(c\) in functions of the form \( y = a \sin(k(x - d)) + c \) and \( y = a \cos(k(x - d)) + c \).
  • C3.4 Pose and solve real-world problems modelled by sinusoidal functions (e.g., AC current and voltage, tides, sound waves, pendulum motion, vibration in machine parts).

Strand D — Measurement & Geometry

Overall Expectations: solve problems involving measurement and the SI system, including the accuracy of measurements; solve problems involving the geometry of three-dimensional figures; solve problems involving the optimisation of measurements for two-dimensional and three-dimensional figures.

Key Specific Expectations

  • D1.1 Identify the seven SI base units and the most common derived units used in technology; convert between SI prefixes (e.g., milli-, centi-, kilo-, mega-) and between SI and imperial systems.
  • D1.2 Solve problems involving measurement uncertainty by working with significant figures, accuracy, and precision; estimate error propagation in derived quantities like area and volume.
  • D2.1 Determine, through investigation, the relationships among the lengths, areas, and volumes of similar 2-D and 3-D figures (e.g., if linear dimensions scale by \(k\), area scales by \(k^2\) and volume by \(k^3\)).
  • D2.2 Solve problems requiring the calculation of surface area and volume of right prisms, right pyramids, cylinders, cones, and spheres in 2-D and 3-D contexts.
  • D2.3 Solve problems requiring the use of the Pythagorean theorem and trigonometry in three-dimensional figures (e.g., body diagonal of a rectangular prism, slant heights of pyramids).
  • D3.1 Pose and solve problems involving the optimisation of a two-dimensional figure with a given perimeter (largest area) or area (smallest perimeter) using strategies including completing the square.
  • D3.2 Pose and solve problems involving the optimisation of three-dimensional figures (e.g., smallest surface area for a fixed volume of a cylindrical can or rectangular box).
  • D3.3 Solve real-world problems requiring the modelling of two- and three-dimensional design situations (e.g., packaging, sheet metal layout, architectural drawing).

4. Mathematical Processes

The seven mathematical processes are integrated into every strand of MCT3M. Students engage in:

Problem SolvingDevelop, select, apply, compare, and adapt strategies to investigate technological and real-world situations.
Reasoning & ProvingDevelop and apply reasoning skills to make and investigate conjectures (e.g., predicting how scaling affects volume) and to construct/defend arguments.
ReflectingDemonstrate that they are reflecting on and monitoring their thinking to clarify understanding (e.g., checking the reasonableness of a sheet-metal layout).
Selecting Tools & StrategiesUse a variety of tools (Desmos, GeoGebra, scientific calculator, dynamic geometry software, CAD where appropriate) and computational strategies.
ConnectingMake connections among mathematical concepts and to college-technology contexts (e.g., trig ratios → forces in trusses; exponentials → radioactive shielding; volumes → fluid containers).
RepresentingCreate a variety of representations (numeric, geometric, algebraic, graphical, scaled drawings) and translate between them.
CommunicatingCommunicate mathematical thinking orally, visually, and in writing using appropriate vocabulary, notation, and conventions of technology.

5. Achievement Chart

Per Growing Success (2010), each of the four categories carries equal weight (25%) within both term work and the final evaluation.

Knowledge & Understanding
25%

Subject-specific content acquired (knowledge) and the comprehension of its meaning and significance (understanding). Includes exponent laws, exact trig values, formulas for surface area and volume, and SI conversion rules.

Thinking & Inquiry
25%

Use of critical and creative thinking — planning, processing, making connections, justifying conclusions in optimisation problems, sinusoidal modelling, oblique-triangle work, and multi-step technology problems.

Communication
25%

Conveying meaning through various forms — clarity of expression, use of mathematical conventions, vocabulary of technology, scaled diagrams, labelled graphs, properly-formatted units.

Application
25%

Use of knowledge in technological contexts: surveying, navigation, electrical circuits, packaging, sheet metal layout, structural design.

Levels of achievement: Level 4 (80–100%, exceeds standard) · Level 3 (70–79%, provincial standard) · Level 2 (60–69%, approaching) · Level 1 (50–59%, limited) · R (below 50%, insufficient).
