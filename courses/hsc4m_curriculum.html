HSC4M — World Cultures, Grade 12 University/College Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 to 12: Social Sciences and Humanities (2013) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: HSC4M  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: Any university, university/college, or college preparation course in social sciences and humanities, English, or Canadian and world studies (Grade 11 or 12)

1. Course Description

This course examines the nature of culture; the diversity, interconnectedness, and interdependence of world cultures; and the impact of various factors on the development of culture. Students will explore the contributions of various cultures to global society, analyse and assess the influence of cultural identity on individuals and societies, and analyse a variety of issues relating to culture from the perspectives of anthropology, sociology, and psychology. They will also develop research skills used in the social sciences and humanities, applying them in independent inquiry. The course is intended to prepare students for university and college programs in anthropology, sociology, psychology, global studies, journalism, education, and the helping professions.

3. Fundamental Concepts

ConceptApplication in HSC4M
CultureThe shared, learned, transmitted, symbolic system of meanings (beliefs, practices, language, art, artifacts) of a group — a living, contested process rather than a fixed inventory.
DiversityThe range of cultural expression across time, place, and identity (gender, ethnicity, class, sexuality, ability, age), and the value of pluralism within and between societies.
InterdependenceHow cultures borrow, hybridise, and depend on one another; the entanglement of local and global, traditional and modern, dominant and minority.
Cultural IdentityThe dynamic, intersectional sense of self formed through socialization, language, place, kinship, and participation in cultural practices.
Social-Science InquiryA disciplined, ethical, evidence-based way of asking questions about human behaviour and social patterns — drawing on anthropological, sociological, and psychological theories and methods.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Social-Science Inquiry & Skills (A): Disciplined inquiry — drawing on anthropology, sociology, and psychology — produces evidence-based understanding of cultural patterns and prepares students for university research.
  • Foundations of Culture (B): Culture is learned, shared, symbolic, and transmitted; it is composed of material and non-material elements, and exists in dynamic relation with environment, economy, and history.
  • Cultural Expressions (C): Art, language, religion, ritual, music, and dance carry, reproduce, and contest a society's deepest values and worldview.
  • Identity & Globalization (D): Cultural identity is intersectional and continually negotiated under conditions of migration, media saturation, and global economic integration.
  • Cultural Change (E): Cultures evolve through diffusion, innovation, revitalization, resistance, and conflict; understanding change requires attention to power and to the agency of the colonised, the diasporic, and the Indigenous.

5. Strand A — Social-Science Inquiry & Skills (embedded across all units)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. use the social sciences inquiry process and the concepts of social sciences thinking when investigating issues related to world cultures;
  • A2. apply in everyday contexts skills and work habits developed through the investigation of issues related to world cultures;
  • A3. demonstrate an understanding of the connections between investigations of culture and a variety of careers in social sciences and humanities.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 explore a variety of topics related to world cultures to identify topics for inquiry;
  • A1.2 formulate effective questions to guide their research on world cultures;
  • A1.3 locate and select information relevant to their investigations from a variety of primary and secondary sources (e.g., ethnographies, peer-reviewed journals, news media, oral histories, archival photographs);
  • A1.4 assess various aspects of information gathered from primary and secondary sources (e.g., source's bias, accuracy, relevance, currency);
  • A1.5 analyse and interpret research information, applying concepts of social sciences thinking (e.g., culture, identity, diversity, power);
  • A1.6 synthesize findings, formulate conclusions, and make judgements based on the evidence;
  • A1.7 communicate the results of their inquiries using appropriate formats and citation conventions (APA, 7th edition);
  • A2.2 apply the knowledge and skills developed in the study of world cultures when analysing current social, political, and economic issues.
Cross-curricular focus: ethnographic mini-fieldwork; structured interview with community member; literature-review essay; oral presentation of inquiry findings; ISP (Independent Study Project) with APA references.

6. Strand B — Foundations of Culture (Unit 1)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. demonstrate an understanding of culture and the diverse ways in which culture has been defined and analysed;
  • B2. analyse the relationship between culture and the natural and social environments in which it exists;
  • B3. demonstrate an understanding of the connections between cultures and the historical, geographic, economic, and political contexts that shape them.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 explain the meaning of the term culture, with reference to its various dimensions (e.g., material and non-material, cognitive, behavioural, symbolic);
  • B1.2 describe the key characteristics of culture (e.g., learned, shared, symbolic, integrated, dynamic);
  • B1.3 explain key concepts associated with the study of culture (e.g., subculture, counterculture, dominant culture, cultural universals, ethnocentrism, cultural relativism);
  • B1.4 compare and contrast the approaches of anthropology, sociology, and psychology to the study of culture;
  • B2.1 analyse how various aspects of the natural environment (climate, geography, available resources) have influenced cultural practices;
  • B3.2 analyse how political and economic systems shape cultural beliefs and practices in particular societies.
Anchor case studies: Inuit relationship with sea-ice and Arctic environment; Hopi maize cosmology and dryland agriculture; the Yanomami of the Amazon; Bedouin pastoralism and desert culture.

7. Strand C — Cultural Expressions (Unit 2)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. analyse the role of language, art, religion, ritual, music, and material culture in expressing and shaping a society's values and worldview;
  • C2. analyse the role of social institutions (family, education, economy, religion, government) in transmitting culture;
  • C3. demonstrate an understanding of the relationship between cultural expressions and the construction of meaning in everyday life.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 analyse how language carries and shapes worldview (e.g., the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, linguistic relativity, the loss of Indigenous languages);
  • C1.2 analyse the function of religion and spirituality across cultures (e.g., as cosmology, social glue, ethical system, ritual practice, source of contestation);
  • C1.3 explain the role of art (visual, performance, narrative) in expressing cultural values and contesting power;
  • C1.4 analyse how ritual, ceremony, and rites of passage shape cultural identity (e.g., bar/bat mitzvah, quinceañera, Indigenous coming-of-age ceremonies, graduation);
  • C2.1 analyse the role of the family as the primary agent of enculturation across cultures (e.g., extended families, nuclear families, kinship systems);
  • C2.3 analyse the role of education in transmitting cultural knowledge and ideology.
Anchor cases: Anishinaabe seven grandfather teachings; Balinese gamelan music; Spanish/Mexican Day of the Dead; West-African djembe and griot tradition; Indigenous oral storytelling.

8. Strand D — Identity & Globalization (Unit 3 + 4)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. demonstrate an understanding of the relationship between cultural identity and individual identity, including the impact of socialization;
  • D2. analyse the impact of globalization and contact between cultures on individual and collective identity;
  • D3. assess the impact of media, technology, and migration on cultural identity in the contemporary world.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 describe how individuals acquire cultural identity through primary and secondary socialization (family, peers, school, media);
  • D1.2 analyse how gender and sexuality are culturally constructed and expressed (e.g., comparative gender roles, hijra in South Asia, two-spirit identity);
  • D1.3 analyse the role of race and ethnicity in shaping cultural identity and experience of belonging;
  • D1.4 explain the concept of intersectionality and apply it to analyse social position;
  • D2.1 analyse the impact of globalization (economic integration, media flow, migration) on local cultures, evaluating both homogenizing pressures and counter-movements;
  • D2.2 distinguish between cultural appreciation, cultural exchange, and cultural appropriation, applying clear ethical criteria;
  • D3.2 analyse how social media and platform algorithms shape cultural identity formation among adolescents.
STSE focus: diaspora communities in the GTA (Chinatown, Little India, Little Italy, Little Jamaica); the experience of Syrian-Canadian refugees; K-pop and global cultural flow; Indigenous cultural revitalization; debates over headdresses, dreadlocks, yoga as appropriation cases.

9. Strand E — Cultural Change (Unit 4 + 5)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. demonstrate an understanding of the dynamic nature of culture and the forces that drive cultural change;
  • E2. analyse responses to cultural change, including resistance, revitalization, hybridization, and adaptation;
  • E3. analyse the long-term impacts of colonialism, decolonization, and Truth-and-Reconciliation processes on cultural identity in Canada and globally.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 explain the mechanisms of cultural change (e.g., innovation, diffusion, acculturation, assimilation);
  • E1.2 analyse the impact of European colonialism on Indigenous cultures globally;
  • E2.1 analyse cultural revitalization movements (e.g., Māori language nests, Indigenous land-back movements, the Sámi parliament, Hawaiian language schools);
  • E2.2 evaluate the responses of cultures to globalization (e.g., the Slow Food movement, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness, anti-Coca-Cola activism in India);
  • E3.1 analyse Canada's residential school system and its ongoing impact on Indigenous communities;
  • E3.2 analyse the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada's Calls to Action (2015) and assess progress on implementation.
STSE focus: the TRC Calls to Action and TRC.94; UNDRIP (UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples); cultural genocide as a legal concept; Idle No More; reconciliation in education and museums.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, analytical essays, ethnographic mini-projects, seminar contributions, presentations, in-class writing. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of the Independent Study Project (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, inquiry-journal entries, peer-feedback on essay drafts, cultural-reflection logs).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., diagnostic concept check, ISP-proposal feedback, source-evaluation checks).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, essays, ethnographic projects, oral presentations, ISP, final exam).

14. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (video, audio, infographics, primary cultural artefacts), multiple means of engagement (student-selected ISP cultural focus, choice of inquiry method, choice of presentation format), and multiple means of expression (essay, ethnographic field report, documentary, podcast, infographic, art installation) to meet the needs of diverse learners and to honour the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools and the TRC Calls to Action 62 and 63.

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in HSC4M for sustaining the multi-week Independent Study Project, for ethical conduct of fieldwork, and for respectful seminar discussion across cultural difference.
