🔍 1.1 Why Self-Knowledge Comes First
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Before you pick a study technique, before you write a goal, before you choose a postsecondary pathway, you need a working answer to one question: what kind of learner am I right now? The reason is practical. Every study strategy has a context where it works well and a context where it wastes time. A student who knows that their working memory crashes after 30 minutes of dense reading can plan accordingly — switch to retrieval practice, take a real break, change rooms — instead of guilt-spiralling through a fifth re-read of the same paragraph. Self-knowledge is not a feel-good exercise; it is the diagnostic that makes every other choice in this course rational.

The Ministry of Education makes this explicit in Creating Pathways to Success: career and life planning starts with the question "Who am I?" and only then turns to "What are my opportunities?" The same logic applies to learning. Skipping the self-knowledge step and jumping straight to "what's the best way to study for chemistry?" produces strategies that wobble the first time something unexpected happens — an unfamiliar test format, an early-morning exam when you are not a morning person, a group project with people whose communication styles differ from yours.

Self-knowledge for learning is built from multiple kinds of evidence. Inventories (VARK, Kolb, Gardner's Multiple Intelligences, growth-mindset surveys, Self-Determination Theory profiles) give you structured snapshots. Performance data (returned tests, marked essays, teacher feedback) tells you what is actually working. Self-observation (which class do I dread, which feels easy, when in the day am I sharp) gives you the lived texture that no inventory captures. Other people's feedback (peer, parent, teacher) catches blind spots you cannot see yourself. A learner profile that uses only one source — usually a single online quiz — gives you a thin sketch. One that triangulates across all four sources gives you a portrait detailed enough to act on.

A common worry: "Won't a label limit me?" It depends on how you hold it. The point of an inventory is not to file you under a category and seal the drawer. It is to generate a working hypothesis — "I might be a visual-preference learner; let me test that by trying mind maps for two weeks and tracking what helps." The most damaging way to use self-knowledge is to treat one result as a permanent identity. The most useful way is to keep updating the picture as you grow. The student who has revised their learner profile four times by graduation has a much better basis for senior-grade course load decisions than the student who relied on a single Grade 9 quiz.

  • Self-knowledge — accurate understanding of your own learning preferences, strengths, and growth areas.
  • Triangulation — combining multiple sources of evidence to produce a richer picture.
  • Working hypothesis — a tentative claim about yourself that you commit to testing.
  • Learner profile — the summary document where you record self-knowledge for ongoing use.
  • Reflective practice — the habit of pausing to examine your own learning, not just doing more of it.

Worked Example — Building a One-Page Learner Profile

Amir, a Grade 11 student, takes four short inventories in the first two weeks of GLE3O: a VARK quiz (top mode: read/write, with strong visual second), a multiple-intelligences quiz (top three: linguistic, intrapersonal, logical-mathematical), a Dweck mindset survey (mostly growth, weak around math), and a Self-Determination Theory check (autonomy met, relatedness mostly met, competence shaky after his last math test). He cross-references with three pieces of performance data: a recent 68% on a math quiz, a 86% on a history essay, comments from his English teacher about "clear, well-organised written work." He asks his older sister to name one strength she sees — she says "you keep going on things that frustrate you, longer than most people would." Amir writes a one-page profile: "I learn best by reading and writing; I think clearly when I write; my mindset is mostly growth, but math threatens my sense of competence; my motivation needs me to feel I'm getting better at things — I should track my math progress visibly." The whole document is a hypothesis, not a verdict. It will guide his choice of study strategies in Units 2 and 3, his communication strategy in Unit 4, and his goal-setting in Unit 5.

  • Treating a single online quiz as the final answer.
  • Ignoring teacher feedback or returned-test data — externally-observed evidence is critical.
  • Writing a profile so vague ("I learn by doing") that it can't guide a concrete next action.
  • Refusing to revise the profile when contradicting evidence appears.
  • Confusing self-knowledge with self-criticism — the goal is accurate description, not judgment.

👁️ 1.2 Learning-Style Inventories — VARK and Kolb
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Two of the most widely-used learning-style frameworks in Canadian classrooms are VARK (Neil Fleming, 1987) and the Kolb Learning Style Inventory (David Kolb, 1984). Both are useful — and both are widely misunderstood. The first thing to know is that learning-styles research is genuinely contested. Researchers like Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) reviewed the evidence and concluded that "matching" instruction to a learner's claimed style does not measurably improve outcomes. That does not mean the inventories are useless. It means the inventories are best used as a way to notice your own preferences — what feels comfortable, what you reach for first — not as a prescription that says you can only learn one way.

VARK sorts learners by their preferred mode of taking in new information: Visual (diagrams, charts, mind maps), Aural (lectures, podcasts, discussion), Read/write (textbooks, notes, lists), and Kinesthetic (hands-on, examples, demonstrations). Many learners are multimodal — they prefer a mix. Your VARK result tells you something useful: when you encode information in your preferred mode, it tends to feel easier; when you encode it in another mode, you may be doing more work for the same outcome. The practical move is not to demand that all your teachers redesign their lessons. It is to translate material into your preferred mode after class. If you are a visual preference learner in a discussion-heavy English class, draw concept maps from your notes the same evening. If you are a read/write preference learner in a hands-on chemistry lab, write a one-paragraph summary of what you observed.

Kolb's experiential learning cycle is a different lens. Kolb argued that real learning has four phases: concrete experience (you do something), reflective observation (you think about what happened), abstract conceptualisation (you build a model that explains it), and active experimentation (you try the model out in a new situation). Different learners are stronger at different phases. Divergers are strong in experience and reflection; Assimilators are strong in reflection and conceptualisation; Convergers are strong in conceptualisation and experimentation; Accommodators are strong in experience and experimentation. A complete learning cycle uses all four phases. Knowing your strongest two helps you notice which phase you skip — and what you can do about it.

The most important takeaway: learning styles describe preferences, not capacities. You can learn through any mode; it is just that some modes feel easier and some require deliberate effort. The most successful Grade 11 students are not the ones who match every lesson to their style; they are the ones who recognise when a lesson is in a non-preferred mode and have a deliberate translation strategy ready.

  • VARK — Fleming's four-mode framework (Visual, Aural, Read/write, Kinesthetic).
  • Multimodal preference — having more than one strong mode; the most common result.
  • Kolb's learning cycle — four-phase model (experience → reflection → conceptualisation → experimentation).
  • Translation strategy — deliberately recoding material from a lesson's mode into your preferred mode.
  • Learning-styles myth — the unsupported claim that you can only learn in your preferred mode.

Worked Example — Using VARK Without Falling for the Myth

Priya scores Visual=8, Aural=2, Read/write=6, Kinesthetic=4 on VARK — strongly visual with read/write secondary. In Grade 11 Biology, the teacher delivers most content in long lecture-style classes (Aural). The unhelpful response is "I can't learn this because it doesn't match my style." The helpful response is the translation strategy: during class Priya keeps Cornell-style notes (using her read/write strength), and within 24 hours she redraws each topic as a concept map or labelled diagram (using her visual strength). She has not changed the teacher's mode; she has translated the material into the modes that encode best for her. On the next unit test she scores 12% higher than the previous one. Two important pieces of evidence: (1) the gain came from her translation work, not from "matching"; (2) the gain came from spacing the translation across days, which is also a memory-effect (we revisit in Unit 3). VARK helped her start; spaced retrieval finished the job.

  • Believing you can only learn in your preferred mode — this is the central learning-styles myth.
  • Demanding teachers change their style to match yours — it is your job to translate.
  • Picking only your top mode and ignoring secondary modes — most learners are multimodal.
  • Treating Kolb as a personality test rather than a description of which phases of learning you tend to skip.
  • Skipping the translation step — without translating, the inventory gives you nothing actionable.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A — A1.1 "use multiple self-assessment tools (e.g., VARK, Kolb) to develop a balanced learner profile."

🧠 1.3 Multiple Intelligences & Strengths Inventories
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences framework (1983, Frames of Mind) argued that intelligence is not one thing measured by a single IQ test, but at least eight related capacities. Linguistic intelligence is sensitivity to language — writers, speakers, lawyers. Logical-mathematical is the capacity for reasoning, calculation, and pattern detection — scientists, programmers, accountants. Spatial is the capacity to picture and manipulate spatial information — architects, surgeons, navigators. Bodily-kinesthetic is fine-grained control of the body — athletes, dancers, paramedics. Musical is sensitivity to pitch, rhythm, and tone. Interpersonal is the capacity to understand other people. Intrapersonal is the capacity to understand yourself. Naturalistic is the capacity to recognise and categorise features of the natural world. Gardner later proposed a possible ninth — existential — though it remains contested.

Like learning styles, Multiple Intelligences has had a complicated reception in education research. Critics have pointed out that the eight intelligences correlate more strongly than Gardner's theory predicts (which suggests they may share a common factor — what psychologists call g, general intelligence). But teachers and counsellors find the framework useful for a specific reason: it gives students who have been told they "aren't smart" — usually because they did not excel on verbal or mathematical tasks — a vocabulary for the strengths they actually have. A student who is told they have strong bodily-kinesthetic and interpersonal intelligence can see a real future in physiotherapy, paramedicine, coaching, or trades work — fields where those intelligences are precisely what's needed.

The most actionable companion to Multiple Intelligences is a personal strengths inventory. The classic version is the VIA Survey of Character Strengths (Peterson & Seligman, 2004) — 24 strengths grouped under six virtues: wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, and transcendence. Taking the VIA and identifying your top five "signature strengths" gives you another lens on what you bring to a learning task. A student whose top strengths are curiosity, perseverance, kindness, judgement, and humility is going to thrive in different learning environments than a student whose top strengths are bravery, creativity, leadership, humour, and zest. Neither set is better; they are different, and the path to academic success looks different for each.

Two crucial cautions. First: a strength used to excess can become a weakness — the curious student who follows every interesting tangent never finishes the assignment; the brave student who never asks for help drowns in calculus. Strengths are tools, not virtues. Second: a strengths inventory tells you what you have access to, not what you have maxed out. The point is not to retreat to your strengths and avoid your weaker areas. It is to know which strengths to lean on when a task is hard, and which growth areas to invest in deliberately over the next year.

  • Multiple Intelligences (Gardner) — framework of eight intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic).
  • VIA Survey — 24 character-strengths inventory by Peterson & Seligman, free online.
  • Signature strengths — your top five strengths from a validated inventory.
  • Strengths-overuse — when a strength applied without judgement produces a weakness.
  • Strengths-deliberate growth — investing effort in a non-dominant strength because the task requires it.

Worked Example — Pairing Intelligences with Subjects

Jordan's Multiple Intelligences result shows top three: interpersonal, bodily-kinesthetic, and naturalistic. Jordan's transcript shows strong marks in Phys Ed, weaker marks in History. The unhelpful read is "I'm not good at history because I'm not linguistic." The helpful read is: "I have strong interpersonal and bodily-kinesthetic intelligences I haven't yet used in History — I should try strategies that engage them. I'll form a study group (interpersonal), and I'll act out historical events with classmates as we review (kinesthetic), and I'll connect the period we study to environmental and geographical detail (naturalistic)." Jordan does not change Gardner's framework or pretend their linguistic intelligence is stronger than it is. They just use the framework to mobilise their actual strengths in service of a course that doesn't naturally play to them. Six weeks later Jordan's history mark has improved from 72% to 81%.

  • Treating Multiple Intelligences as a fixed identity rather than a current strength profile.
  • Avoiding subjects that don't match your top intelligence — you can develop any of them.
  • Confusing "intelligence" with "interest" — they overlap but are not the same.
  • Ignoring the strengths-overuse problem — every strength has a shadow side.
  • Skipping the actionable step: each top strength should generate one specific study strategy.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A — A1.1 "use multiple self-assessment tools (Multiple Intelligences, RIASEC) to develop a balanced learner profile"; A1.2 "identify personal strengths and areas for growth."

🌱 1.4 Growth Mindset vs Fixed Mindset (Dweck)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck's research over four decades has shown that the most consequential belief a student holds is not about a particular subject — it is a belief about themselves. Dweck distinguishes fixed mindset from growth mindset. Fixed-mindset students believe that intelligence and ability are essentially fixed traits — you have some quantity of "math ability" or "writing ability" and the test simply reveals it. Growth-mindset students believe that ability is developed through effort, strategy, and good feedback — the test reveals where you currently are, not what you can become.

The consequences are large. Fixed-mindset students tend to avoid challenging tasks because failure is taken as evidence about who they are. They give up sooner when they hit difficulty. They feel threatened by other people's success ("if she's good at math, that must mean I'm worse"). They tend to hide mistakes rather than examine them. Growth-mindset students, by contrast, seek out moderate challenge because they see it as the engine of growth. They persist through difficulty because they read the discomfort as the feeling of learning. They feel inspired by other people's success ("she did it; I can figure out how"). They examine mistakes carefully because mistakes are data.

Two cautions are important. First: mindset is not a single trait you have everywhere. Most people are growth-minded in some domains and fixed-minded in others. A student who is growth-minded about basketball ("I'll keep practising") might be deeply fixed-minded about math ("I'm just not a math person"). The work is domain-specific. Second: a growth mindset is not just positive thinking. It is the specific belief that effort + strategy + feedback produces growth. Without the strategy piece, "just try harder" produces frustration. Dweck has repeatedly clarified that growth mindset must be coupled to effective learning strategies (which is the rest of this course).

How do you shift from fixed to growth in a domain where you are stuck? The research suggests three concrete moves. (1) Change the language of self-talk. Replace "I'm bad at this" with "I can't do this yet." The word "yet" makes the belief about a current state, not a fixed identity. (2) Praise the process, not the trait. When you do well, attribute it to specific strategies you used ("I did three rounds of retrieval practice"). When you struggle, attribute it to a specific strategy you can change ("I crammed; next time I'll space it"). (3) Make failure productive. After a poor test, do a structured error analysis: which mistakes were careless, which were conceptual, which were unfamiliar. Each category points to a different study strategy. Mistakes are only wasted if you don't read them.

  • Fixed mindset — the belief that intelligence and ability are essentially fixed traits.
  • Growth mindset — the belief that ability is developed through effort, strategy, and feedback.
  • Domain-specificity — your mindset can be growth in one domain and fixed in another.
  • "Yet" language — small verbal shift that re-frames a fixed claim as a current state.
  • Process praise — feedback that targets strategy and effort rather than innate trait.
  • Productive failure — using mistakes as data for the next learning cycle.

Worked Example — Catching Fixed-Mindset Self-Talk

Ella gets back a math test with 58%. Her first thought is: "I'm just not a math person." She writes that line in her learning journal, then catches it and re-writes: "I scored 58% on this test. I haven't yet learned how to do quadratic word problems reliably." She does an error analysis: of 12 wrong marks, 4 are arithmetic slips she caught when she re-read; 5 are word-problem set-up errors; 3 are conceptual gaps in factoring. Each category points to a different action: arithmetic slips → slow down and re-check, build a habit of estimation; word-problem set-up → 10 retrieval-practice problems over the next two weeks; conceptual gaps → re-watch the factoring lesson and work three problems. Two weeks later Ella scores 74% on the next test. Her fixed-mindset moment didn't disappear — but she caught it, re-framed it, and turned it into actionable strategy. That cycle is the whole point.

  • Treating mindset as a single global trait — it varies by domain.
  • Believing growth mindset is just "positive thinking" — it requires effective strategy.
  • Praising trait ("you're so smart") instead of process ("you tried three approaches").
  • Hiding bad results rather than analysing them — wasted information.
  • Adding "yet" to language but not actually changing study behaviour — the word alone does nothing.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A — A2.2 "distinguish between growth mindset and fixed mindset (Dweck) and explain how each affects effort and outcome."

🔁 1.5 Self-Regulated Learning & Metacognition
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Metacognition — Greek for "thinking about thinking" — is the single most-validated meta-skill in education research. John Hattie's Visible Learning synthesis ranks metacognitive strategies among the highest-effect interventions across all of K-12 education, with effect sizes around 0.6 — meaning students who use them gain about 18 months of academic growth in a single year. Metacognition is not vague self-reflection. It has three concrete phases: planning ("what is this task asking, what do I already know, which strategy fits?"), monitoring ("is my strategy working, am I on track, do I need to adjust?"), and evaluating ("how did that go, what would I keep, what would I change?"). A student who runs all three phases on every major task is — empirically — learning at a faster rate than a peer who skips them.

Self-regulated learning (SRL) is the broader framework that contains metacognition. Barry Zimmerman's classic three-phase model maps neatly onto metacognition's three phases. Forethought is the planning phase: task analysis, goal-setting, strategic-planning, self-motivation. Performance is the monitoring phase: self-control, attention-focusing, help-seeking, self-observation. Self-reflection is the evaluation phase: self-judgement, self-reaction, causal attribution, adaptation. Strong self-regulated learners cycle through all three phases for each task; weaker self-regulated learners often skip directly from forethought to performance, or jump from one performance attempt to another without ever reflecting.

The concrete tools of metacognition are simple. Think-alouds — narrating your thinking aloud while working a problem — surface invisible decisions and let you catch errors of approach. Learning journals — three or four sentences after each study session — track what worked. Error analysis — categorising your mistakes after a returned test — points to specific next-action strategies (see the worked example in 1.4). Pre-mortems — imagining in advance how a study session could go wrong and pre-emptively fixing it — prevent recurring failures. None of these are time-expensive; each takes 3–5 minutes; together they compound into substantially better outcomes.

One common student worry: "If I spend time on metacognition, that's time I'm not spending on the actual content." The research disagrees. Hattie's meta-analyses, Bjork's work on "desirable difficulties," and large-scale randomised trials in Canadian and U.K. classrooms all show that 5–10 minutes of metacognitive work per study hour yields a net gain — because the time is reinvested in strategies that work, and away from strategies that didn't. The break-even is fast. Within a week or two of consistent practice, the metacognitive habits pay for themselves in efficiency. The goal of GLE3O is to make metacognition a habit you don't have to think about — by the end of the course, the planning-monitoring-evaluating cycle should run almost automatically.

  • Metacognition — the practice of thinking deliberately about one's own thinking, in three phases (planning, monitoring, evaluating).
  • Self-regulated learning (Zimmerman) — three-phase model: forethought → performance → self-reflection.
  • Think-aloud — narrating your reasoning aloud to surface invisible decisions.
  • Learning journal — brief written reflection (3–4 sentences) after a study session.
  • Error analysis — categorising mistakes after a returned test to drive next-action strategies.
  • Pre-mortem — imagining in advance how a session could fail, and pre-emptively fixing it.

Worked Example — Running One Full Metacognitive Cycle

Yusuf has a chemistry quiz on stoichiometry on Friday. Planning (Monday, 5 minutes): "What is the task? Mole-ratio problems with limiting reagents. What do I know? I'm good at the balancing step but I confuse limiting vs excess reagent. What strategy? Three retrieval-practice problems Monday, three Wednesday, three Thursday — spaced. I'll do them without notes the first time, then check." Monitoring (Wednesday, 2 minutes mid-session): "Two of three Wednesday problems wrong. The error is the same one — I'm picking the wrong reagent. Strategy adjustment: I'll re-watch the 4-minute video on limiting reagents and write a one-line rule on a card I can see while practising." Evaluating (Friday after the quiz, 3 minutes): "Quiz felt better than the unit test before this. Of 10 marks, I got 8. One mistake was a careless arithmetic error; one was a unit-conversion slip. Strategy adjustment for the unit test: build in a 'check units' habit. Spaced retrieval practice clearly worked — I'll use it again." Total time spent on metacognition over five days: 10 minutes. Total quiz improvement: significant. This is the cycle the rest of the course will train into habit.

  • Skipping the planning phase and diving into work — a metacognitive miss.
  • Doing the work without checking in mid-session ("monitoring") — recurring errors compound.
  • Failing to reflect after a task — every cycle without reflection is a missed feedback loop.
  • Treating reflection as vague feeling-talk ("that was hard") instead of specific strategy-talk.
  • Believing metacognition is "extra work" — the data are clear that it saves more time than it costs.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A — A2.1 "define metacognition and describe its three phases"; A2.3 "describe the cycle of self-regulated learning"; A2.4 "apply metacognitive strategies to their own coursework."

⚡ 1.6 Motivation & Self-Determination Theory
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The most influential motivation framework in current education research is Self-Determination Theory (SDT) — developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan at the University of Rochester over four decades. SDT argues that human motivation is healthiest when three basic psychological needs are met: autonomy (you have meaningful choice and ownership in what you do), competence (you experience yourself as growing in skill), and relatedness (you feel connected to people who matter to you in this work). When all three are met, motivation tends to be intrinsic — you do the work because it is meaningful, interesting, or aligned with who you are. When one or more is missing, motivation becomes extrinsic — you do the work only because of an outside reward (grades, parental approval) or to avoid a punishment.

The practical implication: when you feel unmotivated, ask which of the three needs is unmet. Autonomy is unmet when you feel you have no choice — every assignment dictated, every minute scheduled, no sense of ownership. Competence is unmet when the work feels either too easy (no growth) or too hard (no traction). Relatedness is unmet when you feel isolated — when no one in the room cares whether you learn this and you have no peer with whom to share the struggle. Each diagnosis points to a different remedy. Lack of autonomy → introduce small choices ("which essay topic; which order of study"). Lack of competence → adjust the challenge level or find a sub-skill you can grow visibly. Lack of relatedness → form a study group, or take the question to your teacher.

SDT also distinguishes a spectrum of motivation, not just the binary intrinsic-vs-extrinsic. From least to most autonomous: amotivation (no drive at all); external regulation (you do it for reward/punishment); introjected regulation (you do it because not doing it would make you feel guilty); identified regulation (you do it because you accept its importance, even if you don't enjoy it); integrated regulation (you do it because it aligns with your identity); intrinsic motivation (you do it because it is inherently enjoyable). Most schoolwork sits in the middle — identified or introjected. The realistic goal is not to make every assignment intrinsically fun. It is to keep work above the "external regulation" threshold, where doing it depends on the next imposed reward.

One important distinction: SDT predicts that the kind of motivation matters more than the amount. A student running on pure external regulation may put in many hours of work but will quit the moment the reward is removed, and will retain less because their attention is on the reward, not the content. A student running on identified or intrinsic motivation puts in fewer hours that go further. This is why the language of "work harder" often backfires — it intensifies external regulation. The language of "find the meaning, build the skill, share the work" tends to move motivation along the spectrum toward more autonomous forms.

  • Self-Determination Theory (SDT) — Deci & Ryan's framework of three needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness).
  • Autonomy — meaningful choice and ownership in what you do.
  • Competence — the experience of growing in skill.
  • Relatedness — feeling connected to others who matter to you in this work.
  • Intrinsic motivation — engaging in an activity because it is inherently meaningful.
  • Extrinsic motivation — engaging for an outside reward or to avoid punishment.
  • Mastery vs performance goals — pursuing improvement (mastery) vs pursuing being-judged-favourably (performance).

Worked Example — Diagnosing a Motivation Crash

Sara has stopped doing her English homework. She is a strong reader and used to love the subject. What happened? She asks the three SDT questions. (1) Autonomy: the current unit is a teacher-chosen novel she dislikes; assigned essay topics are narrow. Partly unmet. (2) Competence: she is getting feedback she doesn't understand on her essays ("more analysis") and her marks have dropped from 88% to 76%. Strongly unmet. (3) Relatedness: she sits at the back of class; her closest English-class friend transferred out. Mostly unmet. Diagnosis: all three needs are partially unmet. Action plan: (a) request a brief teacher conversation about the "more analysis" feedback to build competence; (b) form a study pair with one classmate to rebuild relatedness; (c) negotiate with the teacher to add a personal angle to the next essay topic to build autonomy. Two weeks later her English work has resumed. The crash wasn't a moral failing — it was three psychological needs going unmet simultaneously. Naming them gave her a path back.

  • Treating "lack of motivation" as a character flaw rather than as evidence of an unmet need.
  • Trying to "just push through" without diagnosing which need is missing — usually fails.
  • Stacking only extrinsic rewards (grades, parental pressure) and wondering why motivation crashes when the reward is removed.
  • Forgetting that competence and relatedness can be rebuilt by small, deliberate actions (a teacher conversation, a study buddy).
  • Believing motivation must come before action — often the opposite is true: starting the action rebuilds the motivation.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A — A3.1 "distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation"; A3.2 "describe the three psychological needs in Self-Determination Theory"; A3.3 "distinguish mastery-oriented from performance-oriented goal pursuits."
