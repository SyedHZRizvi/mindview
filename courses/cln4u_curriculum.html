CLN4U — Canadian and International Law, Grade 12 University Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Canadian and World Studies (2015, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CLN4U  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: Any university-preparation course in Canadian and world studies, English, or social sciences and humanities

1. Course Description

This course explores a range of contemporary legal issues and how they are addressed in both Canadian and international law. Students will examine the philosophical foundations of law, the historical and political contexts that have shaped Canadian constitutional law, and the legal frameworks of the international community, including the United Nations and its specialised agencies. They will investigate the implications of selected legal issues for various stakeholders and citizens in Canada and the world, and they will apply their understanding of legal philosophy, principles, and procedures to develop reasoned arguments and informed positions on legal questions of significance.

3. Concepts of Legal Thinking

ConceptApplication in CLN4U
Legal SignificanceDetermining the importance and lasting impact of a constitutional ruling, an international treaty, or a doctrinal shift (e.g., R v Oakes, the Rome Statute of the ICC, the responsibility-to-protect doctrine).
Continuity & ChangeExamining what has endured (Westphalian sovereignty, common-law method) and what is shifting (jus cogens norms, transitional justice, climate-change litigation, Indigenous legal recognition).
Cause & ConsequenceTracing why a legal framework was adopted (UDHR after the Holocaust, ICC after Rwanda and the Balkans) and the consequences — intended and unintended — for states, individuals, and Indigenous nations.
Legal PerspectiveRecognising that states, victims, marginalised communities, Indigenous nations, and international tribunals may interpret the same norm differently, and that perspective is a part of legal argument.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Legal Inquiry & Skills: Applying the legal-studies inquiry process — framing legal questions, gathering and weighing evidence, evaluating arguments, communicating conclusions — develops critical thinking and informed citizenship at both domestic and global scales.
  • Strand B — Foundations of Canadian Law & the Constitution: Canada's legal system is shaped by competing philosophical traditions (natural law, positivism, legal realism), inherits from multiple historical sources, and is structured by the Constitution Acts of 1867 and 1982 and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
  • Strand C — International Law: International law governs relations between states through treaties, customary norms, and the general principles of law, and is enforced through institutions whose authority is consensual rather than coercive.
  • Strand D — Human Rights and Citizenship: The international human-rights regime built after 1945 (UDHR, ICCPR, ICESCR, regional systems) and Canadian human-rights mechanisms protect individuals and groups against state power, with both convergence and tension between domestic and international standards.
  • Strand E — Resolving Disputes between Nations: The international community manages conflict through diplomacy, treaty-making, judicial settlement (ICJ, ICC, ad hoc tribunals), peacekeeping, and the law on the use of force (UN Charter, jus ad bellum and jus in bello), with mixed but real success.

5. Strand A — Legal Inquiry & Skills (embedded across all strands)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Legal Inquiry: use the legal-studies inquiry process and the concepts of legal thinking when investigating issues, events, and developments in Canadian and international law;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills developed through investigations in Canadian and international law, and identify careers in which a background in legal studies might be useful.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in Canadian and international law;
  • A1.2 select and organize relevant evidence and information from a variety of primary and secondary sources (statutes, case law, treaties, UN documents, scholarly commentary, news media);
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations (e.g., bias, perspective, expertise, jurisdiction);
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence and information using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for legal inquiry (e.g., IRAC, case-brief format, treaty interpretation under VCLT Articles 31–32);
  • A1.5 use the concepts of legal thinking (legal significance, continuity and change, cause and consequence, legal perspective) when analysing legal issues;
  • A1.6 evaluate and synthesize findings to formulate conclusions and/or make informed judgements or predictions;
  • A1.7 communicate ideas, arguments, and conclusions using formats and styles appropriate for the audience and purpose (factum, memorandum, oral submission);
  • A2.1 describe ways in which legal studies has helped them develop skills, including the essential skills in the Ontario Skills Passport, that they can apply in everyday life and in further academic and professional pursuits.
Skill focus: Case-brief writing (IRAC), statutory and treaty interpretation, mooting, persuasive legal argument, citation conventions (McGill Guide), critically evaluating media reporting of legal cases.

6. Strand B — Foundations of Canadian Law & the Constitution (Chapters 1–2)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Philosophical Foundations: analyse the philosophical foundations of law and the legal concepts that underpin different legal systems;
  • B2. Canadian Constitutional Framework: analyse the structure and key features of the Canadian Constitution and the relationship between the federal and provincial governments;
  • B3. Charter of Rights and Freedoms: analyse the rights and freedoms guaranteed by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and how they have been interpreted by the courts.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 analyse the main philosophical schools of legal thought (natural law, legal positivism, legal realism, critical legal studies, feminist jurisprudence, Indigenous legal traditions);
  • B1.2 analyse the relationship between law and morality, and between law and justice;
  • B1.3 explain the sources of law in Canada (constitution, statute, common law, royal prerogative, Indigenous legal orders);
  • B1.4 analyse the influence of different legal systems and traditions on Canadian law (common law, civil law, religious-law traditions, Indigenous legal traditions);
  • B2.1 describe the structure of the Canadian Constitution and the key features of the Constitution Acts of 1867 and 1982;
  • B2.2 explain the division of powers between federal and provincial governments (Constitution Act, 1867, ss. 91–92);
  • B2.3 analyse the role of the courts in interpreting the Constitution and resolving constitutional disputes (e.g., reference questions, federalism cases);
  • B3.1 analyse the rights and freedoms protected by the Charter (fundamental freedoms, democratic, mobility, legal, equality, language rights);
  • B3.2 explain the application of the section 1 Oakes test to limits on Charter rights;
  • B3.3 analyse the operation of section 33 (notwithstanding clause) and the controversy surrounding its use;
  • B3.4 analyse Aboriginal and treaty rights protected by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
Anchor cases: R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103 (section 1 test); Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 217; R v Big M Drug Mart [1985] 1 SCR 295; R v Sparrow [1990] 1 SCR 1075; Tsilhqot'in Nation v BC [2014] 2 SCR 257.

7. Strand C — International Law (Chapter 3)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Sources and Subjects: analyse the sources, principles, and subjects of international law;
  • C2. Institutions: analyse the structure, functions, and authority of key international legal institutions;
  • C3. Reception in Canada: analyse how international law is received and applied in Canadian law.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 analyse the sources of international law as set out in Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice (treaties, custom, general principles, judicial decisions, scholarly writings);
  • C1.2 explain the concepts of state sovereignty, jurisdiction, and immunity;
  • C1.3 analyse the principles of treaty formation, interpretation, and termination under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969);
  • C1.4 explain the role of jus cogens (peremptory norms) and erga omnes obligations;
  • C2.1 analyse the structure and functions of the United Nations (General Assembly, Security Council, ICJ, Secretariat, ECOSOC, specialised agencies);
  • C2.2 analyse the jurisdiction and procedure of the International Court of Justice and the International Criminal Court;
  • C2.3 analyse the role of regional organisations and specialised legal regimes (WTO, ICAO, IMO);
  • C3.1 explain the monism–dualism distinction and Canada's dualist approach to treaty reception;
  • C3.2 analyse leading Canadian cases on the reception of customary international law and treaty law (e.g., Baker v Canada, R v Hape).
Anchor sources: UN Charter (1945); Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969); ICJ Statute Article 38; Reference re Secession of Quebec (uti possidetis and self-determination); Baker v Canada [1999] 2 SCR 817; R v Hape [2007] 2 SCR 292.

8. Strand D — Human Rights and Citizenship (Chapter 4)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. International Human-Rights Framework: analyse the development and content of the international human-rights regime;
  • D2. Mechanisms and Enforcement: analyse the institutional mechanisms used to monitor and enforce international human rights;
  • D3. Citizenship and Indigenous Rights: analyse the relationship between human rights, citizenship, and the rights of Indigenous peoples.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 analyse the development of the international human-rights regime from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) to contemporary instruments;
  • D1.2 analyse the structure and content of the International Bill of Human Rights (UDHR, ICCPR 1966, ICESCR 1966);
  • D1.3 analyse specialised human-rights treaties (Genocide Convention 1948, CERD 1965, CEDAW 1979, CAT 1984, CRC 1989, CRPD 2006);
  • D1.4 analyse the legal regime governing refugees (1951 Refugee Convention, 1967 Protocol) and the principle of non-refoulement;
  • D2.1 analyse the work of the UN Human Rights Council and treaty monitoring bodies;
  • D2.2 analyse regional human-rights systems (European, Inter-American, African);
  • D2.3 analyse the role of international criminal law and the principles of universal jurisdiction;
  • D3.1 analyse the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007) and its reception in Canada (UNDRIP Act, 2021);
  • D3.2 analyse the doctrine of responsibility to protect (R2P, 2005);
  • D3.3 explain the concept of citizenship and the legal rights and responsibilities it entails in Canadian and international contexts.
Anchor instruments: Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948); International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966); Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (1948); UNDRIP (2007); 2005 World Summit Outcome Document (R2P).

9. Strand E — Resolving Disputes between Nations (Chapter 5)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. Use of Force: analyse the legal framework governing the use of force between states;
  • E2. International Humanitarian Law: analyse the law of armed conflict and individual criminal responsibility;
  • E3. Peaceful Dispute Settlement: analyse the methods used to resolve disputes between nations peacefully.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 analyse the prohibition on the use of force in Article 2(4) of the UN Charter and the exceptions to it;
  • E1.2 analyse the right of self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter, including the requirements of necessity and proportionality;
  • E1.3 analyse the Security Council's collective-security authority under Chapter VII of the UN Charter;
  • E2.1 analyse the historical development and core principles of international humanitarian law (distinction, proportionality, necessity, humanity);
  • E2.2 analyse the four Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols (1977);
  • E2.3 analyse the definitions of war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide under the Rome Statute (1998);
  • E2.4 analyse the role of the International Criminal Court and ad hoc tribunals (ICTY, ICTR);
  • E3.1 analyse the methods of peaceful settlement of disputes under Article 33 of the UN Charter (negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement);
  • E3.2 analyse the jurisdiction and procedure of the International Court of Justice in contentious cases and advisory opinions;
  • E3.3 analyse the role of UN peacekeeping operations and the evolution of peace-enforcement mandates.
STSE focus: Modern crises and the use of force (Iraq 2003, Libya 2011, Ukraine 2022, Israel/Gaza 2023–); accountability for international crimes (ICC arrest warrants); cyber operations and the law on the use of force; climate change and inter-state litigation.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, case briefs, a mid-course constitutional factum, the moot court (international law strand), in-class essay responses, and the inquiry portfolio. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a culminating Independent Legal Inquiry (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, case-brief drafts, peer-review of factums, reflection on moot performance).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., diagnostic IRAC, prereq Charter quiz, factum-outline feedback).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, factums, moot, culminating inquiry, final exam).

14. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (annotated case excerpts, treaty timelines, courtroom-procedure diagrams, video), multiple means of engagement (student-selected inquiry topic, choice of moot side, choice of jurisdiction for comparative work), and multiple means of expression (factum, podcast, oral submission, infographic) to meet the needs of diverse learners and to honour the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools.

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CLN4U for sustaining the culminating inquiry, for the rigorous reading load (statutes, cases, treaties), and for civil disagreement in mooting and seminar discussion.
