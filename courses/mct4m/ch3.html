3.1 — Angles and Radian Measure
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

So far you have measured angles in degrees, where a full revolution is 360°. In MCT4M you also learn radian measure, where a full revolution is \(2\pi\). The radian is the natural angle unit for calculus, physics, and most engineering software, so getting comfortable with both systems and converting between them is essential. The key relationship to memorize is \(180°=\pi\) radians, from which every conversion follows.

The geometric definition of a radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc whose length equals the radius. If a circle has radius \(r\) and the arc subtends an angle of \(\theta\) radians, the arc length is \(s=r\theta\) — a clean formula that is much messier in degrees. This arc-length formula is the workhorse of any application involving rotation (gears, pulleys, sprockets, sectors in technical drawings).

To convert between degrees and radians, multiply by the appropriate ratio. Degrees → radians: multiply by \(\pi/180\). Radians → degrees: multiply by \(180/\pi\). The "exact" radian forms for the special angles \(30°, 45°, 60°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°\) are \(\pi/6, \pi/4, \pi/3, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2, 2\pi\), and you should memorize these as a unit-conversion table.

  • Radian — the angle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius.
  • \(180°=\pi\) rad — the master equivalence; everything else follows.
  • Arc length — \(s = r\theta\) with \(\theta\) in radians.
  • Sector area — \(A = \tfrac{1}{2} r^{2}\theta\) with \(\theta\) in radians.

Worked Example — Converting between units

Convert \(75°\) to radians and \(\tfrac{5\pi}{12}\) rad to degrees.

Step 1. Degrees to radians: \(75 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{75\pi}{180} = \dfrac{5\pi}{12}\) rad.

Step 2. Radians to degrees: \(\dfrac{5\pi}{12} \cdot \dfrac{180}{\pi} = \dfrac{5\cdot 180}{12} = 75°\). ✓

Worked Example — Arc length in a technical application

A gear of radius 8 cm rotates through 135°. How far along its circumference has a tooth on the rim travelled?

Step 1. Convert 135° to radians: \(135 \cdot \pi/180 = 3\pi/4\) rad.

Step 2. Apply the arc-length formula: \(s = r\theta = 8 \cdot (3\pi/4) = 6\pi\) cm.

Step 3. Numerical value: \(6\pi \approx 18.85\) cm.

  • Always check which mode your calculator is in (DEG vs RAD) — wrong-mode errors are extremely common.
  • The arc-length and sector-area formulas only work with \(\theta\) in radians.
  • Negative angles are measured clockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis; positive angles are counterclockwise.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "determine the exact values of the sine, cosine, and tangent of the special angles 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°, and their multiples; convert between degree and radian measures."

3.2 — The Unit Circle and Trigonometric Ratios for Any Angle
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centred at the origin. It is the universal tool for defining the trigonometric ratios of any angle, not just acute angles in a right triangle. For an angle \(\theta\) in standard position (vertex at origin, initial arm on the positive \(x\)-axis), if the terminal arm meets the unit circle at the point \((x,y)\), then \(\cos\theta = x\) and \(\sin\theta = y\). This single picture replaces the SOH-CAH-TOA mnemonic for all values of \(\theta\).

From these definitions, all the other ratios follow. \(\tan\theta = y/x\) (provided \(x\ne 0\)); the reciprocal ratios \(\csc, \sec, \cot\) are reciprocals of \(\sin, \cos, \tan\) respectively. The unit circle also makes the signs of the ratios obvious: in quadrant I all are positive; in quadrant II only sine is positive; in quadrant III only tangent is positive; in quadrant IV only cosine is positive. The CAST mnemonic captures this — All (Q I), Sine (Q II), Tangent (Q III), Cosine (Q IV).

For the special angles \(\theta = 0, \pi/6, \pi/4, \pi/3, \pi/2\) (and their reflections in the other quadrants), the ratios have exact values you must memorize: \(\sin(\pi/6)=1/2\), \(\sin(\pi/4)=\sqrt{2}/2\), \(\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt{3}/2\), and so on. Build a "special angles" chart you can sketch from memory.

  • Unit circle — \(x^{2}+y^{2}=1\); the terminal point's \(x\)-coord is \(\cos\theta\), \(y\)-coord is \(\sin\theta\).
  • Reference angle — the acute angle between the terminal arm and the \(x\)-axis; trig ratios match this acute angle up to sign.
  • CAST rule — quadrant-by-quadrant sign pattern (All/Sine/Tangent/Cosine).

Worked Example — Trig ratio in quadrant III

Find the exact values of \(\sin(210°)\) and \(\cos(210°)\).

Step 1. \(210°\) is in quadrant III; reference angle is \(210°-180°=30°\).

Step 2. In quadrant III, both \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) are negative (CAST: only tangent positive).

Step 3. So \(\sin(210°) = -\sin(30°) = -1/2\) and \(\cos(210°) = -\cos(30°) = -\sqrt{3}/2\).

Worked Example — Finding \(\theta\) from a ratio

If \(\cos\theta = -\sqrt{2}/2\) and \(\theta\in[0,2\pi)\), find all possible values of \(\theta\).

Step 1. The reference angle satisfies \(\cos\alpha=\sqrt{2}/2\), so \(\alpha=\pi/4\).

Step 2. Cosine is negative in quadrants II and III.

Step 3. Quadrant II: \(\theta = \pi - \pi/4 = 3\pi/4\). Quadrant III: \(\theta = \pi + \pi/4 = 5\pi/4\).

  • The unit circle works for all angles, including negative ones and angles larger than \(2\pi\).
  • Always identify the quadrant before assigning signs to the reference-angle ratios.
  • The reciprocal ratios are undefined when their reciprocal partner equals zero — e.g., \(\tan(\pi/2)\) is undefined because \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "determine the values of the trigonometric ratios for angles less than 360° using the unit circle and CAST rule."

3.3 — Sine and Cosine Laws (Oblique Triangles)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The primary trigonometric ratios only work directly in right triangles. For oblique triangles (no 90° angle) you need two general-purpose tools: the sine law and the cosine law. Both relate sides and angles in any triangle, so together they handle every "given some sides and angles, find the rest" problem in technical work — surveying, navigation, force-vector diagrams, robotic-arm geometry.

The sine law states that \(\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}\), where lower-case letters are sides and upper-case letters are the opposite angles. Use the sine law when you know an angle and its opposite side, plus one more piece. The cosine law generalizes the Pythagorean theorem: \(c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos C\). Use the cosine law when you know two sides and the angle between them (SAS) or all three sides (SSS).

One subtlety with the sine law is the ambiguous case: if you are given two sides and a non-included angle (SSA), there might be zero, one, or two valid triangles. Always check by computing the side opposite the given angle and comparing — if it falls between the two given sides, two triangles exist.

  • Sine law — \(\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}\); needs an angle-side pair.
  • Cosine law — \(c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos C\); needs SAS or SSS information.
  • Ambiguous case (SSA) — sine law may yield zero, one, or two triangles; check geometrically.

Worked Example — Sine law

In \(\triangle ABC\), \(A = 40°\), \(B=75°\), and side \(a = 10\). Find side \(b\).

Step 1. Set up: \(\dfrac{10}{\sin 40°}=\dfrac{b}{\sin 75°}\).

Step 2. Solve for \(b\): \(b = \dfrac{10 \sin 75°}{\sin 40°}\).

Step 3. Evaluate: \(b \approx 10(0.9659)/(0.6428) \approx 15.03\).

Worked Example — Cosine law (SAS)

A surveyor measures two sides of a triangular lot at 50 m and 80 m with the included angle 110°. How long is the third side?

Step 1. Apply the cosine law: \(c^{2} = 50^{2}+80^{2}-2(50)(80)\cos 110°\).

Step 2. Compute: \(c^{2} = 2500+6400-8000(-0.3420)\approx 2500+6400+2736 = 11636\).

Step 3. \(c \approx \sqrt{11636} \approx 107.9\) m.

  • Make sure angles and sides "match up" — the sine law pairs each angle with its opposite side.
  • The cosine law's last term has a minus sign — losing it produces wildly wrong answers.
  • For the ambiguous case, sketch the triangle to see whether 0, 1, or 2 solutions exist.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.4 — "solve problems involving oblique triangles in two and three dimensions using the sine law, including the ambiguous case, and the cosine law."

3.4 — Graphs of Sine and Cosine Functions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

When you let the angle \(\theta\) range over all real numbers and plot \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\), you get the iconic sinusoidal wave shapes. The sine and cosine functions are periodic — they repeat after every \(2\pi\) radians (or 360°) — and bounded between \(-1\) and \(1\). These properties make them the universal model for any repeating physical phenomenon: alternating current, sound waves, light waves, tides, daylight, and many mechanical vibrations.

Each function has identifiable features: amplitude 1 (half the difference between max and min); period \(2\pi\) (length of one complete cycle); equation of the axis \(y=0\) (the horizontal midline); zeros at \(\sin: n\pi\) and \(\cos: \pi/2 + n\pi\). The cosine graph is just the sine graph shifted left by \(\pi/2\) — they are the same curve viewed from different starting points.

For the transformed function \(f(x) = a\sin(k(x-d)) + c\), the parameters control the shape: \(|a|\) is the amplitude, \(2\pi/|k|\) is the period, \(d\) is the horizontal (phase) shift, and \(c\) is the equation of the axis. Mastering these conversions is the heart of sinusoidal modelling.

  • Amplitude — \(|a|\); half the difference between the maximum and minimum values.
  • Period — \(2\pi/|k|\); the horizontal length of one complete cycle.
  • Phase shift — \(d\); how far the curve is shifted horizontally from the parent function.
  • Equation of axis — \(y = c\); the horizontal line about which the curve oscillates.

Worked Example — Reading parameters from an equation

For \(f(x) = 3\sin(2(x-\pi/4)) + 5\), find amplitude, period, phase shift, equation of axis.

Step 1. Amplitude: \(|a|=3\).

Step 2. Period: \(2\pi/|k|=2\pi/2=\pi\).

Step 3. Phase shift: \(d=\pi/4\) to the right. Equation of axis: \(y=5\).

Step 4. Range: \([5-3, 5+3]=[2,8]\).

Worked Example — Finding the equation from features

A sinusoidal curve has maximum 10, minimum 2, period \(6\), and a maximum at \(x=1\). Write a sine equation.

Step 1. Amplitude \(a = (10-2)/2 = 4\). Vertical shift \(c = (10+2)/2 = 6\).

Step 2. Period 6 means \(k = 2\pi/6 = \pi/3\).

Step 3. Sine reaches its maximum at \(\pi/2\) from its start; if the maximum is at \(x=1\), then the start is at \(1 - (\pi/2)/k = 1 - (\pi/2)/(\pi/3) = 1 - 3/2 = -1/2\). So \(d = -1/2\).

Step 4. Equation: \(f(x) = 4\sin\!\big(\tfrac{\pi}{3}(x+\tfrac{1}{2})\big) + 6\).

  • Always factor \(k\) out of the bracket before reading the phase shift \(d\).
  • Don't confuse amplitude (vertical) with period (horizontal) — they answer different questions.
  • If the amplitude is given as a positive number, \(a\) might still be negative if the graph is reflected.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "sketch the graphs of \(f(x)=a\sin(k(x-d))+c\) and \(f(x)=a\cos(k(x-d))+c\); identify and explain the role of each parameter."

3.5 — Sinusoidal Modelling and Applications
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Many real-world quantities repeat: the depth of water at a harbour, the angle of a Ferris wheel car, the voltage in an AC circuit, the hours of daylight throughout the year. To build a model, identify five features from the data: maximum, minimum, period, where the maximum (or minimum) occurs, and the starting value. From those, recover the four parameters in \(f(x) = a\sin(k(x-d))+c\) or the cosine equivalent.

The standard recipe: amplitude \(a = (\text{max}-\text{min})/2\); axis \(c = (\text{max}+\text{min})/2\); period \(T\) gives \(k = 2\pi/T\); horizontal shift \(d\) is set so the formula matches the observed timing. Sine starts at the axis going up; cosine starts at the max. Use whichever is more convenient and the phase shift will adjust.

Once the model is in hand, you can answer practical questions: when does the tide first reach a depth of 4 m? When is the Ferris wheel rider at maximum height? These become trigonometric equations that you can solve graphically with a graphing calculator or algebraically using inverse trig functions.

  • Max / min — extreme values of the data; their midpoint is the axis, their half-difference is the amplitude.
  • Time of max / min — used to determine the phase shift \(d\).
  • Frequency — reciprocal of period; for AC circuits and waves, often more natural than period.

Worked Example — Tide model

The depth of water at a wharf cycles between a low of 2 m at midnight and a high of 10 m at 6 a.m., with a 12-hour period. Write a sine model \(D(t)\) where \(t\) is hours after midnight.

Step 1. Amplitude \(a=(10-2)/2=4\). Axis \(c=(10+2)/2=6\). Period 12 \(\Rightarrow k=2\pi/12=\pi/6\).

Step 2. Max occurs at \(t=6\); sine's max happens at \(\pi/2/k\) past the start. Set the start so the max lands at 6: \(d = 6 - (\pi/2)/(\pi/6) = 6 - 3 = 3\).

Step 3. Model: \(D(t) = 4\sin\!\big(\tfrac{\pi}{6}(t-3)\big) + 6\). Verify at \(t=0\): \(4\sin(-\pi/2)+6 = -4+6=2\). ✓ (Low tide at midnight.)

Worked Example — Ferris wheel

A Ferris wheel of diameter 20 m has its centre 12 m above the ground and rotates once every 60 s. A rider boards at the bottom at \(t=0\). Model the rider's height \(h(t)\).

Step 1. Amplitude \(a=10\) (radius). Axis \(c=12\) (centre height). Period 60 \(\Rightarrow k=2\pi/60=\pi/30\).

Step 2. Boards at bottom \(=12-10=2\) m. Use cosine reflected: \(h(t) = -10\cos\!(\tfrac{\pi}{30}t) + 12\).

Step 3. Check at \(t=0\): \(-10(1)+12 = 2\). ✓ At \(t=30\) (half-cycle): \(-10(-1)+12 = 22\) m (the top). ✓

  • Always sketch a rough graph of the data first to spot whether sine or cosine fits more naturally.
  • Negative amplitude is fine — it produces a reflected sinusoid (start at max instead of axis, for cosine).
  • Confirm your model on at least two known data points before reporting it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.4 — "pose and solve problems based on applications involving sinusoidal functions arising from real-world contexts (e.g., periodic motion, tides, electrical signals)."

3.6 — Trigonometric Identities
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A trigonometric identity is an equation involving trig functions that holds for every value of the variable in the common domain. Identities let you rewrite messy expressions in equivalent, simpler forms — an indispensable skill for solving equations and for any engineering work involving phasors or signal processing. The two families to master in MCT4M are the quotient identities and the Pythagorean identities.

The quotient identity \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\) (and its reciprocal \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\)) follows directly from the unit-circle definitions. The Pythagorean identity \(\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1\) follows from the equation of the unit circle \(x^{2}+y^{2}=1\) — substitute \(x=\cos\theta, y=\sin\theta\). Dividing this Pythagorean identity by \(\cos^{2}\theta\) gives \(\tan^{2}\theta + 1 = \sec^{2}\theta\); dividing by \(\sin^{2}\theta\) gives \(1 + \cot^{2}\theta = \csc^{2}\theta\).

To prove an identity, manipulate one side until it matches the other; do not move terms across the equals sign because that would assume what you are trying to prove. The standard tactics: convert everything to sine and cosine; find a common denominator; apply a Pythagorean identity; factor; cancel. Practise often — proofs improve with repetition.

  • Quotient identity — \(\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta\).
  • Pythagorean identity — \(\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1\).
  • Reciprocal identities — \(\csc\theta = 1/\sin\theta\), \(\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta\), \(\cot\theta = 1/\tan\theta\).

Worked Example — Simplifying an expression

Simplify \(\dfrac{\sin^{2}\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\) (assume \(\cos\theta \ne 1\)).

Step 1. Use the Pythagorean identity to rewrite the numerator: \(\sin^{2}\theta = 1 - \cos^{2}\theta\).

Step 2. Factor the numerator: \(1-\cos^{2}\theta = (1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)\).

Step 3. Cancel: \(\dfrac{(1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)}{1-\cos\theta} = 1+\cos\theta\).

Worked Example — Proving an identity

Prove \(\dfrac{1-\cos^{2}\theta}{\sin\theta} = \sin\theta\) (for \(\sin\theta\ne 0\)).

Step 1. Work on the left side. Substitute the Pythagorean identity: \(1-\cos^{2}\theta = \sin^{2}\theta\).

Step 2. So LHS \(= \sin^{2}\theta / \sin\theta = \sin\theta\).

Step 3. LHS = RHS, so the identity holds. ∎

  • When proving identities, never multiply both sides by the same expression — that assumes the equation; instead manipulate one side only.
  • If you get stuck, convert everything to sine and cosine; that often opens up cancellations.
  • Watch for restricted values: an identity is "true wherever both sides are defined".

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.5 — "make connections between numeric, graphical, and algebraic representations of trigonometric functions; verify simple Pythagorean and quotient identities."
