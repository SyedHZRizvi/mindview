4.1 — Scalars, Vectors, and Components
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In Grade 12 College Technology you encounter quantities that need more than a single number to describe them. Forces, velocities, and electric-field strengths all have both magnitude (size) and direction. We call these quantities vectors — to distinguish them from scalars like mass, temperature, or speed, which have magnitude only. The vector concept is fundamental in mechanical, civil, and electrical technology.

A vector in two dimensions is represented as a directed arrow: a line segment with a definite tail (start) and head (end). The length of the arrow is the magnitude, and the angle it makes (commonly measured from the positive \(x\)-axis or from north) is the direction. Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and direction, regardless of where in the plane they are drawn — this position-independence is what makes vectors flexible.

Every two-dimensional vector can be broken into horizontal and vertical components using basic trigonometry: if a vector has magnitude \(|\vec{v}|\) at angle \(\theta\) from the positive \(x\)-axis, then \(v_x = |\vec{v}|\cos\theta\) and \(v_y = |\vec{v}|\sin\theta\). Working in components is by far the easiest way to add and subtract vectors, because you simply add components separately.

  • Scalar — a quantity with magnitude only (mass, temperature, energy).
  • Vector — a quantity with both magnitude and direction (force, velocity, displacement).
  • Components — \(v_x = |\vec{v}|\cos\theta\) and \(v_y = |\vec{v}|\sin\theta\).
  • Magnitude — \(|\vec{v}|=\sqrt{v_x^{2}+v_y^{2}}\); always non-negative.

Worked Example — Components from magnitude and direction

A force of 50 N acts at 30° above the horizontal. Find the horizontal and vertical components.

Step 1. Identify: \(|\vec{F}|=50\) N, \(\theta = 30°\).

Step 2. Horizontal component: \(F_x = 50\cos 30° = 50(\sqrt{3}/2) \approx 43.30\) N.

Step 3. Vertical component: \(F_y = 50\sin 30° = 50(1/2) = 25\) N.

Conclusion: the force has 43.3 N horizontally and 25 N vertically.

Worked Example — Magnitude and direction from components

A velocity vector has components \(v_x = -6\) m/s and \(v_y = 8\) m/s. Find its magnitude and direction (from positive \(x\)-axis, counterclockwise).

Step 1. Magnitude: \(|\vec{v}|=\sqrt{(-6)^{2}+8^{2}} = \sqrt{36+64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\) m/s.

Step 2. Reference angle: \(\tan\alpha = 8/6\), so \(\alpha = \tan^{-1}(8/6) \approx 53.13°\).

Step 3. The vector is in quadrant II (negative \(x\), positive \(y\)), so direction \(=180°-53.13°\approx 126.87°\) from positive \(x\)-axis.

  • Speed is the magnitude of velocity; the vector form keeps direction information, which matters for navigation and engineering.
  • When using \(\tan^{-1}\) to find the angle, your calculator returns values only in \((-90°,90°)\) — adjust using the quadrant.
  • Drawing the vector first and identifying the quadrant prevents direction-sign mistakes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "recognize the difference between scalar and vector quantities, and represent vectors as directed line segments in two dimensions; resolve a vector into its horizontal and vertical components."

4.2 — Adding and Subtracting Vectors
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

To add two vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\), use the triangle method: place the tail of \(\vec{v}\) at the head of \(\vec{u}\); the sum \(\vec{u}+\vec{v}\) is the vector from the tail of \(\vec{u}\) to the head of \(\vec{v}\). Equivalently, the parallelogram method draws \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) from a common tail, completes the parallelogram, and takes the diagonal. Both pictures convey the same algebraic content: \(\vec{u}+\vec{v}\) takes you to the same final position no matter the route.

Algebraically, vector addition is component-wise: \((u_x, u_y) + (v_x, v_y) = (u_x+v_x, u_y+v_y)\). This is enormous practical leverage — you never have to draw a triangle if you can compute components. For subtraction, \(\vec{u}-\vec{v} = \vec{u} + (-\vec{v})\), where \(-\vec{v}\) is the vector pointing in the opposite direction with the same magnitude.

The biggest engineering application is the resultant: when two forces (or velocities) act at the same point, their resultant is their vector sum, and its magnitude and direction determine the net effect. You will use this for net forces, equilibrium analysis, and navigation problems (a plane's ground velocity is the vector sum of its airspeed and the wind).

  • Resultant — the single vector with the same effect as several vectors added together.
  • Triangle method — tail-to-head addition; sum is head-to-tail.
  • Parallelogram method — tails together, diagonal is the sum.
  • Component method — add \(x\)-components and \(y\)-components separately; fastest for calculation.

Worked Example — Resultant of two forces

Two forces act at a point: \(\vec{F}_{1} = 40\) N at \(20°\) above horizontal, \(\vec{F}_{2} = 60\) N at \(110°\) (so 90° above the first). Find the resultant.

Step 1. Components: \(F_{1x} = 40\cos 20° \approx 37.59\); \(F_{1y} = 40\sin 20° \approx 13.68\). \(F_{2x} = 60\cos 110° \approx -20.52\); \(F_{2y} = 60\sin 110° \approx 56.38\).

Step 2. Sum of components: \(R_x \approx 37.59 - 20.52 = 17.07\); \(R_y \approx 13.68 + 56.38 = 70.06\).

Step 3. Magnitude: \(|\vec{R}| = \sqrt{17.07^{2}+70.06^{2}} \approx \sqrt{291.4 + 4908.4} \approx \sqrt{5199.8} \approx 72.11\) N.

Step 4. Direction: \(\tan^{-1}(70.06/17.07)\approx 76.3°\) above horizontal.

Worked Example — Aircraft navigation

An aircraft flies at 220 km/h on a bearing of \(50°\). A wind blows at 40 km/h from the west (toward the east). Find the ground velocity.

Step 1. Aircraft: \(220\) km/h, bearing 50° means \(50°\) east of north \(\Rightarrow\) angle \(40°\) from positive \(x\)-axis (north–east compass). Components: \(v_{ax}=220\sin 50°\approx 168.5\); \(v_{ay}=220\cos 50°\approx 141.4\).

Step 2. Wind toward east: \(v_{wx}=40\); \(v_{wy}=0\).

Step 3. Ground velocity components: \(v_{gx}\approx 208.5\); \(v_{gy}\approx 141.4\). Magnitude \(\approx \sqrt{208.5^{2}+141.4^{2}} \approx 251.9\) km/h. Direction \(\tan^{-1}(208.5/141.4)\approx 55.9°\) east of north.

  • Always work in components when more than two vectors are involved; the sketching methods become messy.
  • Bearing notation (compass) measures from north, clockwise; this differs from the math convention. Be careful which is being used.
  • The resultant of perpendicular vectors uses the Pythagorean theorem; for non-perpendicular vectors, use components.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.4 — "add and subtract vectors using parallelogram and triangle methods; solve problems involving the addition of vectors (e.g., force, velocity)."

4.3 — Properties of Circles
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A handful of classical theorems about circles unlock a huge range of technical problems: belt-and-pulley geometry, gear ratios, building columns, observation arcs in surveying, and architectural arches. The four big ones to keep at your fingertips are the chord-perpendicular bisector, the inscribed-angle theorem, the tangent-radius perpendicularity, and the cyclic quadrilateral theorem.

The chord property says the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord — which gives a quick way to find the centre or radius given two chord measurements. The inscribed-angle theorem says the angle subtended by an arc at any point on the major arc is half the central angle subtending the same arc; this is the key to many proofs and is the basis of Thales' theorem (an inscribed angle in a semicircle is a right angle). The tangent-radius property says the radius drawn to a point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent line — useful any time a line just touches a circle.

Cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles of a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle sum to \(180°\). All four big results follow from the inscribed-angle theorem, so it pays to understand that theorem really well; the rest become easy corollaries.

  • Chord-perpendicular bisector — the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
  • Inscribed-angle theorem — inscribed angle = \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) of the central angle on the same arc.
  • Tangent-radius perpendicularity — the radius to a point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent.
  • Cyclic quadrilateral — opposite angles sum to \(180°\).

Worked Example — Inscribed angle

A central angle in a circle measures 80°. What is the inscribed angle subtending the same arc?

Step 1. By the inscribed-angle theorem, inscribed angle = (1/2)(central angle) = (1/2)(80°) = 40°.

Step 2. This holds for any point on the major arc subtending the same chord.

Worked Example — Radius from chord-perpendicular bisector

A chord of length 16 cm sits 6 cm from the centre of a circle. Find the radius.

Step 1. The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, creating two segments of 8 cm each.

Step 2. Form a right triangle with legs 6 cm (perpendicular) and 8 cm (half-chord); the hypotenuse is the radius.

Step 3. Radius \(= \sqrt{6^{2}+8^{2}} = \sqrt{36+64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\) cm.

  • Sketch the circle and add radii — most circle problems become routine triangle problems once you draw the right line.
  • Remember the angle in a semicircle is always \(90°\) (special case of the inscribed-angle theorem).
  • For the cyclic-quadrilateral test, opposite angles must sum to exactly 180°; otherwise the figure does not lie on a circle.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "recognize the major properties of circles (chord-perpendicular bisector, inscribed angle, tangent-radius, cyclic quadrilateral) and apply them in solving problems."

4.4 — Surface Area and Volume of 3-D Figures
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

College technology students need to compute surface area and volume for tanks, ducts, structural members, machine parts, and architectural features. The required formulas split into two groups: classical solids (prism, cylinder, pyramid, cone, sphere) and composite figures built from those classical pieces. Memorize the small set; build the big set by adding or subtracting.

Three formulas earn special focus in MCT4M because they involve a third dimension and are commonly mis-applied. For a pyramid or cone, \(V = \tfrac{1}{3}(\text{base area})(\text{height})\) — the "one-third" coefficient is what trips students up. For a sphere, \(V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\) and \(SA = 4\pi r^{2}\). For a cone, lateral surface area is \(\pi r \ell\), where \(\ell\) is the slant height (not the perpendicular height \(h\)) — find \(\ell\) using the Pythagorean theorem when needed.

For a composite figure — say, a cylinder topped by a hemisphere (typical fuel tank) — compute each part separately, then add volumes. For surface area, you subtract the "shared" face between the pieces from the total of the individual surface areas; otherwise you double-count an interior surface. Always sketch and label before computing.

  • Pyramid / cone volume — \(V=\tfrac{1}{3}(\text{base})(\text{height})\).
  • Sphere — \(V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\); \(SA=4\pi r^{2}\).
  • Cone slant height — \(\ell = \sqrt{r^{2}+h^{2}}\), used in \(SA_{\text{lateral}}=\pi r\ell\).
  • Composite figure — sum volumes; for surface area, subtract any shared interior face from the total.

Worked Example — Cone volume and surface area

A cone has base radius 5 cm and height 12 cm. Find its volume and total surface area (including the base).

Step 1. Volume: \(V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^{2} h = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi (25)(12) = 100\pi \approx 314.16\) cm³.

Step 2. Slant height: \(\ell = \sqrt{5^{2}+12^{2}} = \sqrt{169} = 13\) cm.

Step 3. Lateral area: \(\pi r \ell = \pi(5)(13) = 65\pi\) cm². Base area: \(\pi r^{2}=25\pi\). Total: \(90\pi \approx 282.74\) cm².

Worked Example — Composite figure (cylinder + hemisphere tank)

A fuel tank consists of a cylinder of radius 1 m and length 3 m capped by a hemisphere of the same radius. Find total volume.

Step 1. Cylinder: \(V_{c}=\pi r^{2}h=\pi(1)^{2}(3)=3\pi\) m³.

Step 2. Hemisphere: \(V_{h}=\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}=\tfrac{2}{3}\pi (1)^{3}=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\) m³.

Step 3. Total: \(3\pi + 2\pi/3 = (11\pi)/3 \approx 11.52\) m³.

  • Confirm units: lengths in cm \(\Rightarrow\) volume in cm³, surface area in cm².
  • Don't forget the \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) factor in cones and pyramids — the most common formula slip.
  • For composite surface areas, subtract any shared interior face from the total; for volumes, simply add.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "determine the surface area and volume of pyramids, cones, spheres, and composite figures using formulas and modelling software."

4.5 — Optimization of Perimeter, Area, and Volume
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An optimization problem asks for the maximum or minimum of a geometric quantity (area, perimeter, surface area, volume) subject to a constraint. Examples: enclose the largest rectangular field with 200 m of fencing; design a soda can that minimizes metal for a fixed volume; build the largest open box from a fixed sheet of cardboard. In college technology this is the bridge between geometry and the cost-minimisation/profit-maximisation work of engineering economy.

The standard recipe has four steps. (1) Draw a labelled diagram. (2) Identify the quantity to optimise (the objective) and the quantity that is fixed (the constraint). (3) Use the constraint to express the objective as a function of a single variable. (4) Find the maximum or minimum using the vertex formula (if quadratic) or by sketching the graph; check endpoints if the domain is restricted.

Two classical results worth memorising for technical work: for a fixed perimeter, the rectangle of maximum area is a square; for a fixed surface area, the cylinder of maximum volume has height equal to its diameter. These shortcuts let you sanity-check answers in seconds.

  • Objective — the quantity to maximise or minimise (e.g., area).
  • Constraint — the fixed quantity (e.g., perimeter).
  • Single-variable model — the objective rewritten in terms of one variable; usually quadratic in this course.
  • Vertex formula — for \(y=ax^{2}+bx+c\), the extremum occurs at \(x=-b/(2a)\).

Worked Example — Maximum area with fixed perimeter

A farmer has 200 m of fencing and wants to enclose a rectangular field with the largest possible area. Find the dimensions.

Step 1. Let length \(=x\) and width \(=y\). Constraint: \(2x+2y=200 \Rightarrow y=100-x\).

Step 2. Objective: \(A = xy = x(100-x) = 100x - x^{2}\).

Step 3. Vertex of \(A(x) = -x^{2}+100x\): \(x=-100/(2(-1))=50\). So \(y=50\) too — a square.

Step 4. Maximum area: \(A(50)=50\cdot 50 = 2500\) m².

Worked Example — Open-top box

An open-top box is to be made from a 20 cm × 30 cm sheet of cardboard by cutting equal squares of side \(x\) from each corner and folding up the sides. Find \(x\) that maximises the volume.

Step 1. Box dimensions: length \(30-2x\), width \(20-2x\), height \(x\). Domain: \(0

Step 2. Volume: \(V(x) = x(30-2x)(20-2x) = x(600 - 100x + 4x^{2}) = 4x^{3} - 100x^{2} + 600x\).

Step 3. Without calculus, evaluate \(V\) on a grid: \(V(2)=4(8)-100(4)+1200=32-400+1200=832\); \(V(4)=4(64)-100(16)+2400=256-1600+2400=1056\); \(V(5)=500-2500+3000=1000\); \(V(3)=4(27)-100(9)+1800=108-900+1800=1008\). Refine: \(V(4)\) is largest near \(x \approx 4\) cm; numerical/graphical methods confirm \(x \approx 3.92\) cm gives maximum volume \(\approx 1056\) cm³.

  • Always state and justify the domain — physical setups exclude negative or zero dimensions.
  • Quadratic optimisation has an exact vertex; cubic or higher needs graphical or numerical work in this course.
  • Sanity-check: max area under fixed perimeter ⇒ square; min surface area under fixed cylinder volume ⇒ height = diameter.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "solve problems involving the optimization of perimeter, area, surface area, and volume in real-world contexts."

4.6 — Applications of Geometry in Technology
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

This section consolidates the vector, circle, and 3-D ideas from the chapter by working through three classes of technical applications. The first involves gears and pulleys: a gear's angular speed and a belt's linear speed are related by \(v = r\omega\), where \(\omega\) is the angular velocity in radians per second. The arc-length formula from Section 3.1 underwrites all such mechanical-power-transmission calculations.

The second involves structural analysis: when forces or beams meet at a point, the resultant decides whether the structure is in equilibrium. Engineers compute resultants and ensure that they fall within tolerated limits — a direct application of vector addition from Section 4.2.

The third involves design and packaging: minimising material for a container of fixed volume, optimising the dimensions of a metal sheet, or determining the radius of an arch that fits a given span and rise. Each problem reduces to the same loop: write the constraint, write the objective, reduce to one variable, optimise.

  • Linear and angular speed — \(v = r\omega\), with \(\omega\) in radians per unit time.
  • Equilibrium — the net (resultant) of all forces acting on an object is zero.
  • Tolerance — the acceptable range around a target dimension or quantity; defined by engineering practice.

Worked Example — Pulley belt speed

A pulley of radius 8 cm rotates at 300 revolutions per minute (rpm). What is the linear speed of a point on its rim in m/s?

Step 1. Convert rpm to rad/s: \(300 \cdot 2\pi / 60 = 10\pi\) rad/s.

Step 2. Apply \(v = r\omega\): \(v = 0.08 \cdot 10\pi = 0.8\pi\) m/s \(\approx 2.51\) m/s.

Step 3. This is the speed of the belt riding on this pulley; equal to the rim speed of any other pulley on the same belt.

Worked Example — Rope tension in equilibrium

A 100 N weight hangs from two ropes; the left rope is 30° above horizontal and the right rope is 45° above horizontal. The system is in equilibrium. Find the magnitudes of the tensions \(T_{1}\) (left, 30°) and \(T_{2}\) (right, 45°).

Step 1. Horizontal equilibrium: \(T_{1}\cos 30° = T_{2}\cos 45°\). So \(T_{1} = T_{2}\cos 45° / \cos 30°\).

Step 2. Vertical equilibrium: \(T_{1}\sin 30° + T_{2}\sin 45° = 100\). Substitute \(T_{1}\): \((T_{2}\cos 45°/\cos 30°)(\sin 30°) + T_{2}\sin 45° = 100\).

Step 3. Use values \(\cos 45°=\sin 45°=\sqrt{2}/2\), \(\cos 30°=\sqrt{3}/2\), \(\sin 30°=1/2\): \(T_{2}(\sqrt{2}/2)(1/2)/(\sqrt{3}/2) + T_{2}(\sqrt{2}/2) = T_{2}(\sqrt{2}/(2\sqrt{3})) + T_{2}(\sqrt{2}/2) = 100\). Numerically \(T_{2}(0.408 + 0.707) = T_{2}(1.115) = 100\), so \(T_{2}\approx 89.66\) N.

Step 4. Then \(T_{1} = 89.66 \cdot 0.707 / 0.866 \approx 73.20\) N.

  • Whenever angles in a problem mix degrees and radians, convert everything to one system before computing.
  • For equilibrium, set the resultant equal to zero, not just one component.
  • State units throughout; tension in newtons, speed in m/s — calculus textbooks may use other units, so be careful.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.3 — "solve problems involving the properties of circles and arcs in real-world contexts (e.g., gears, pulleys, architectural design)."
