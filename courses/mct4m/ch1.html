1.1 — Rational Exponents and the Laws of Exponents
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In Grade 11 you mastered integer exponents and saw a few rational ones. MCT4M opens with a careful reconstruction of the entire laws of exponents so that you can simplify and evaluate every power expression you will meet — including those with negative and fractional exponents — without ambiguity. Most college technology programs (electronics, civil, mechanical) demand fluency with exponents in formulas like \(P = P_0 (1+r)^n\), \(I = V/R^{1/2}\) (in some empirical models), and the time-constant expressions in RC circuits, so this opening section is high-leverage.

The single most important rule to internalize is the meaning of a rational exponent: \(a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^{m}\), provided \(a \ge 0\) when \(n\) is even. The numerator says "raise to the \(m\)th power"; the denominator says "take the \(n\)th root". Once that is clear, every "fractional exponent" question reduces to a chain of familiar operations. A negative exponent simply means reciprocate: \(a^{-k} = 1/a^{k}\). Combine these and you can rewrite expressions like \(8^{-2/3}\) in seconds.

The classic exponent laws — product (\(a^{m}\cdot a^{n}=a^{m+n}\)), quotient (\(a^{m}/a^{n}=a^{m-n}\)), power-of-a-power (\((a^{m})^{n}=a^{mn}\)), power-of-a-product (\((ab)^{m}=a^{m}b^{m}\)), zero-power (\(a^{0}=1\) for \(a\ne 0\)) — all extend cleanly to rational exponents. You will reach for them constantly when simplifying technical formulas.

  • Base — the number being raised to a power; in \(a^{m/n}\) it is \(a\).
  • Exponent (index) — the rational number telling you how many times to multiply (and which root to take).
  • Rational exponent — an exponent of the form \(m/n\) with \(n\ne 0\); equivalent to the radical \(\sqrt[n]{a^{m}}\).
  • Radical — the root form \(\sqrt[n]{x}\); the \(n\)-value is called the index of the radical.

Worked Example — Evaluating a fractional power

Evaluate \(27^{2/3}\) exactly.

Step 1. Rewrite using the rational-exponent rule: \(27^{2/3} = (\sqrt[3]{27})^{2}\).

Step 2. Compute the cube root: \(\sqrt[3]{27} = 3\) (since \(3^{3}=27\)).

Step 3. Square the result: \(3^{2}=9\).

Conclusion: \(27^{2/3}=9\).

Worked Example — Simplifying with negative rational exponents

Simplify \(\dfrac{x^{1/2}\cdot x^{-3/4}}{x^{-1/4}}\) and write the answer with positive exponents.

Step 1. Combine the numerator with the product law: \(x^{1/2-3/4} = x^{-1/4}\).

Step 2. Divide by the denominator (quotient law): \(x^{-1/4} \div x^{-1/4} = x^{-1/4-(-1/4)} = x^{0} = 1\).

Conclusion: the expression simplifies to \(1\) (for \(x>0\)).

  • Do not confuse \(a^{-1}\) with \(-a\). The first reciprocates; the second negates.
  • \(a^{1/n}\) is only defined as a real number for \(a<0\) when \(n\) is odd; \((-8)^{1/3}=-2\), but \((-4)^{1/2}\) is not a real number.
  • The exponent laws only apply when the same base is used. \(2^{3}\cdot 3^{3}\) is not \(6^{6}\) — it is \(6^{3}\) via the power-of-a-product rule.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "simplify algebraic expressions containing integer and rational exponents, using the laws of exponents."

1.2 — Exponential Functions and Their Graphs
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An exponential function has the form \(f(x) = a \cdot b^{x}\), where \(a\ne 0\) and \(b>0\), \(b\ne 1\). The base \(b\) drives the function's behaviour: if \(b>1\) the curve grows; if \(0

Three graphical facts are worth committing to memory. First, every exponential of the form \(b^{x}\) passes through \((0,1)\), because \(b^{0}=1\). Second, the horizontal line \(y=0\) is a horizontal asymptote — exponential functions never reach zero, they only approach it. Third, exponentials are monotonic: they are either always increasing (\(b>1\)) or always decreasing (\(0

You will also work with transformed exponentials \(f(x) = a\cdot b^{k(x-d)} + c\). The vertical shift \(c\) moves the horizontal asymptote up or down to \(y=c\); reading the asymptote first then back-solving for the other parameters is the easiest way to find an equation from a graph.

  • Exponential function — \(f(x)=a\cdot b^{x}\) with a constant base and the variable in the exponent.
  • Growth factor — base \(b>1\); each unit of \(x\) multiplies the output by \(b\).
  • Decay factor — base \(0
  • Horizontal asymptote — the line the curve approaches but never crosses; \(y=c\) for the shifted form.

Worked Example — Reading parameters from a model

A culture of bacteria starts at 200 cells and triples every hour. Write the equation, identify base and \(y\)-intercept, and predict the population after 5 hours.

Step 1. Initial value \(a=200\). Tripling per hour gives growth factor \(b=3\), so \(P(t) = 200\cdot 3^{t}\).

Step 2. Check: \(P(0)=200\cdot 3^{0}=200\). ✓

Step 3. After 5 hours: \(P(5)=200\cdot 3^{5}=200\cdot 243 = 48{,}600\) cells.

Worked Example — Sketching with a vertical shift

Describe the asymptote and key point of \(f(x) = 2^{x} - 5\).

Step 1. Parent function \(y=2^{x}\) has asymptote \(y=0\) and passes through \((0,1)\).

Step 2. Subtract 5 from every output: asymptote moves to \(y=-5\); the point \((0,1)\) moves to \((0,-4)\).

Step 3. \(x\)-intercept: solve \(2^{x}=5\). The graph crosses where the unshifted curve hits height 5 — approximately \(x\approx 2.32\).

  • An exponential function cannot have base 1 (constant) or a negative base (oscillates and is not a real-valued continuous function).
  • The \(y\)-intercept of \(a\cdot b^{x}\) is just \(a\); do not confuse \(a\) with the base \(b\).
  • A growth factor of, say, 1.05 corresponds to a 5% growth rate per period — not 105%.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "solve simple exponential equations numerically and graphically, with technology, and recognize that the solution may also be found using inverse operations."

1.3 — Meaning of a Logarithm
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A logarithm is just an exponent in disguise. The statement \(\log_{b} N = x\) is read "the logarithm of \(N\) to base \(b\) equals \(x\)" and means exactly the same thing as \(b^{x} = N\). Once you absorb this equivalence you can flip between exponential and logarithmic form at will, which is exactly what every Strand A problem demands.

Two special logs come up so often they get their own notation. The common logarithm \(\log N\) (no base shown) means \(\log_{10} N\); your calculator's log key returns this value. The natural logarithm \(\ln N\) means \(\log_{e} N\), where \(e \approx 2.71828\) is Euler's number. The natural log appears in continuous-growth and electrical-engineering formulas; the common log appears in pH, decibels, the Richter scale, and most engineering charts.

Because logs and exponentials are inverse operations, two identities are essentially free: \(\log_{b}(b^{x}) = x\) and \(b^{\log_{b} x} = x\). Use them aggressively to undo exponentials by logs and vice versa. Also remember that the input of a log must be positive: \(\log_{b} N\) is undefined for \(N\le 0\) in real numbers.

  • Logarithm — \(\log_{b} N = x \;\Leftrightarrow\; b^{x} = N\). The log returns the exponent.
  • Common log — base-10 logarithm; written \(\log N\).
  • Natural log — base-\(e\) logarithm; written \(\ln N\).
  • Argument (input) of a log — the value \(N\); must be strictly positive.

Worked Example — Translating between forms

Rewrite each statement in the other form: (a) \(\log_{2} 32 = 5\); (b) \(10^{3} = 1000\).

(a) Exponential form: \(2^{5} = 32\). Verify: \(2^{5}=32\). ✓

(b) Logarithmic form: \(\log_{10} 1000 = 3\), commonly written \(\log 1000 = 3\). ✓

Worked Example — Evaluating exact logs

Evaluate \(\log_{5} 125\) and \(\log_{2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{16}\right)\).

Step 1. Ask: \(5\) to what power equals \(125\)? Since \(5^{3}=125\), we have \(\log_{5} 125 = 3\).

Step 2. Ask: \(2\) to what power equals \(\tfrac{1}{16}\)? Since \(2^{-4}=\tfrac{1}{16}\), we have \(\log_{2}(1/16) = -4\).

  • \(\log_{b} 1 = 0\) for any valid base, because \(b^{0}=1\).
  • \(\log_{b} b = 1\) for any valid base, because \(b^{1}=b\).
  • Logs of negative numbers and of zero are undefined in real numbers — calculators return an error.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.1 — "recognize the logarithm of a number to a given base as the exponent to which the base must be raised to get the number; evaluate simple logarithmic expressions."

1.4 — Laws of Logarithms
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Because logs are exponents, every exponent law has a logarithm counterpart. The three core laws of logarithms are: the product law \(\log_{b}(MN) = \log_{b} M + \log_{b} N\), the quotient law \(\log_{b}(M/N) = \log_{b} M - \log_{b} N\), and the power law \(\log_{b}(M^{k}) = k\log_{b} M\). Together with the change-of-base formula \(\log_{b} M = \dfrac{\log M}{\log b}\), these four tools handle every log-simplification problem in this course.

Two practical guidelines will save you from common errors. First, the laws only combine logs with the same base — you cannot merge \(\log_{2} 8\) with \(\log_{3} 27\) directly. Second, the laws never apply inside a logarithm to its argument unless the argument is multiplied, divided, or raised to a power. For instance, \(\log(M+N)\) is not \(\log M + \log N\); that is one of the most common Grade 12 errors. The product law applies only to products, not sums.

The change-of-base formula is what your calculator silently uses every time you press log on a log of an unfamiliar base. Internally, the calculator evaluates \(\log_{b} M\) by computing \(\log M / \log b\) using its stored base-10 algorithm.

  • Product law — \(\log_{b}(MN)=\log_{b} M+\log_{b} N\).
  • Quotient law — \(\log_{b}(M/N)=\log_{b} M-\log_{b} N\).
  • Power law — \(\log_{b}(M^{k})=k\log_{b} M\).
  • Change of base — \(\log_{b} M = \dfrac{\log_{c} M}{\log_{c} b}\); usually \(c=10\) or \(c=e\).

Worked Example — Expanding a complicated log

Write \(\log\!\left(\dfrac{100 x^{3}}{y^{2}}\right)\) as a sum/difference of simpler logs.

Step 1. Use the quotient law: \(\log(100 x^{3}) - \log(y^{2})\).

Step 2. Use the product law on the first term: \(\log 100 + \log(x^{3}) - \log(y^{2})\).

Step 3. Use the power law: \(\log 100 + 3\log x - 2\log y\). Since \(\log 100 = 2\), this simplifies to \(2 + 3\log x - 2\log y\).

Worked Example — Combining into a single log

Express \(2\log x + 3\log y - \tfrac{1}{2}\log z\) as a single logarithm.

Step 1. Apply the power law inside out: \(\log x^{2} + \log y^{3} - \log z^{1/2}\).

Step 2. Use the product law on the first two: \(\log(x^{2}y^{3}) - \log z^{1/2}\).

Step 3. Use the quotient law: \(\log\!\left(\dfrac{x^{2}y^{3}}{\sqrt{z}}\right)\).

  • The laws of logarithms only work for products, quotients, and powers — never sums or differences inside the argument.
  • \(\log(M\cdot N) \ne \log M \cdot \log N\). The product law turns the multiplication into a sum.
  • Always check that arguments stay positive: \(\log(x-3)\) requires \(x>3\), which restricts the domain.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.3 — "make connections between related logarithmic and exponential equations and apply the laws of logarithms to simplify logarithmic expressions."

1.5 — Solving Exponential Equations
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An exponential equation has the variable in the exponent — for example \(2^{x}=64\) or \(5^{x+1}=18\). There are two main techniques, and the technique you pick depends on whether the two sides can be expressed with a common base. If they can, equate the exponents directly; if not, take the logarithm of both sides and isolate \(x\) using the power law.

The common-base method is fast: \(2^{x}=64\) becomes \(2^{x}=2^{6}\), so \(x=6\). The key skill is recognizing that "ugly" numbers like 64, 32, 1024 are powers of 2; that 27, 81, 243 are powers of 3; that 125, 625, 3125 are powers of 5. A clean prime factor of both sides is a strong signal that the common-base method works.

When the bases will not match — for instance \(5^{x}=30\) — the logarithm method is general and reliable. Apply \(\log\) to both sides, use the power law to bring \(x\) out of the exponent, then divide. The final answer is usually exact in log form (\(x=\log 30 / \log 5\)) but can be approximated with a calculator (\(x\approx 2.113\)).

  • Common-base method — write both sides with the same base, then equate exponents.
  • Logarithm method — apply \(\log\) (any base) to both sides, then use the power law to solve for \(x\).
  • Exact vs approximate — \(x=\log_{5} 30\) is exact; \(x\approx 2.113\) is an approximation.

Worked Example — Common-base method

Solve \(4^{x-1} = 32\).

Step 1. Write both sides as powers of 2: \(4 = 2^{2}\) and \(32 = 2^{5}\), so \((2^{2})^{x-1}=2^{5}\).

Step 2. Simplify the left side: \(2^{2(x-1)} = 2^{2x-2}\). So \(2^{2x-2}=2^{5}\).

Step 3. Equate exponents: \(2x-2=5\), so \(x = 7/2\).

Worked Example — Logarithm method with application

An investment doubles every year (continuous compounding, factor 2 per year). After how many years will $500 grow to $3000? Use \(3000 = 500\cdot 2^{t}\).

Step 1. Divide both sides by 500: \(2^{t} = 6\).

Step 2. Apply \(\log\): \(\log 2^{t} = \log 6\), so \(t \log 2 = \log 6\).

Step 3. Divide: \(t = \log 6 / \log 2 \approx 0.7782 / 0.3010 \approx 2.585\) years (about 2 years 7 months).

  • If the exponent is itself an expression like \(x+1\), expand using the laws of exponents before isolating \(x\).
  • Both methods can solve the same problem when the common base exists — pick whichever is faster.
  • Always check your final answer is reasonable in context: if \(t\) represents time, it should be positive.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A3.2 — "solve exponential equations using logarithms (e.g., solve \(5^{x}=30\) by taking the logarithm of both sides and using the power law).”

1.6 — Applications: Growth, Decay, and Compound Interest
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The exponential model \(A(t) = A_{0} b^{t}\) is the workhorse for technical applications. Three classic situations dominate college technology courses: population growth, radioactive decay, and compound interest. Each problem reduces to choosing \(A_{0}\) (the starting amount), the growth/decay factor \(b\), and the time variable \(t\).

For population growth at rate \(r\) per period, the factor is \(b = 1+r\) and the model is \(A(t) = A_{0}(1+r)^{t}\). For radioactive decay with half-life \(H\), the factor is \(b = (1/2)^{1/H}\) and the model is \(A(t) = A_{0}(1/2)^{t/H}\). For compound interest with annual rate \(r\) compounded \(n\) times per year over \(t\) years, the formula is \(A = P(1+r/n)^{nt}\), which is the workhorse of finance and is on the front page of every banker's spreadsheet.

When you are asked to find the time it takes to reach a target amount, you get an exponential equation in \(t\) — apply the techniques from 1.5 to solve. Always state units (years, hours, half-lives) and never forget that the model is an idealization: in real engineering, you would also include error margins.

  • Compound interest — \(A = P(1+r/n)^{nt}\); \(P\) is principal, \(r\) is annual rate (as decimal), \(n\) is compoundings per year, \(t\) is years.
  • Half-life — the time \(H\) for a quantity to decay to half its previous value; the decay model is \(A(t)=A_{0}(1/2)^{t/H}\).
  • Doubling time — analogue of half-life for growth; the time \(D\) for the quantity to double.
  • Continuous compounding — the limit as \(n\to\infty\); formula becomes \(A=Pe^{rt}\) (introduced later in college courses).

Worked Example — Compound interest

$2000 is invested at 6% annual interest compounded quarterly. What is the balance after 5 years?

Step 1. Identify: \(P=2000\), \(r=0.06\), \(n=4\), \(t=5\).

Step 2. Substitute: \(A=2000(1+0.06/4)^{4\cdot 5}=2000(1.015)^{20}\).

Step 3. Evaluate: \((1.015)^{20}\approx 1.3469\), so \(A \approx 2000 \cdot 1.3469 \approx \$2693.71\).

Worked Example — Radioactive decay

Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5730 years. A sample retains 30% of its original C-14. How old is it?

Step 1. Model: \(A(t) = A_{0}(1/2)^{t/5730}\). Use \(A/A_{0}=0.30\): \((1/2)^{t/5730}=0.30\).

Step 2. Apply \(\log\): \((t/5730)\log(1/2) = \log 0.30\).

Step 3. Solve: \(t = 5730 \cdot \log 0.30 / \log(1/2) \approx 5730 \cdot (-0.523) / (-0.301) \approx 9953\) years.

  • Write the rate as a decimal, not a percent — \(6\%\) is \(0.06\), not \(6\).
  • When compounding is more frequent, check that you use the per-period rate \(r/n\) and the total number of periods \(nt\).
  • Round only at the final step — intermediate rounding magnifies the error in long-term projections.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A3.4 — "pose and solve problems involving exponential growth and decay (e.g., compound interest, population growth, radioactive decay) using the exponential function as a model."
