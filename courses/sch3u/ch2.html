⚖️ Balancing Chemical Equations
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A chemical equation represents a chemical change using formulas: reactants on the left, products on the right, separated by an arrow. The law of conservation of mass requires that the number of atoms of each element be identical on both sides; we satisfy this by adjusting coefficients (the whole numbers in front of formulas). Subscripts inside a formula must never be changed — that would change the identity of the substance.

A workable balancing procedure: (1) write the unbalanced "skeleton" equation with correct formulas; (2) list elements that appear in just one reactant and one product and balance those first; (3) balance polyatomic ions that remain intact as single units; (4) balance H and O last; (5) verify atom counts on each side; (6) reduce coefficients to lowest whole-number ratio. State symbols (s, l, g, aq) are added at the end. For combustion of organic fuels, balance C first (from CO₂), then H (from H₂O), then O (from O₂) — and double the equation if you end with fractional O₂.

Mass is conserved in every balanced equation. The total mass of reactants must equal the total mass of products. Charge is also conserved in ionic equations: the net charge on the left must equal the net charge on the right.

  • Coefficient — number multiplying a whole formula (can change).
  • Subscript — number inside a formula (never change).
  • Reactant / product — substance consumed / formed.
  • State symbols — (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.
  • Skeleton equation — unbalanced equation with correct formulas.

Worked Example: Balance the combustion of butane

Skeleton: \(C_4H_{10} + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + H_2O\).

Balance C: 4 CO₂ on right. Balance H: 5 H₂O on right (10 H atoms). Now O: right has 4(2) + 5(1) = 13 O atoms → need 13/2 O₂. Multiply by 2 to clear the fraction: \(2C_4H_{10} + 13O_2 \rightarrow 8CO_2 + 10H_2O\). Verify: C: 8 = 8 ✓; H: 20 = 20 ✓; O: 26 = 26 ✓.

Common mistakes:

  • Changing a subscript (e.g., writing H₂O as H₂O₂) instead of placing a coefficient in front.
  • Forgetting that diatomic elements are H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ — "BrINClHOF".
  • Leaving a fractional coefficient in the final answer — multiply through to get whole numbers.
  • Not reducing coefficients to lowest whole-number ratio (e.g., \(4H_2 + 2O_2 \rightarrow 4H_2O\) should be \(2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O\)).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.5 — "write balanced chemical equations to represent the synthesis or decomposition of a chemical compound by trial and error and by inspection, and using information obtained from the periodic table."

🧩 Classification of Reactions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Most reactions encountered in SCH3U fit into six general types. Recognizing the type lets you predict products from given reactants.

  • Synthesis (combination): \(A + B \rightarrow AB\). Two or more reactants combine into one product. Example: \(2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO\).
  • Decomposition: \(AB \rightarrow A + B\). One reactant breaks into two or more products, often driven by heat or electricity. Example: \(2H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{electrolysis}} 2H_2 + O_2\).
  • Single displacement: \(A + BC \rightarrow AC + B\). A free element replaces another in a compound. The free element must be more reactive (consult the activity series for metals and a separate halogen series for non-metals). Example: \(Zn + CuSO_4 \rightarrow ZnSO_4 + Cu\).
  • Double displacement (metathesis): \(AB + CD \rightarrow AD + CB\). Cation-anion partners swap between two ionic compounds in aqueous solution. A reaction occurs only if a precipitate forms (use solubility rules), a gas evolves, or a weak electrolyte (such as water) is produced. Example: \(AgNO_3(aq) + NaCl(aq) \rightarrow AgCl(s) + NaNO_3(aq)\).
  • Combustion: a fuel reacts with O₂ to release energy. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon yields CO₂ + H₂O; incomplete combustion produces CO and/or soot (C). Example: \(CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O\).
  • Neutralization (acid-base): acid + base → salt + water. A special type of double displacement. Example: \(HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O\).

The activity series of metals lists metals in order of decreasing reactivity (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Ni, Sn, Pb, H, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au, Pt). A metal will displace any metal listed below it from an aqueous solution. Metals above hydrogen react with dilute acid to release H₂. The halogen activity series is F > Cl > Br > I — each halogen displaces those below it.

Worked Example: Predict the product of a single-displacement reaction

Will copper metal react with hydrochloric acid?

Cu is below H in the activity series, so Cu cannot displace H from HCl. No reaction occurs at standard conditions. Write: \(Cu(s) + HCl(aq) \rightarrow NR\). For contrast, Zn (above H) does react: \(Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow ZnCl_2(aq) + H_2(g)\).

Common mistakes:

  • Predicting a single-displacement reaction without checking the activity series — it may not occur.
  • Forgetting that double-displacement reactions require aqueous ionic reactants and a driving force (precipitate / gas / weak electrolyte).
  • Writing incomplete combustion products (CO, C) when conditions specify excess O₂.
  • Calling a synthesis reaction a "combination of elements only" — synthesis can also be compound + compound (e.g., \(CaO + H_2O \rightarrow Ca(OH)_2\)).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "describe, on the basis of laboratory experiments, the chemical reactions of various types of compounds (e.g., synthesis, decomposition, single displacement, double displacement, neutralization, combustion)."

🧪 Net Ionic Equations & Acid-Base Introduction
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

For reactions in aqueous solution between ionic compounds, three forms of the equation can be written. The molecular (formula) equation shows complete formulas of every reactant and product, e.g., \(AgNO_3(aq) + NaCl(aq) \rightarrow AgCl(s) + NaNO_3(aq)\). The complete (total) ionic equation writes every dissolved strong electrolyte as its constituent ions: \(Ag^+(aq) + NO_3^-(aq) + Na^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \rightarrow AgCl(s) + Na^+(aq) + NO_3^-(aq)\). The net ionic equation removes ions that appear unchanged on both sides — these are called spectator ions — leaving only the species that actually take part in the chemical change: \(Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \rightarrow AgCl(s)\). Net ionic equations highlight the actual chemistry and must be balanced for both mass and charge.

Arrhenius acids are substances that release H⁺ in aqueous solution; Arrhenius bases release OH⁻. Examples: HCl(aq), H₂SO₄(aq), HNO₃(aq) are strong acids; NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ are strong bases. A neutralization reaction combines an acid and a base, producing a salt and water: \(HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O\). The net ionic equation of any neutralization between a strong acid and strong base is the same: \(H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)\).

To predict whether a precipitate forms in a double-displacement reaction, use the solubility rules (provided on a reference card). Most compounds of Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, and NO₃⁻ are soluble; most carbonates (CO₃²⁻), phosphates (PO₄³⁻), sulfides (S²⁻), and hydroxides (OH⁻) are insoluble (with notable exceptions). If both possible products are soluble, no reaction occurs.

  • Spectator ion — present in solution but does not participate in the reaction.
  • Precipitate — insoluble solid that forms in aqueous reaction.
  • Strong electrolyte — dissociates completely (soluble ionic compounds, strong acids, strong bases).
  • Weak electrolyte — partially dissociates (weak acids, weak bases, water).
  • Salt — ionic product of an acid-base reaction (not necessarily NaCl).

Worked Example: Write the net ionic equation for the reaction of Pb(NO₃)₂ and KI

Molecular: \(Pb(NO_3)_2(aq) + 2KI(aq) \rightarrow PbI_2(s) + 2KNO_3(aq)\). PbI₂ is insoluble (a bright yellow precipitate, the classic "golden rain" demo).

Complete ionic: \(Pb^{2+}(aq) + 2NO_3^-(aq) + 2K^+(aq) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow PbI_2(s) + 2K^+(aq) + 2NO_3^-(aq)\).

Cross out K⁺ and NO₃⁻ (spectators). Net ionic: \(Pb^{2+}(aq) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow PbI_2(s)\). Check: charge balanced (+2 −2 = 0 each side) ✓; atoms balanced ✓.

Common mistakes:

  • Forgetting to balance charge as well as mass in net ionic equations.
  • Writing insoluble compounds (e.g., AgCl) as separate ions — they remain as one unit on the side they appear.
  • Writing weak acids (e.g., CH₃COOH) as separated ions — they stay as molecular formulas because they are mostly undissociated.
  • Forgetting to include state symbols on every species.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.5 — "write balanced chemical equations for reactions involving acids and bases (e.g., neutralization, the formation of ionic precipitates), using net ionic equations."
