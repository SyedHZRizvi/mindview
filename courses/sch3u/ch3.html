🔢 The Mole and Molar Mass
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The mole (mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance. One mole contains Avogadro's number \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\) particles (atoms, molecules, ions, formula units, electrons — whatever the context specifies). The mole is the chemist's counting unit, just as "dozen" is for eggs; it makes the link between the atomic mass we see on the periodic table (in atomic mass units, u) and a measurable mass in grams.

The molar mass \(M\) of any substance, in grams per mole, is numerically equal to the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in the formula, taken from the periodic table. For example, \(M(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02\) g/mol; \(M(\text{CO}_2) = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01\) g/mol; \(M(\text{NaCl}) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44\) g/mol.

The three master conversions tying particles, moles, and mass together:

  • Particles ↔ moles: \(n = N / N_A\).
  • Mass ↔ moles: \(n = m / M\), i.e. \(m = n \cdot M\).
  • Moles ↔ gas volume at STP: \(V = n \cdot 22.4\) L/mol (treated more carefully in Chapter 5).

Significant figures matter in every quantitative answer. Treat exact counted numbers (and SI definitions like \(N_A\)) as having infinite precision; treat measured values as having only the digits explicitly written. The result of a multiplication/division has the same number of significant figures as the least-precise factor.

Worked Example: Convert between mass, moles, and particles

How many molecules of water are in 9.00 g of H₂O?

Step 1 — mass to moles: \(n = m/M = 9.00\,\text{g} / 18.02\,\text{g/mol} = 0.499\,\text{mol}\). Step 2 — moles to particles: \(N = n \cdot N_A = 0.499 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}\) molecules.

Check magnitude: 9 g is half a mole of water, and half of Avogadro's number is about \(3 \times 10^{23}\). ✓

Common mistakes:

  • Confusing molecules with atoms. 1 mol of H₂O contains \(N_A\) molecules but \(3N_A\) atoms.
  • Using atomic mass (u) where molar mass (g/mol) is required. They are numerically equal but in different units.
  • Forgetting Avogadro's number is approximately \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\), not \(6.022 \times 10^{-23}\).
  • Reporting too many significant figures — the final answer is limited by the least-precise input.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "describe the relationship between Avogadro's number, the mole concept, and the molar mass of any given substance."

📊 Percent Composition & Empirical / Molecular Formulas
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The percent composition of a compound tells what fraction of its total mass each element contributes:

\[ \%\text{ element} = \frac{\text{mass of element in 1 mol}}{\text{molar mass of compound}} \times 100\% \]

For example, in water, \(\%\text{H} = (2 \times 1.01) / 18.02 \times 100\% = 11.2\%\) and \(\%\text{O} = 88.8\%\) (summing to ~100% within rounding). Percent composition is useful for evaluating the purity of fertilizers, ores, and pharmaceutical actives.

An empirical formula gives the smallest whole-number ratio of atoms; a molecular formula gives the actual number of each atom in one molecule. The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Determining an empirical formula from percent composition (or mass) follows a five-step routine:

  1. Assume a 100 g sample → percent values become mass in grams.
  2. Convert each mass to moles via \(n = m / M\).
  3. Divide all mole values by the smallest of them.
  4. If results are not whole numbers, multiply through by a small integer (e.g., 2 for 1.5, 3 for 1.33 or 1.67) to clear fractions.
  5. The whole-number ratios are the subscripts of the empirical formula.

To find the molecular formula, divide the experimentally measured molar mass by the empirical formula mass and multiply each subscript by the integer ratio.

  • Empirical formula — simplest whole-number ratio (e.g., CH₂O for glucose).
  • Molecular formula — actual molecule (e.g., C₆H₁₂O₆ for glucose; 6× the empirical formula).
  • Formula unit — empirical formula of an ionic compound (e.g., NaCl, MgCl₂).
  • Hydrate — ionic compound that includes a fixed number of water molecules per formula unit (e.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O).

Worked Example: Empirical and molecular formula from percent composition

A compound contains 40.0% C, 6.7% H, and 53.3% O by mass, and has a molar mass of 60.0 g/mol. Find both the empirical and molecular formulas.

Assume 100 g: 40.0 g C, 6.7 g H, 53.3 g O. Convert to moles: C: 40.0/12.01 = 3.33 mol; H: 6.7/1.01 = 6.63 mol; O: 53.3/16.00 = 3.33 mol. Divide by smallest (3.33): C 1.00, H 1.99, O 1.00 → ratio C:H:O = 1:2:1. Empirical formula = CH₂O, mass = 12.01 + 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 30.03 g/mol. Molecular/empirical ratio = 60.0/30.03 ≈ 2, so molecular formula = C₂H₄O₂ (which is the formula for ethanoic acid).

Common mistakes:

  • Rounding mole ratios too aggressively — 1.5 must be multiplied by 2, not rounded to 2.
  • Forgetting to multiply the empirical formula by the integer scaling factor when finding the molecular formula.
  • Mixing percent composition with mole percent — always work in mass, then convert.
  • Treating an experimentally determined molar mass as exact — the integer multiplier should be the nearest small whole number.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.4 — "explain the law of definite proportions and the law of multiple proportions, and the relationship between these laws and chemical formulas; determine the empirical and/or molecular formulae of various chemical compounds, given the relevant data (e.g., the percentage composition by mass of the elements present, the molar mass)."

⚖️ Stoichiometry: Mass, Moles & Mole Ratios
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Stoichiometry is the quantitative analysis of reactants and products in a balanced chemical equation. The coefficients of a balanced equation give the mole ratio between any two species. To convert from grams of one substance to grams of another, the universal map is:

\[ \text{mass A} \xrightarrow{\div M_A} \text{moles A} \xrightarrow{\text{mole ratio}} \text{moles B} \xrightarrow{\times M_B} \text{mass B} \]

Variants of the same map handle particles and volumes: just replace the conversion at either end with \(\times N_A\) (particles) or \(\times 22.4\,\text{L/mol}\) (gas at STP). Always start from a balanced equation; the mole ratio must come from the coefficients of the chemicals named in the question.

For non-stoichiometric reactant mixtures, the limiting reactant is the one that runs out first — the rest are in excess. To identify it, compute how many moles of product each reactant would yield if it were used up completely; the reactant giving the smaller amount is limiting. The theoretical yield of product is governed by the limiting reactant; the others are present in excess.

The percent yield compares the experimentally measured (actual) yield to the calculated (theoretical) yield: \(\%\text{ yield} = (\text{actual} / \text{theoretical}) \times 100\%\). A yield less than 100% may arise from impurities, side reactions, losses in transferring samples, incomplete reactions, or measurement error. A yield above 100% almost always indicates an experimental error (typically, the product was not fully dried).

  • Mole ratio — ratio of coefficients between two species in a balanced equation.
  • Limiting reactant — runs out first; sets the maximum amount of product.
  • Excess reactant — present in greater than stoichiometric amount; some remains after the reaction.
  • Theoretical yield — maximum mass of product predicted from stoichiometry.
  • Actual yield — measured mass of product collected.
  • Percent yield — actual / theoretical × 100%.

Worked Example: Limiting-reactant and percent-yield calculation

10.0 g of Mg reacts with 15.0 g of HCl: \(Mg + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2\). A student isolates 7.50 g of MgCl₂. Find the limiting reactant, theoretical yield, and percent yield.

Moles: \(n_{Mg} = 10.0/24.31 = 0.411\,\text{mol}\); \(n_{HCl} = 15.0/36.46 = 0.411\,\text{mol}\). From the equation, 1 mol Mg needs 2 mol HCl. Mg requires \(2 \times 0.411 = 0.823\,\text{mol HCl}\) — but only 0.411 mol HCl is available, so HCl is limiting. Moles MgCl₂ from HCl: \(0.411 \times (1/2) = 0.206\,\text{mol}\). Theoretical mass MgCl₂: \(0.206 \times 95.21 = 19.6\,\text{g}\). Percent yield = \(7.50/19.6 \times 100\% = 38.2\%\).

Common mistakes:

  • Using mass ratios instead of mole ratios — only mole ratios match the coefficients.
  • Forgetting to balance the equation before computing the mole ratio.
  • Picking the limiting reactant by comparing initial moles directly, instead of comparing how much product each would form.
  • Reporting a percent yield over 100% without acknowledging it as an experimental error.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.5 — "solve problems related to quantities in chemical reactions, using stoichiometric relationships expressed in balanced chemical equations (e.g., calculations involving the mass, moles, and number of particles in chemical reactions); calculate, for a chemical reaction, the percentage yield, the limiting reactant, and the amount of excess reactant left after the reaction."
