⚛️ Kinetic Molecular Theory & the Simple Gas Laws
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The kinetic molecular theory (KMT) of gases provides a microscopic model that explains the macroscopic gas laws. Its postulates are:

  • Gas particles are tiny and far apart; the volume of the particles themselves is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • Particles are in constant, random, straight-line motion until they collide.
  • Collisions between particles, and with the container walls, are perfectly elastic (no net loss of kinetic energy).
  • There are no intermolecular forces between particles (no attractions or repulsions).
  • The average kinetic energy is proportional to the absolute (Kelvin) temperature: \( \bar{E}_k \propto T \).

Pressure arises from particle collisions with the container walls. Temperature in Kelvin is proportional to average kinetic energy; in Celsius it is offset (T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15). A real gas behaves most ideally at high temperature and low pressure, where particles are far apart and moving quickly. Deviation from ideality is largest at low T and high P, where intermolecular attractions and particle volume become significant.

The three simple gas laws are special cases relating two of the four state variables \(P, V, n, T\) while holding the other two constant:

  • Boyle's Law: at constant \(n, T\), \(PV = k\) (pressure and volume are inversely proportional). \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2\).
  • Charles's Law: at constant \(n, P\), \(V/T = k\) (volume directly proportional to absolute temperature). \(V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2\).
  • Gay-Lussac's Law: at constant \(n, V\), \(P/T = k\). \(P_1/T_1 = P_2/T_2\).
  • Combined gas law: \(P_1V_1/T_1 = P_2V_2/T_2\) (constant \(n\)).
  • Avogadro's Law: at constant \(P, T\), \(V/n = k\) — equal volumes of any gas at the same \(P\) and \(T\) contain equal numbers of particles.

Two reference conditions are commonly used: STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure) = 0 °C and 101.325 kPa (1 atm); SATP (Standard Ambient Temperature and Pressure) = 25 °C and 100 kPa. Always convert temperatures to Kelvin in any gas-law calculation.

Worked Example: Applying the combined gas law

A balloon contains 2.50 L of helium at 20 °C and 101 kPa. The balloon is heated to 60 °C while the external pressure drops to 95.0 kPa. What is the new volume?

Convert temperatures: T₁ = 293 K, T₂ = 333 K. Combined gas law: \(V_2 = V_1 \cdot (P_1/P_2) \cdot (T_2/T_1) = 2.50 \times (101/95.0) \times (333/293)\). Compute: \(2.50 \times 1.0632 \times 1.1365 = 3.02\,\text{L}\). The balloon expands (lower P + higher T both favor expansion). ✓

Common mistakes:

  • Using °C instead of K in a gas-law calculation — KMT requires absolute temperature.
  • Forgetting that the gas laws assume the amount of gas (\(n\)) is constant; if gas is added or removed, the combined gas law does not apply directly.
  • Confusing STP (0 °C, 101.325 kPa) with SATP (25 °C, 100 kPa); the molar volumes are 22.4 L/mol and 24.8 L/mol respectively.
  • Reporting volumes in different units on each side of an equation — use the same units throughout.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F3.2 — "explain Boyle's law, Charles's law, Gay-Lussac's law, the combined gas law, Dalton's law of partial pressures, and the ideal gas law, in qualitative and quantitative terms."

📐 Ideal Gas Equation & Gas Stoichiometry
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Combining the simple gas laws gives the ideal gas equation:

\[ PV = nRT \]

where \(R\) is the universal gas constant. SCH3U typically uses \(R = 8.314\,\text{J/(mol·K)} = 8.314\,\text{L·kPa/(mol·K)}\). When using \(R = 8.314\,\text{L·kPa/(mol·K)}\), pressures must be in kPa, volumes in L, amounts in mol, temperatures in K. An alternate form \(R = 0.0821\,\text{L·atm/(mol·K)}\) is used when pressure is in atm.

At STP (0 °C = 273.15 K, 101.325 kPa), the molar volume of any ideal gas is

\[ V_m = RT/P = (8.314)(273.15)/(101.325) = 22.4\,\text{L/mol} \]

At SATP (25 °C, 100 kPa) the molar volume is 24.8 L/mol. These shortcuts let you convert directly between moles and volume of gases at standard conditions only; for any other conditions, use \(PV = nRT\).

Combining gas-volume relationships with stoichiometry: the mole ratio from a balanced equation works for any combination of mass, particles, and gas volume. The full stoichiometry map is:

\[ V_A,\text{P, T} \xrightarrow{PV=nRT} n_A \xrightarrow{\text{mole ratio}} n_B \xrightarrow{PV=nRT} V_B,\text{P, T} \]

Dalton's law of partial pressures: in a mixture of non-reacting gases, the total pressure equals the sum of the individual (partial) pressures: \(P_{\text{total}} = P_A + P_B + P_C + \cdots\). This applies when collecting a gas over water — the measured pressure includes water vapour, which must be subtracted (using a reference table for vapour pressure of water vs T) to find the dry-gas pressure.

  • Ideal gas — hypothetical gas obeying \(PV = nRT\) exactly.
  • Real gas — actual gas; deviates from ideality at low T, high P.
  • Molar volume — volume of 1 mol of gas at given P and T (22.4 L/mol at STP).
  • Partial pressure — pressure each gas in a mixture would exert if alone in the container.
  • Standard conditions — STP (0 °C, 101.325 kPa) or SATP (25 °C, 100 kPa).

Worked Example: Gas stoichiometry with the ideal gas equation

What volume of CO₂(g), at 25 °C and 100 kPa, is produced from the complete combustion of 5.00 g of methane?

Equation: \(CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O\). Moles CH₄: \(n = 5.00/16.05 = 0.3115\,\text{mol}\). Mole ratio CH₄:CO₂ = 1:1, so moles CO₂ = 0.3115 mol.

Apply \(PV = nRT\): \(V = nRT/P = (0.3115)(8.314)(298)/(100) = 7.71\,\text{L}\). (Cross-check using molar volume at SATP: \(0.3115 \times 24.8 = 7.73\,\text{L}\) — agrees within rounding.)

Common mistakes:

  • Mixing units of \(R\): use 8.314 L·kPa/mol·K with P in kPa and V in L (the most common SCH3U convention).
  • Forgetting that "L of CO₂ collected over water" includes water-vapour pressure — subtract \(P_{H_2O}\) to find the dry-gas pressure.
  • Using molar volume 22.4 L/mol at conditions other than STP — only valid at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa.
  • Forgetting to balance the equation before using mole ratios.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F3.4 — "solve problems related to gases by performing calculations involving Avogadro's law, the gas laws, and the ideal gas law (e.g., calculate the volume occupied by a known number of moles of gas at STP using molar volume; use the ideal gas law to determine an unknown variable, given the other three)."

🌍 Atmospheric Chemistry: Ozone, Greenhouse Effect, Acid Rain
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Earth's atmosphere is a layered mixture of gases: roughly 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.9% Ar, plus trace CO₂ (currently ≈ 420 ppm, rising), water vapour (variable), and other gases. SCH3U focuses on three atmospheric chemistry issues with major societal and environmental consequences: stratospheric ozone depletion, the greenhouse effect, and acid precipitation.

Stratospheric ozone (15–35 km altitude) absorbs UV-B radiation that would otherwise damage DNA in living organisms. The Chapman cycle maintains a steady state: \(O_2 + h\nu \rightarrow 2O\); \(O + O_2 \rightarrow O_3\); \(O_3 + h\nu \rightarrow O_2 + O\). Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), used as refrigerants before the 1990s, drift up to the stratosphere, where UV releases chlorine atoms that catalytically destroy ozone: \(Cl + O_3 \rightarrow ClO + O_2\); \(ClO + O \rightarrow Cl + O_2\). One Cl atom can destroy thousands of O₃ molecules. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs internationally, and the ozone layer is now slowly recovering.

The greenhouse effect is the warming of Earth's surface by atmospheric gases that are transparent to incoming visible/UV solar radiation but absorb outgoing infrared. Major greenhouse gases include H₂O (the largest natural contributor), CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, and fluorinated gases. Combustion of fossil fuels, deforestation, and agriculture have raised atmospheric CO₂ from ~280 ppm pre-industrial to over 420 ppm today, producing the enhanced greenhouse effect responsible for observed warming. Mitigation involves reducing emissions (energy efficiency, renewable energy, carbon pricing) and removing CO₂ from the atmosphere.

Acid precipitation ("acid rain") forms when atmospheric SO₂ (from sulfur-containing fuels and metal smelting) and NO_x (from high-temperature combustion in engines and power plants) react with water vapour to produce H₂SO₄ and HNO₃: \(SO_2 + H_2O + \tfrac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow H_2SO_4\); \(2NO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow HNO_3 + HNO_2\). Rain with pH below ~5.6 (the natural pH due to dissolved CO₂) is termed acidic. Effects include acidification of lakes and soils, damage to forests, corrosion of buildings and statues, and mobilization of toxic Al³⁺ from soil. Canadian programs (CEPA, Eastern Canada Acid Rain Program) have reduced SO₂ emissions substantially since the 1980s by smokestack scrubbing and low-sulfur fuel mandates.

  • Stratosphere — atmospheric layer 15–50 km up containing the ozone layer.
  • Troposphere — atmospheric layer 0–15 km up containing weather; ground-level ozone is a pollutant here (smog).
  • CFC — chlorofluorocarbon (e.g., CCl₂F₂, "Freon-12"); banned by the Montreal Protocol.
  • Greenhouse gas — atmospheric gas that absorbs IR radiation and contributes to warming.
  • ppm / ppb — parts per million / billion; standard units for trace atmospheric components.
  • Acid precipitation — rain or snow with pH < 5.6 (sulfuric or nitric acid origin).

Worked Example: Concentration in ppm

The atmospheric concentration of CO₂ is approximately 420 ppm (by volume). What partial pressure does CO₂ contribute when total atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa?

By volume, 420 ppm = \(420 \times 10^{-6} = 4.20 \times 10^{-4}\) fraction. Using Dalton's law, partial pressure = mole fraction × total pressure = \(4.20 \times 10^{-4} \times 101.3 = 0.0425\,\text{kPa}\). Despite this very low pressure, CO₂ absorbs strongly in the IR and is the dominant anthropogenic driver of climate change.

Common mistakes:

  • Conflating stratospheric ozone (beneficial UV shield) with tropospheric ozone (harmful component of smog).
  • Treating water vapour as anthropogenic when it is largely a feedback to warming, not a forcing.
  • Saying CO₂ "causes ozone depletion" — different molecules, different layers, different mechanisms.
  • Assuming acid rain affects only the location where pollutants are emitted — atmospheric transport carries them across borders.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F1.1 — "analyse the cumulative effects of human activities and technologies on air quality, and describe some Canadian initiatives to reduce air pollution, including ways to reduce their own carbon footprint."
