💧 Solutions, Solubility Curves & the Solution Process
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The solvent is the component present in the largest amount (usually water in this course), and the solutes are the dissolved substances. Solutions can be solid (alloys), liquid (sugar water), or gaseous (air). The classifications by amount of dissolved solute are:

  • Unsaturated — less than the maximum solute at a given temperature; more solute would still dissolve.
  • Saturated — contains the maximum solute that dissolves at that temperature; in dynamic equilibrium with any undissolved solid.
  • Supersaturated — contains more than the saturated amount, prepared by cooling a hot saturated solution carefully; unstable and quickly precipitates excess solute when disturbed.
  • Miscible / immiscible — two liquids that mix in all proportions / do not mix.

A solubility curve plots the solubility of a solute (usually in g per 100 g of water) versus temperature. Curves for most ionic solids slope upward (KNO₃ rises sharply with T), some are nearly flat (NaCl ≈ 36 g/100 g at all common temperatures), and a few slope downward (gases such as O₂ and CO₂ dissolve less in warmer water — a major factor in aquatic ecosystems and global climate change).

Dissolution is driven by the rule "like dissolves like": polar solvents (water, ethanol) dissolve polar and ionic solutes; non-polar solvents (hexane, oil) dissolve non-polar solutes. In water, ionic compounds dissociate into hydrated cations and anions; molecular solutes that can hydrogen-bond with water (alcohols, sugars) dissolve well as intact molecules. The strength of the solute-solvent interaction relative to the solute-solute and solvent-solvent interactions determines whether dissolution is energetically favourable.

  • Aqueous (aq) — dissolved in water.
  • Hydration — formation of water shells around dissolved ions.
  • Electrolyte — solute that produces ions in solution and conducts electricity (strong vs weak).
  • Non-electrolyte — solute that dissolves without producing ions (e.g., sucrose, ethanol).
  • Henry's Law (qualitative) — solubility of a gas in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid; increasing T decreases gas solubility.

Worked Example: Reading a solubility curve

The solubility of KNO₃ is approximately 32 g/100 g water at 20 °C and 110 g/100 g at 60 °C. A solution prepared by dissolving 60 g of KNO₃ in 100 g of water at 60 °C is cooled to 20 °C. Describe what happens.

At 60 °C the solution is unsaturated (60 g < 110 g maximum). When cooled to 20 °C, the maximum the water can hold drops to 32 g. The remaining \(60 - 32 = 28\,\text{g}\) of KNO₃ crystallizes out. This is the basis of recrystallization, a common purification technique.

Common mistakes:

  • Saying a solute "won't dissolve" — most solutes have some non-zero solubility; the question is whether the amount is significant.
  • Treating "concentrated" as the same as "saturated" — concentrated means a lot dissolved relative to dilute, but it could still be unsaturated.
  • Forgetting that gas solubility DROPS with rising temperature (the opposite of most ionic solids).
  • Equating non-polar solvents like hexane with water — they cannot dissolve ionic compounds.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.2 — "describe the difference between an unsaturated, saturated, and supersaturated solution, and the effect of changing temperature on solubility, using a solubility curve."

🧮 Concentration & Dilution
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The concentration of a solution measures how much solute is dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution. SCH3U uses five common units:

  • Molar concentration (molarity): \(c = n/V\) where \(n\) is moles of solute and \(V\) is litres of solution. Unit: mol/L (also written M). This is the most-used in stoichiometry.
  • Mass / volume percent (% m/v): grams of solute per 100 mL of solution. Used in medicine (e.g., 0.9% saline = 0.9 g NaCl in 100 mL).
  • Volume percent (% v/v): mL of solute per 100 mL of solution. Used for alcohol in beverages.
  • Parts per million (ppm): mg of solute per kg of solvent (or mg/L for dilute aqueous solutions). Used for environmental analytes (e.g., drinking-water guidelines).
  • Parts per billion (ppb): μg solute per kg solvent. Used for trace contaminants such as heavy metals.

Dilution reduces the concentration of a solution by adding more solvent; the amount of solute remains constant. Since \(n = cV\), the master equation is

\[ c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2 \]

This formula works with any consistent concentration unit so long as the units on both sides match. Concentrated stock solutions are commonly stored in labs and diluted as needed; serial dilution (a dilution of a previous dilution) is used to span large concentration ranges (e.g., in calibration curves).

  • Stock solution — a concentrated solution from which dilutions are made.
  • Standard solution — solution of precisely known concentration; prepared in volumetric glassware.
  • Volumetric flask — glassware calibrated to contain an exact volume (e.g., 100.00 mL) when filled to the etched line.
  • Pipette — glassware that delivers a precise volume (often 10 mL, 25 mL).

Worked Example: Dilution calculation

A chemist needs 250 mL of 0.100 mol/L HCl. What volume of 6.00 mol/L stock HCl is required?

\(c_1V_1 = c_2V_2 \Rightarrow V_1 = c_2V_2/c_1 = (0.100)(250)/6.00 = 4.17\,\text{mL}\).

Procedure: pipette 4.17 mL of 6.00 mol/L HCl into a 250.0 mL volumetric flask containing some water, then dilute to the mark. (Always add acid to water, not water to acid — concentrated HCl releases substantial heat on mixing.)

Common mistakes:

  • Using volumes in different units in \(c_1V_1 = c_2V_2\) — both volumes must be in the same unit.
  • Forgetting that \(V\) refers to the total volume of the solution, not of the solvent added.
  • Treating g/L as the same as mol/L. They differ by a factor of the molar mass.
  • Mixing ppm (mg/L) with mol/L without converting via molar mass.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.3 — "calculate the concentration of solutions in mass/volume percent, parts per million, parts per billion, and moles per litre; solve problems involving dilutions."

⚗️ Acids, Bases & Titration
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Arrhenius definitions describe an acid as a substance that releases H⁺ (more precisely, H₃O⁺) in water, and a base as a substance that releases OH⁻ in water. Strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄) dissociate completely; weak acids (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, HF) only partially dissociate. Strong bases are typically Group 1 hydroxides (NaOH, KOH) plus heavier Group 2 hydroxides (Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂); weak bases include NH₃ and amines.

Water itself slightly autoionizes: \(2H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + OH^-\), with the autoionization constant \(K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\) at 25 °C. The pH scale compresses the wide range of [H⁺] in aqueous solutions into a logarithmic scale:

\[ \text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+] \qquad [H^+] = 10^{-\text{pH}} \qquad \text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14 \]

Pure water has pH 7 (neutral). Acidic solutions have pH < 7; basic solutions have pH > 7. Indicators change colour over specific pH ranges (litmus: red < 5, blue > 8; phenolphthalein: colourless < 8, pink > 10).

A titration determines the unknown concentration of an acid or base by neutralizing it with a measured volume of a standard solution from a burette. The equivalence point is when stoichiometric amounts of acid and base have reacted; an indicator's endpoint ideally coincides with the equivalence point. For a monoprotic acid-monoprotic base titration: \(c_a V_a = c_b V_b\). For a strong-acid + strong-base titration the equivalence-point pH is 7; for weak acid + strong base it is > 7 (basic salt); for strong acid + weak base it is < 7 (acidic salt).

  • Conjugate pair — acid/base differing by one H⁺ (e.g., HCl/Cl⁻, NH₃/NH₄⁺).
  • Endpoint vs equivalence point — colour change vs exact stoichiometric mixing.
  • Standard solution — solution of precisely known concentration.
  • Burette — graduated glass tube for delivering variable measured volumes.
  • Diprotic acid — releases two H⁺ per molecule (e.g., H₂SO₄, requires 2× the moles of base).

Worked Example: Acid-base titration calculation

A 25.00 mL sample of HCl(aq) of unknown concentration is titrated to the equivalence point with 18.45 mL of 0.1050 mol/L NaOH(aq). Find the molar concentration and pH of the HCl.

Reaction: \(HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O\); mole ratio 1:1. Moles NaOH: \(n = cV = 0.1050 \times 0.01845 = 1.937 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{mol}\). Moles HCl = \(1.937 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{mol}\). Concentration: \(c = n/V = 1.937 \times 10^{-3} / 0.02500 = 0.07749\,\text{mol/L}\).

Since HCl is a strong acid, \([H^+] = 0.07749\,\text{mol/L}\), and \(\text{pH} = -\log(0.07749) = 1.11\).

Common mistakes:

  • Forgetting to convert volume from mL to L before computing moles.
  • Assuming the mole ratio is always 1:1 — for H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH it's 1:2.
  • Confusing pH with [H⁺] — pH is a logarithmic transformation.
  • Reporting a negative pH for very concentrated strong acids — pH < 0 is possible but rare in SCH3U.
  • Picking an indicator without considering whether its colour-change range covers the equivalence-point pH.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.5 — "describe the Arrhenius theory of acids and bases, and use the pH scale; solve problems involving acid-base titrations (e.g., calculate concentration of an acid or base from titration data, using \(c_aV_a = c_bV_b\) for monoprotic reactions)."

Lab safety reminder (WHMIS / SDS)
Always wear splash-proof goggles and gloves during titrations. When diluting concentrated acids, add the acid to the water (never the reverse) and stir while pouring. Dispose of acid-base waste only in the neutral waste container after pH-checking with universal indicator strips.
