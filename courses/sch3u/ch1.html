⚛️ Atomic Theory & Periodic Trends
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The modern understanding of the atom has emerged from a sequence of models, each refined to explain new observations. John Dalton (1808) proposed indivisible spheres with masses unique to each element. J.J. Thomson (1897) discovered the electron in cathode rays and proposed the plum-pudding model. Ernest Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911) showed that most of an atom is empty space, with a small dense positive nucleus. Niels Bohr (1913) explained the line spectrum of hydrogen by proposing that electrons occupy discrete energy levels (shells); transitions between levels emit or absorb photons of specific energies, \(\Delta E = h\nu\). The quantum-mechanical model that followed treats electrons as standing waves located in orbitals, but for SCH3U the Bohr-Rutherford framework is the working model: protons + neutrons in a nucleus, electrons arranged in shells (2, 8, 8, 18 for the first four shells).

The periodic table organizes the elements by increasing atomic number into periods (rows) and groups (columns). Elements in the same group have similar valence electron configurations and therefore similar chemical behaviour. Key periodic trends, all consequences of effective nuclear charge \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) and shielding:

  • Atomic radius decreases across a period (\(Z_{\text{eff}}\) rises with no new shell) and increases down a group (new shells added).
  • First ionization energy (energy to remove the outermost electron) increases across a period and decreases down a group — opposite to radius.
  • Electron affinity (energy released when an atom gains an electron) generally becomes more negative across a period.
  • Electronegativity (Pauling scale) increases across a period and decreases down a group; F is the most electronegative element (4.0), Cs/Fr the least.
  • Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group — the inverse of electronegativity.

Worked Example: Predicting Periodic Trends

Arrange Na, Mg, Cl, F in order of increasing first ionization energy.

Down a group, IE drops; across a period, IE rises. Na (period 3, group 1) is the lowest. Mg (group 2, same period) is higher than Na. Cl (period 3, group 17) is higher than Mg because it is much further across. F (period 2, same group as Cl) is highest because radius is smaller in period 2. Final order: \(\text{Na} \lt \text{Mg} \lt \text{Cl} \lt \text{F}\).

Common mistakes:

  • Reading "ionization energy decreases down a group" as meaning IE always decreases left-to-right — it does the opposite across a period.
  • Forgetting that noble gases have very high ionization energies and effectively zero electron affinity.
  • Mixing up electron affinity (energy released) and electronegativity (tendency to attract bonding electrons).
  • Predicting trends from group number alone without considering the period.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "use the periodic table to predict and explain the chemical properties of elements (e.g., reactivity, atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, metallic character) and to predict the formulae and properties of compounds formed by these elements."

🔗 Ionic & Covalent Bonding
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A chemical bond is the force that holds atoms together in a compound, and its character is determined by the difference in electronegativity \(\Delta\text{EN}\) between the bonded atoms. A useful (approximate) rule of thumb: \(\Delta\text{EN} \gt 1.7\) → ionic; \(0.4 \lt \Delta\text{EN} \lt 1.7\) → polar covalent; \(\Delta\text{EN} \lt 0.4\) → non-polar covalent. These are not strict boundaries — bonding is a continuum.

In ionic bonding, valence electrons transfer from a metal (low IE) to a non-metal (high EN), forming cations and anions held together by electrostatic attraction in a regular crystal lattice. Ionic compounds have high melting points, brittle crystals, are typically soluble in water, and conduct electricity when molten or in solution (charged ions are mobile) but not as solids. Formulas use the "crossover" rule from oxidation numbers — e.g., \(\text{Al}^{3+} + \text{O}^{2-} \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\).

In covalent bonding, electrons are shared between non-metal atoms. A bond is non-polar if shared equally (e.g., \(\text{H}_2\), \(\text{Cl}_2\)) and polar if shared unevenly because the more electronegative atom pulls the electron density toward itself (e.g., HCl has a partial \(\delta^+\) on H and \(\delta^-\) on Cl). The molecule as a whole can still be non-polar if the polar bonds are arranged symmetrically (e.g., \(\text{CO}_2\) — linear — bond dipoles cancel; \(\text{CCl}_4\) — tetrahedral — bond dipoles cancel). The number of bonds an atom forms equals its number of unpaired valence electrons; the octet rule states that main-group atoms tend to achieve eight valence electrons. Hydrogen takes only two (a duet); boron and beryllium often have fewer than eight; \(P\), \(S\), \(Cl\) can have expanded octets in some compounds.

  • Cation — positively charged ion (lost electrons).
  • Anion — negatively charged ion (gained electrons).
  • Polyatomic ion — covalently bonded group carrying a net charge (e.g., \(\text{NO}_3^-\), \(\text{SO}_4^{2-}\), \(\text{NH}_4^+\)).
  • Lattice energy — energy released when gaseous ions form an ionic solid; larger for higher-charge, smaller-radius ions.
  • Bond polarity — depends on \(\Delta\text{EN}\); molecular polarity — depends on shape AND bond polarities.

Worked Example: Classify the bond type

Classify the bonds in (a) NaCl, (b) HF, (c) Br₂ using \(\Delta\text{EN}\). (Pauling values: Na 0.9, Cl 3.2; H 2.2, F 4.0; Br 3.0.)

(a) \(\Delta\text{EN} = 3.2 - 0.9 = 2.3 \gt 1.7\) → ionic. (b) \(\Delta\text{EN} = 4.0 - 2.2 = 1.8 \gt 1.7\) → borderline polar covalent / ionic, in practice treated as very polar covalent because HF is a molecular gas. (c) \(\Delta\text{EN} = 0\) → non-polar covalent.

Common mistakes:

  • Calling a metal-non-metal bond "covalent" — it is ionic by default.
  • Writing the chemical formula of \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\) as \(\text{AlO}\) or \(\text{Al}_3\text{O}_2\) — apply the crossover rule and simplify.
  • Assuming a molecule is polar simply because it contains polar bonds — geometry matters.
  • Forgetting that polyatomic ions are treated as a single unit and require parentheses if multiple are needed: \(\text{Mg(NO}_3\text{)}_2\).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.5 — "explain the formation of ionic and covalent bonds, including ionic compounds with polyatomic ions, and represent the formation of these bonds, using diagrams that show electron transfer or electron sharing."

🧊 Lewis Structures, VSEPR & Intermolecular Forces
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A Lewis (electron-dot) structure shows all valence electrons in a molecule, with bonding pairs drawn as lines (or dots between atoms) and non-bonding "lone" pairs drawn on individual atoms. Procedure: (1) count the total valence electrons (adjust for charge); (2) place the least electronegative atom in the centre (H is never central); (3) connect each atom with a single bond; (4) distribute remaining electrons as lone pairs to satisfy octets, starting with the outer atoms; (5) if the central atom is short of an octet, form double or triple bonds by moving lone pairs.

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory predicts molecular shape: valence-electron domains (bond pairs and lone pairs) around the central atom arrange themselves to minimize repulsion. Memorize the AXE notation, where A is the central atom, X = bonded atoms, E = lone pairs: AX₂ → linear (180°), AX₃ → trigonal planar (120°), AX₂E → bent (~117°), AX₄ → tetrahedral (109.5°), AX₃E → trigonal pyramidal (~107°), AX₂E₂ → bent (~104.5°). Lone pairs occupy more space than bond pairs, so they compress bond angles. Examples: \(\text{CO}_2\) linear, \(\text{SO}_2\) bent, \(\text{NH}_3\) pyramidal, \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) bent, \(\text{CH}_4\) tetrahedral.

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are the attractions between separate molecules and determine bulk properties such as boiling point, surface tension, and solubility. The three types covered in SCH3U, weakest to strongest, are: (1) London dispersion forces (LDFs) — transient dipoles arising in any molecule; strength grows with molar mass and surface area; the only IMF between non-polar molecules. (2) Dipole-dipole forces — between permanent dipoles in polar molecules. (3) Hydrogen bonding — a particularly strong dipole-dipole attraction occurring when H is bonded to N, O, or F and is attracted to a lone pair on N, O, or F on a neighbouring molecule. Hydrogen bonding accounts for water's high boiling point, ice being less dense than liquid water, and the cohesion that makes capillary action possible.

  • Lewis structure — diagram of valence electrons; obeys the octet rule for most main-group atoms.
  • VSEPR — predicts shape from valence-electron pair geometry.
  • Bond pair / lone pair — bonded electrons / non-bonding electrons.
  • LDF, dipole-dipole, H-bond — three IMFs (weakest → strongest).
  • "Like dissolves like" — polar dissolves polar; non-polar dissolves non-polar.

Worked Example: Lewis structure and VSEPR shape of \(\text{NH}_3\)

Valence electrons: N has 5, each H has 1 → total \(5 + 3 = 8\) electrons (4 pairs). Place N in centre, three N–H bonds use 6 electrons; one lone pair remains on N. AXE notation: AX₃E (three bond pairs + one lone pair). Geometry: tetrahedral electron pair arrangement; molecular shape trigonal pyramidal. Predicted bond angle: less than 109.5° (≈ 107°), because the lone pair compresses the H–N–H angles.

Common mistakes:

  • Forgetting to count lone pairs in the AXE notation — they affect shape even though they are invisible.
  • Writing five bonds on a second-row atom (B, C, N, O, F can have at most four electron domains).
  • Assuming \(\text{CO}_2\) is polar because C–O bonds are polar — symmetry cancels the dipoles, so \(\text{CO}_2\) is non-polar.
  • Confusing intramolecular bonds (within a molecule) with intermolecular forces (between molecules) — boiling involves breaking IMFs, not covalent bonds.
  • Drawing H-bonds between H and any electronegative atom — H must be bonded to N, O, or F for an H-bond.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.6 — "predict the polarity of various chemical bonds, using a table of electronegativities; describe and illustrate, using diagrams, the formation of and bonding in simple molecular substances (e.g., using Lewis structures), and predict the shape and polarity of simple molecules using VSEPR theory and the concept of bond dipoles."
