CHW3M — World History to the End of the Fifteenth Century, Grade 11 University/College

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 to 12: Canadian and World Studies (2015, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CHW3M  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: CHC2D or CHC2P (Grade 10 Canadian History since World War I)

1. Course Description

This course explores the history of various societies and civilizations from earliest times to around 1500 CE, with a focus on the political, cultural, social, economic, and intellectual dimensions of human experience. Students will investigate a range of social structures, beliefs, and ways of life, examining cross-cultural contacts as well as the development of governmental, religious, philosophical, scientific, and artistic traditions. They will use the concepts of historical thinking and the historical-inquiry process to investigate the past and to communicate their findings. The course prepares students for further studies in history, classics, anthropology, and the humanities at university or college.

2. Historical Thinking Concepts (Seixas, integrated throughout)

ConceptDefinition
Historical SignificanceDetermining which events, people, and developments matter, and to whom — significance is constructed, not given.
Cause and ConsequenceIdentifying multiple, layered causes (immediate, underlying, contextual) and short- vs long-term consequences (intended and unintended).
Continuity and ChangeRecognising that change is uneven, that some things persist while others transform, and that "progress" is not the same as "change."
Historical PerspectiveUnderstanding past people on their own terms, suspending present-day judgement long enough to enter their worldview (without abandoning critical analysis).
Ethical DimensionEngaging the moral judgements past actions invite, and acknowledging what we owe to past victims and the legacies we inherit.
Evidence (Primary Sources)Sourcing, contextualising, and corroborating documents, artefacts, oral traditions, images, and archaeological material.

3. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Historical Inquiry & Skill Development (A): The historical-inquiry process — formulating questions, gathering and analysing evidence, communicating findings — disciplines our understanding of the past.
  • Early Societies to 500 BCE (B): The transition from nomadic to settled life produced the first cities, states, writing systems, and organised religions; the river-valley civilizations were geographically distinct but addressed common human problems.
  • Classical Civilizations 500 BCE–500 CE (C): Greek, Roman, Han Chinese, and Mauryan/Gupta Indian societies developed lasting political, philosophical, and artistic traditions whose tensions (citizenship, empire, frontier) continue to shape modern states.
  • Medieval Period 500–1500 CE (D): After classical collapse, four broad civilizational orders — Latin Christian, Byzantine, Islamic, and Confucian East-Asian — each developed distinct responses to questions of authority, faith, knowledge, and exchange.
  • Interactions Among Civilizations (E): The pre-1500 world was richly connected — Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan caravans, Pacific voyaging — and the closing of these older networks by 1500 set conditions for the Columbian Exchange.

4. Strand A — Historical Inquiry and Skill Development (embedded across all units)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Historical Inquiry: use the historical-inquiry process and the concepts of historical thinking when investigating aspects of world history to the end of the fifteenth century;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills developed through historical investigation; identify careers in which a background in history might be an asset.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues and developments in world history;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations (e.g., authority of author, corroboration, bias, contextual fit);
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse information gathered, using a variety of tools (e.g., diagrams, maps, timelines, graphs);
  • A1.5 use the concepts of historical thinking (significance, cause and consequence, continuity and change, historical perspective) to investigate world history;
  • A1.7 communicate the results of their inquiries using appropriate vocabulary and formats (e.g., essay, debate, multimedia presentation, museum exhibit);
  • A2.1 describe ways in which historical investigation can help develop skills, including the essential skills in the Ontario Skills Passport (e.g., reading text, document use, writing, computer use, oral communication).
Inquiry threads: evaluating one ancient document and one secondary source; constructing a position essay using historical-thinking concepts; designing a culminating museum exhibit.

5. Strand B — Early Societies to 500 BCE

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe key social, economic, and political characteristics of various early societies (Mesopotamia, Egypt, Indus, Shang) and explain how they developed;
  • B2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse social and political structures and relationships within and between early societies;
  • B3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse aspects of the legacies of early societies (e.g., writing, law, monumental architecture, religion).

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 describe the geographic and environmental conditions that shaped the development of early settled societies;
  • B1.4 describe key economic features of early societies (e.g., subsistence agriculture, long-distance trade, taxation in kind);
  • B2.1 analyse the structure of social hierarchies (e.g., Mesopotamian kingship, Egyptian pharaonic state, the Vedic varna system);
  • B2.4 describe causes and consequences of significant conflicts (e.g., Akkadian conquest, Egypt vs Hyksos);
  • B3.2 describe lasting cultural and intellectual contributions of early societies (cuneiform, hieroglyphics, the Code of Hammurabi, the Rig Veda, oracle bones).
Anchor sources: excerpts from the Code of Hammurabi; the Narmer Palette; the Pyramid Texts; the Rig Veda; Shang oracle-bone inscriptions.

6. Strand C — Classical Civilizations 500 BCE–500 CE

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe characteristics of classical societies (Greece, Rome, Han China, Mauryan/Gupta India) and analyse how they developed;
  • C2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse internal and external conflicts, alliances, and influences during this period;
  • C3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse the legacy of classical civilizations and their continuing influence.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.2 describe key political institutions in classical civilizations (Athenian democracy, the Roman Republic and Empire, Confucian bureaucracy);
  • C1.5 describe characteristics of major belief systems (Greek philosophy, Roman religion, Confucianism, early Christianity, Hinduism, Buddhism);
  • C2.1 analyse the causes and consequences of conflicts within classical societies (Peloponnesian War, the Gracchi reforms, Han collapse);
  • C2.3 analyse the impact of contact with peoples beyond the empire (Hellenistic exchange, Roman frontier, the Silk Road);
  • C3.1 describe enduring legacies of classical civilizations (Roman law, Greek philosophy and science, Sanskrit literature, Confucian ethics).
Anchor sources: Thucydides' Funeral Oration of Pericles; Plato's Republic excerpt; Livy on the Gracchi; Tacitus on the Roman frontier; Sima Qian on the Qin emperor.

7. Strand D — Medieval Period 500–1500 CE

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe characteristics of various medieval societies (Latin Christian Europe, Byzantium, the Caliphates, Tang/Song China, Mongol Eurasia, sub-Saharan African states, Mesoamerica) and analyse how they developed;
  • D2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse internal and external interactions during the medieval period;
  • D3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse the legacies of medieval societies for the modern world.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 describe characteristic social, economic, and political features of feudalism in Europe and Japan;
  • D1.3 describe institutions of the Latin Church and Islamic ulama as social, intellectual, and economic actors;
  • D1.5 describe Tang and Song China's social hierarchy, civil service, and technological achievements (printing, gunpowder, compass);
  • D2.1 analyse the causes and consequences of significant medieval conflicts (Crusades, Mongol conquests, Reconquista);
  • D2.3 analyse the impact of the Black Death on Eurasian societies;
  • D3.2 describe enduring cultural, intellectual, and technological legacies of medieval societies (universities, Gothic architecture, Islamic algebra, Chinese printing).
Anchor sources: the Magna Carta (1215); Ibn Battuta on Mali; the Domesday Book; Mansa Musa's hajj account; a Mongol yam-system document; the Yongle Encyclopedia preface.

8. Strand E — Interactions Among Civilizations

Overall Expectations

  • E1. Significance of Interactions: analyse the historical significance of various forms of contact among societies before 1500 CE;
  • E2. Causes and Consequences of Contact: analyse the social, economic, political, and cultural impacts of intercultural exchange;
  • E3. Continuity and Change in Networks: analyse change and continuity in major networks of exchange (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan, Mesoamerican).

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 explain the significance of major networks of trade and contact (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, Saharan caravans);
  • E1.3 analyse the role of specific individuals as intermediaries (Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, Zheng He);
  • E2.1 analyse the impact of intercultural contact on belief systems and technology (e.g., the spread of Buddhism, of paper, of Indian numerals);
  • E2.4 analyse the conditions on the eve of 1492 that enabled European maritime expansion;
  • E3.2 analyse continuity and change in interregional trade networks across this period.
STSE focus: diseases as historical agents (the Black Death, the Columbian Exchange); maritime technologies (the dhow, the junk, the caravel); historiographical debate about "1492" as periodization.

11. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, source-analysis assignments, position essays, in-class debates, oral presentations.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a Culminating Inquiry Project (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

12. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, source-criticism checklists, peer-review on thesis statements).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (pre-unit diagnostics, draft-essay feedback).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, formal essays, inquiry projects, final exam).

13. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (timelines, maps, primary-source documents, audio readings, captioned video), multiple means of engagement (student-selected inquiry topic in Unit 5, choice of essay focus), and multiple means of expression (essay, podcast, mock museum exhibit, infographic) consistent with the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools.

14. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CHW3M for sustaining the multi-week culminating inquiry and for participation in primary-source seminar discussions.
