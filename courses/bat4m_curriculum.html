This course introduces students to the advanced principles of financial accounting. Students will develop financial-accounting practices and procedures for sole proprietorships, partnerships, and corporations. They will examine internal-control procedures and accounting for specific assets (cash, receivables, inventory, capital assets), and they will analyse and interpret financial statements using ratio analysis. The course also explores computerized accounting, ethical issues in the profession, and the changing role of accountants in a global, technology-driven economy. Students will be prepared to study business, accounting, or finance at the postsecondary level.

3. Strands & Expectations

Strand A — Specific Accounts

Overall Expectations: students will demonstrate an understanding of accounting practices and procedures for cash, accounts receivable, short-term investments, inventory, and capital assets; record and report financial transactions for specific accounts using appropriate generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP); and analyse the impact of accounting choices on the financial statements.

Key Specific Expectations

  • A1.1 Demonstrate an understanding of internal controls over cash, including segregation of duties, custody, recordkeeping, and authorization.
  • A1.2 Prepare a bank reconciliation statement, identifying outstanding cheques, deposits in transit, NSF cheques, and bank service charges.
  • A1.3 Demonstrate an understanding of the petty-cash system: establishing the fund, replenishing it, and recording shortages/overages.
  • A2.1 Account for accounts receivable using both the allowance method and the direct write-off method for bad debts.
  • A2.2 Estimate uncollectible accounts using percentage of sales and aging of receivables; compare the two methods.
  • A2.3 Record write-offs and subsequent recoveries of accounts previously written off.
  • A3.1 Account for short-term investments at fair value through profit or loss, including unrealized and realized gains and losses.
  • A4.1 Apply inventory cost-flow assumptions (specific identification, FIFO, weighted-average) under both periodic and perpetual systems.
  • A4.2 Apply the lower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV) rule and explain its conservatism rationale.
  • A4.3 Estimate inventory using the gross-profit method when a physical count is impractical.
  • A5.1 Determine the acquisition cost of capital assets (PP&E) including all costs to bring the asset to its intended use.
  • A5.2 Calculate amortization expense using straight-line, declining-balance, and units-of-production methods; explain why amortization is allocation, not valuation.
  • A5.3 Record disposals of capital assets including gain or loss on disposal and accumulated amortization removal.

Strand B — Tools & Procedures of Accounting

Overall Expectations: students will demonstrate an understanding of the use of specialized journals and subsidiary ledgers to record large volumes of similar transactions efficiently; demonstrate an understanding of accounting practices for partnerships and corporations; and apply payroll accounting procedures.

Key Specific Expectations

  • B1.1 Record transactions in the four specialized journals: sales journal, cash receipts journal, purchases journal, and cash payments journal.
  • B1.2 Maintain accounts-receivable and accounts-payable subsidiary ledgers; reconcile them to their general-ledger control accounts.
  • B1.3 Demonstrate an understanding of how specialized journals improve internal control through division of labour and audit trail.
  • B2.1 Differentiate between perpetual and periodic inventory systems, including the journal entries required under each.
  • B2.2 Compute and record gross profit, sales discounts, sales returns and allowances, and freight-in/freight-out.
  • B3.1 Record formation, division of profit/loss, admission of new partners, withdrawal of partners, and liquidation of a partnership.
  • B3.2 Record issuance of common and preferred shares for cash and for non-cash assets; explain the difference between authorized, issued, and outstanding shares.
  • B3.3 Record declaration and payment of cash dividends, stock dividends, and stock splits; prepare a statement of retained earnings.
  • B4.1 Compute gross pay, statutory deductions (CPP, EI, federal/provincial tax), and net pay; record payroll journal entries.
  • B4.2 Account for the employer's share of payroll (CPP, EI, WSIB, vacation pay, employer health tax) as a separate expense.

Strand C — Financial Analysis & Decision Making

Overall Expectations: students will analyse financial statements using horizontal, vertical, and ratio analysis; interpret the results in the context of decision-making by investors, creditors, and management; and demonstrate an understanding of the limitations of financial-statement analysis.

Key Specific Expectations

  • C1.1 Conduct horizontal analysis (year-over-year change, both dollar amount and percentage) on the income statement and balance sheet.
  • C1.2 Conduct vertical (common-size) analysis on the income statement (as % of net sales) and balance sheet (as % of total assets).
  • C2.1 Compute and interpret liquidity ratios: current ratio, quick (acid-test) ratio, working capital, receivables turnover, days' sales in receivables, inventory turnover, days' sales in inventory.
  • C2.2 Compute and interpret solvency ratios: debt-to-assets, debt-to-equity, times-interest-earned (interest coverage).
  • C2.3 Compute and interpret profitability ratios: gross-profit margin, profit margin, return on assets (ROA), return on equity (ROE), earnings per share (EPS).
  • C2.4 Compute and interpret market ratios: price-to-earnings (P/E), dividend yield, dividend payout.
  • C3.1 Make a recommendation for investing or lending based on a comparative ratio analysis (over time, against industry benchmarks, or between two companies).
  • C3.2 Identify limitations of ratio analysis (historical cost, accounting-policy differences, window dressing, industry differences, non-financial factors).

Strand D — Internal Control & the Audit Function

Overall Expectations: students will demonstrate an understanding of the objectives and components of internal control; design and evaluate internal-control procedures for cash, inventory, payroll, and information systems; and explain the role of internal and external auditors in maintaining financial integrity.

Key Specific Expectations

  • D1.1 State the five objectives of internal control: safeguarding assets, ensuring accurate records, promoting operational efficiency, encouraging adherence to policy, and ensuring reliable financial reporting.
  • D1.2 Describe the five components of the COSO framework (control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information & communication, monitoring).
  • D2.1 Apply segregation of duties: separating authorization, custody, recordkeeping, and reconciliation.
  • D2.2 Design specific internal-control procedures for cash receipts, cash payments, inventory, and payroll.
  • D3.1 Distinguish between internal auditors (employees who continuously evaluate controls) and external auditors (independent professionals who attest to the fairness of the financial statements).
  • D3.2 Explain the auditor's opinion: unqualified (clean), qualified, adverse, and disclaimer; and the role of the audit committee.
  • D4.1 Describe the fraud triangle (pressure, opportunity, rationalization) and identify red flags that indicate fraud or earnings management.
  • D4.2 Explain the role of legislation (CPA Canada Handbook, Bill 198 / CSOX, Sarbanes-Oxley Act) in promoting financial integrity.

Strand E — Ethics, Issues, & the Accounting Profession

Overall Expectations: students will demonstrate an understanding of the ethical responsibilities of the accounting professional and apply ethical-decision-making frameworks; describe the role of computerized accounting systems and the impact of emerging technologies; and demonstrate an understanding of the role of the accounting profession in domestic and international business.

Key Specific Expectations

  • E1.1 Apply the CPA Canada Code of Professional Conduct: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, and professional behaviour.
  • E1.2 Analyse an ethical dilemma using a structured framework (e.g., identify stakeholders, alternatives, consequences, decision).
  • E2.1 Describe the features of computerized accounting software (Sage 50, QuickBooks) — chart of accounts, integrated modules, audit trail, backup.
  • E2.2 Identify the controls required in a computerized environment: physical security, logical access, segregation of IT duties, change management.
  • E2.3 Compare GAAP, IFRS, and ASPE; explain why publicly accountable enterprises in Canada are required to use IFRS.
  • E3.1 Describe the changing role of the accountant in a global, digital economy: business advisor, data analyst, sustainability reporting, ESG.
  • E3.2 Describe the impact of emerging technologies on accounting: cloud computing, artificial intelligence, blockchain, and continuous auditing.
  • E3.3 Describe career paths in accounting (CPA designation, financial accounting, management accounting, tax, audit, consulting).

4. Big Ideas of Business Studies

The Ontario Business Studies curriculum is built around these enduring ideas, integrated throughout BAT4M:

Reliable InformationFinancial statements communicate the economic story of an enterprise; users (investors, lenders, regulators) depend on their reliability.
StewardshipAccountants are stewards of organizational resources; ethics and internal control protect those resources from loss and misuse.
Consistency & ComparabilityGAAP, IFRS, and ASPE provide a common language so that financial statements can be compared across firms and time periods.
Decision UsefulnessAccounting information is only valuable if it helps a user make a better decision — invest, lend, hire, expand, or sell.
Conservatism & MaterialityWhen in doubt, do not overstate assets or income; small misstatements that do not affect a user's decision may be acceptable.
Professional JudgmentMany accounting choices (amortization method, bad-debt rate, inventory cost flow) require professional judgment and disclosure of the assumption.
Technology & ChangeFrom paper journals to AI and blockchain, technology continually reshapes how accountants gather, process, and analyse information.

5. Achievement Chart

Per Growing Success (2010), each of the four categories carries equal weight (25%) within both term work and the final evaluation.

Knowledge & Understanding
25%

Subject-specific content (knowledge of accounting terminology, account classifications, GAAP) and comprehension of meaning and significance (understanding why a journal entry is made).

Thinking & Inquiry
25%

Use of critical and creative thinking — analysing financial statements, designing internal controls, resolving ethical dilemmas, evaluating accounting policy alternatives.

Communication
25%

Conveying meaning through various forms — preparing financial statements clearly, writing memos to management, explaining audit findings, using correct accounting terminology.

Application
25%

Use of knowledge and skills to make connections within and between contexts — applying methods to new businesses, integrating ratio analysis with strategic decisions, using software.

Levels of achievement: Level 4 (80–100%, exceeds standard) · Level 3 (70–79%, provincial standard) · Level 2 (60–69%, approaching) · Level 1 (50–59%, limited) · R (below 50%, insufficient).
