1.1 — Characteristics of Living Things & the Six-Kingdom System
What makes something alive, and how biologists organize the diversity of life into kingdoms and three domains.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Biology is the study of life, but life resists a single tidy definition. Biologists instead identify a cluster of shared properties: cellular organization, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, response to stimuli, reproduction, heredity, and the capacity to evolve as a population. Viruses challenge this list because they lack metabolism outside a host — most biologists therefore classify viruses as non-living, even though they evolve and contain nucleic acid.

To organize the millions of described species, taxonomists group organisms hierarchically. Modern classification recognizes three domains: Bacteria (single-celled, no nucleus, peptidoglycan walls), Archaea (single-celled, no nucleus, distinct lipids and unusual habitats — hot springs, salt flats, deep-sea vents), and Eukarya (cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles). Within Eukarya, four major kingdoms are studied at the Grade 11 level: Protista (mostly unicellular eukaryotes — Amoeba, Paramecium, algae), Fungi (heterotrophic decomposers with chitin walls — yeasts, moulds, mushrooms), Plantae (multicellular autotrophs with cellulose walls and chloroplasts), and Animalia (multicellular heterotrophs that ingest food and have no cell walls). Older textbooks split Protista into several kingdoms; the field is unsettled, and DNA evidence continues to revise the tree.

Notice the move from the older "five-kingdom" system (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) to the three-domain system. The key advance was Carl Woese's 1977 discovery that rRNA sequences split prokaryotes into two genetically distinct lineages — Bacteria and Archaea — that diverged billions of years ago, even though they look similar under a microscope. Domain Archaea is more closely related to Eukarya than to Bacteria in some respects.

  • Taxonomy — the science of naming, describing, and classifying organisms.
  • Prokaryote — organism whose cell lacks a true nucleus (Bacteria, Archaea).
  • Eukaryote — organism whose cells contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Autotroph / heterotroph — produces its own food (e.g., plants) / obtains food from other organisms.
  • Domain — the highest taxonomic rank (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya).
  • Kingdom — the second-highest rank (Plantae, Animalia, Fungi, Protista, plus the bacterial/archaeal groupings).

Worked example — Assigning an unknown organism

You isolate an organism from a Yellowstone hot spring at 90 °C. It is single-celled, lacks a nucleus, and its membrane lipids include ether linkages instead of the usual ester linkages. Which domain does it belong to, and why?

The organism is single-celled and lacks a nucleus, so it is a prokaryote (Bacteria or Archaea). The clinching evidence is the ether-linked lipids — a defining feature of archaeal membranes that lets them tolerate extreme temperatures. Combined with the hyperthermophilic habitat, the organism is best classified in Domain Archaea.

  • Saying viruses are alive — most biologists exclude viruses because they cannot metabolize without a host.
  • Confusing Archaea with Bacteria because both are prokaryotic — they are evolutionarily distinct (rRNA, lipids, gene-regulation machinery).
  • Listing Protista as monophyletic — current evidence shows it is a "grab bag" of several distinct eukaryotic lineages.
  • Treating "five kingdoms" as current — modern classification uses three domains, with kingdoms nested below.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "describe the anatomical and behavioural characteristics used to classify representative organisms in domains and kingdoms."

1.2 — Binomial Nomenclature & the Taxonomic Hierarchy
How Linnaeus's two-name system lets scientists worldwide refer to the same species without ambiguity.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) introduced binomial nomenclature in his 1753 Species Plantarum: every species receives a two-word Latin name — first the genus (capitalized), then the specific epithet (lowercase), both italicized when typed (or underlined when handwritten). Humans are Homo sapiens; the white pine is Pinus strobus; the timber rattlesnake is Crotalus horridus. The genus name groups closely related species (Homo sapiens with extinct Homo neanderthalensis), while the specific epithet uniquely identifies a species within its genus. Once a genus name has been used in a passage, it can be abbreviated to its first letter (H. sapiens). Common names vary by region and language ("buzzard" in Britain refers to a hawk; in North America it refers to a vulture) — only the binomial name is unambiguous.

Names sit inside a nested taxonomic hierarchy: Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. (A common mnemonic: "Do Kings Play Chess On Fine Green Sand?") Each level is called a taxon. Higher taxa group more organisms with broader characteristics; lower taxa are more specific. For humans the full lineage is Eukarya → Animalia → Chordata → Mammalia → Primates → Hominidae → Homo → Homo sapiens. For domestic dogs: Eukarya → Animalia → Chordata → Mammalia → Carnivora → Canidae → Canis → Canis lupus familiaris (a subspecies of grey wolf).

The taxonomy of a species can shift as new evidence accumulates. Pandas were debated for decades — were they bears or raccoons? — and were resolved as bears (family Ursidae) only after DNA analysis. The species concept itself has more than one definition. The most common is the biological species concept: a species is a population whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring in nature. This concept breaks down for asexual or fossil organisms, where biologists rely instead on morphological or phylogenetic species concepts.

  • Binomial nomenclature — Linnaeus's two-word naming system (Genus + species).
  • Taxon (plural taxa) — any level in the taxonomic hierarchy.
  • Biological species concept — a group of organisms that interbreeds in nature to produce fertile offspring.
  • Genus — a group of structurally and evolutionarily related species.
  • Specific epithet — the second word in a scientific name, identifying a species within a genus.
  • Subspecies — a recognized population within a species, often with a third name (e.g., Canis lupus familiaris).

Worked example — Reading a scientific name

Two organisms have the scientific names Felis catus (domestic cat) and Felis silvestris (European wildcat). What does the shared word tell you about their relationship, and what does the differing word tell you?

Both species belong to the genus Felis, so they are closely related and share a recent common ancestor — they have similar skeletons, fur patterns, and behaviour. The differing specific epithets (catus vs silvestris) indicate that they are different species; under the biological species concept, they typically do not freely interbreed in the wild to produce fertile offspring (although hybridisation is documented in some regions and is a conservation concern).

  • Capitalising the specific epithet — only the genus is capitalised.
  • Forgetting to italicise (or underline) the binomial — formatting matters in scientific writing.
  • Confusing genus and species levels — every species belongs to exactly one genus, but a genus contains many species.
  • Using common names in lab reports — always include the binomial at first mention.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "describe the binomial nomenclature system and the Linnaean taxonomic hierarchy (domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species)."

1.3 — Phylogeny & Cladograms
Reconstructing evolutionary trees from shared derived traits — what a branch order really means.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Modern taxonomy is grounded in phylogeny — the evolutionary history of a group. A phylogenetic tree (or cladogram) is a diagram in which each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor and each terminal tip represents a present-day group. Branches that share a recent node are more closely related than branches that meet at a deeper node. The tree is reconstructed using shared derived characters (also called synapomorphies) — features that evolved in a common ancestor and are inherited by its descendants. Examples: the four-chambered heart shared by mammals and crocodiles; the amniotic egg shared by reptiles, birds, and mammals; the presence of feathers shared by all birds (and some non-avian dinosaurs).

A valid taxonomic group should be monophyletic — that is, it must include a common ancestor and all of its descendants. "Reptilia" in the traditional sense is paraphyletic: it excludes birds, even though birds evolved from within the dinosaur lineage. Modern systematics resolves this by either redefining Reptilia to include birds (a clade also called Sauropsida) or by replacing it with the clade Archosauria for crocodiles + birds. A polyphyletic group is one that lumps together unrelated organisms based on convergent traits (e.g., "warm-blooded animals" putting mammals and birds together without their full common ancestry).

Until the 1980s, trees were built mainly from morphology. Today, molecular phylogenetics uses DNA, RNA, and protein sequences. The more two species' sequences differ, the longer they have been evolving separately — a quantitative molecular clock. Combining anatomy, fossils, embryology, and molecular data gives the most reliable trees, but tree topology can change as new evidence arrives.

  • Phylogeny — the evolutionary history of a species or group.
  • Cladogram — branching diagram showing inferred evolutionary relationships.
  • Node — a branch point representing a hypothetical common ancestor.
  • Clade — an ancestor and all of its descendants (a monophyletic group).
  • Synapomorphy — a shared derived character used to define a clade.
  • Outgroup — a more distantly related taxon used to root and orient a cladogram.

Worked example — Interpreting a cladogram

On a cladogram, species A and B share a node that lies above the node connecting that pair to species C. Species C joins the tree at a node that lies above the node connecting that group to species D. Which two species share the most recent common ancestor?

The most recent (closest to the present) shared node is the one between A and B, so A and B share the most recent common ancestor. C joins them next, and D is the outgroup. Note that proximity at the tips of the tree means nothing — only the depth of the shared node matters.

  • Reading "closeness" on a tree from horizontal distance — only node depth (vertical) reflects evolutionary distance.
  • Calling a group monophyletic when it excludes some descendants (this is paraphyletic).
  • Treating any shared similarity as a synapomorphy — only shared derived traits (not ancestral ones) define clades.
  • Reading more evolution into branch lengths than the diagram supports (some cladograms have no scaled lengths).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.3 — "describe the principle of phylogeny and explain how molecular data refine biological classification."

1.4 — Survey of Invertebrate & Vertebrate Diversity
A tour of the major animal phyla and the chordate vertebrate classes.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Kingdom Animalia contains roughly 35 phyla, but six dominate biology curricula. Porifera (sponges) are asymmetrical, lack tissues, and filter-feed through specialized cells called choanocytes. Cnidaria (jellyfish, corals, sea anemones, hydras) have radial symmetry, two tissue layers, and stinging cells called nematocysts. Platyhelminthes (flatworms — planaria, flukes, tapeworms) are the simplest bilateral animals with three tissue layers. Mollusca (snails, clams, octopuses, squids) are unsegmented coelomates with a muscular foot, mantle, and (often) shell. Annelida (segmented worms — earthworms, leeches) show true segmentation. Arthropoda — by far the largest phylum (insects, crustaceans, arachnids, myriapods) — has a hard chitin exoskeleton, jointed appendages, and segmented body. Echinodermata (sea stars, sea urchins) have radial symmetry as adults, a water-vascular system, and tube feet. Chordata includes all vertebrates plus tunicates and lancelets; at some life stage chordates show four traits: a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, and a post-anal tail.

Within Chordata, the seven vertebrate classes capture the rest of the curriculum: jawless fish (Agnatha — lampreys), cartilaginous fish (Chondrichthyes — sharks, rays), bony fish (Osteichthyes — salmon, perch), amphibians (Amphibia — frogs, salamanders), reptiles (Reptilia — snakes, lizards, turtles, crocodiles), birds (Aves), and mammals (Mammalia). Mammals are diagnosed by mammary glands, hair, three middle-ear bones, and a four-chambered heart. Reptiles, birds, and mammals together form the amniotes — animals whose embryo develops inside protective membranes (amnion, chorion, allantois) that freed them from water for reproduction.

Body-plan features cut across phyla: symmetry (asymmetric, radial, bilateral), germ layers (diploblastic vs triploblastic), body cavity (acoelomate, pseudocoelomate, coelomate), and segmentation. Bilateral symmetry with cephalization (concentrated nervous tissue in the head) is the foundation for active, directional locomotion in most successful animal phyla.

  • Bilateral symmetry — left and right sides are mirror images; allows cephalization and directed movement.
  • Coelom — a true body cavity completely lined by mesoderm-derived tissue.
  • Notochord — a flexible rod that defines Chordata; in vertebrates it is replaced by the vertebral column.
  • Amniote — a vertebrate (reptile, bird, mammal) whose embryo has extra-embryonic membranes (amnion).
  • Exoskeleton — external skeleton (e.g., insect chitin) shed during moulting (ecdysis).
  • Endotherm / ectotherm — generates own body heat (mammals, birds) / depends on external heat sources (fish, reptiles, amphibians, invertebrates).

Worked example — Classifying an unknown animal

You collect an organism on a coral reef. It is radially symmetric as an adult, has tube feet, and a hard internal endoskeleton made of calcium-carbonate plates. To which phylum does it belong, and what are two other diagnostic traits?

Radial adult symmetry + tube feet + calcium-carbonate endoskeleton points to phylum Echinodermata (sea stars, sea urchins, brittle stars, sea cucumbers). Two further diagnostic traits: a water-vascular system that operates the tube feet hydraulically, and bilateral symmetry in the larval stage (radial symmetry is secondary). All echinoderms are exclusively marine.

  • Forgetting that adult radial symmetry in echinoderms is secondary — they hatch as bilateral larvae.
  • Putting whales or dolphins into "Fish" — they are mammals (Mammalia).
  • Listing tunicates outside Chordata — adult sea squirts look unlike vertebrates, but their larvae have the four chordate traits.
  • Calling a centipede an insect — centipedes belong to subphylum Myriapoda; insects (Insecta) have three body regions and six legs.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.5 — "explain the major characteristics of representative vertebrate and invertebrate phyla."

1.5 — Biodiversity & Its Measurement
Genetic, species, and ecosystem biodiversity, and the indices used to compare communities.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Biodiversity has three nested meanings: genetic diversity (variation within a single species — alleles, gene combinations), species diversity (number and relative abundance of species in a community), and ecosystem diversity (variety of habitats and the processes that link them). A coral reef and a Canadian boreal forest both show high biodiversity, but the kinds of biodiversity differ — the reef has spectacular species richness while the boreal forest has high genetic diversity within a small number of dominant species (spruce, fir, birch, lichen).

Two simple measurements summarize species diversity. Species richness counts the number of species present (S). Species evenness measures how equally individuals are distributed among species — a community of 100 fish split 25/25/25/25 across four species is more even than one split 97/1/1/1. The most common combined index is Simpson's diversity index: \(D = 1 - \sum p_i^2\), where \(p_i\) is the proportion of individuals (or biomass) belonging to species \(i\). \(D\) approaches 0 in a monoculture and approaches 1 when many species are equally abundant.

Humans depend on biodiversity for food (most calories come from a handful of grains), medicine (aspirin from willow bark; the cancer drug Taxol from yew bark), ecosystem services (pollination, water filtration, nutrient cycling), and cultural and recreational value. The current rate of species extinction is 100–1000 times the background rate seen in the fossil record. Major drivers — habitat loss, invasive species, pollution, overharvesting, and climate change — are often summarized by the acronym HIPPO. Ontario's mixed-wood forests, prairie grasslands, and Great Lakes are all under pressure; protected areas (national and provincial parks), the Species at Risk Act, and Indigenous-led conservation are key responses.

  • Genetic diversity — variation in alleles within a species.
  • Species richness — the number of different species in a community (S).
  • Species evenness — the relative abundance of each species — how equally individuals are spread.
  • Simpson's diversity index (\(D\)) — \(D = 1 - \sum p_i^2\); ranges 0 (no diversity) to ~1 (high diversity).
  • Endemic species — found in only one place (e.g., lemurs in Madagascar).
  • Keystone species — a species whose impact on the community is disproportionately large for its biomass (e.g., sea otters, beavers, Pacific salmon).

Worked example — Calculating Simpson's D

A 50 m² plot contains 100 individuals: 60 red oak, 25 sugar maple, 10 white pine, 5 black cherry. Calculate Simpson's diversity index.

Proportions: red oak \(p = 0.60\), maple \(p = 0.25\), pine \(p = 0.10\), cherry \(p = 0.05\). \(\sum p_i^2 = 0.36 + 0.0625 + 0.01 + 0.0025 = 0.435\). \(D = 1 - 0.435 = 0.565\). For comparison, a four-species community with equal proportions (0.25 each) would give \(\sum p_i^2 = 4(0.0625) = 0.25\) and \(D = 0.75\) — more even, hence more diverse.

  • Confusing species richness (count) with biodiversity (a multi-dimensional concept including evenness and genetics).
  • Forgetting the "1 −" in Simpson's index — Simpson's D as defined here goes up with diversity; the bare \(\sum p_i^2\) goes down.
  • Equating "invasive" with "non-native" — only some non-natives are invasive (cause harm); many cultivated plants are non-invasive.
  • Assuming the highest biodiversity is always in the tropics — temperate wetlands, coral reefs, and mountain belts are also extraordinarily diverse.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "conduct an investigation into the diversity of organisms in a local ecosystem, using sampling techniques and an appropriate biodiversity index."

1.6 — Threats to Biodiversity & Conservation in Canada
Human activities that erode biodiversity, and the response strategies used in Ontario and Canada.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The geological record shows five mass extinctions, the most recent ending the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago. Many biologists describe the current biodiversity crisis as a "sixth extinction" driven by humans. Five forces (the HIPPO acronym popularized by E. O. Wilson) dominate: Habitat loss (deforestation, wetland drainage, urban sprawl), Invasive species (zebra mussels, emerald ash borer, garlic mustard in Ontario), Pollution (nutrient run-off, plastics, pesticides like DDT and neonicotinoids), Population (human population near 8 billion magnifies all the other forces), and Overharvesting (Atlantic cod collapse, illegal wildlife trade). Climate change is sometimes added explicitly to the list (HIPPCO) because it interacts with — and accelerates — every other driver.

The flagship Canadian responses are the Species at Risk Act (SARA, 2002), which protects federally listed species and their critical habitat, and the Ontario Endangered Species Act (2007). The Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada (COSEWIC) assesses species on a scale: extinct, extirpated (gone from Canada but persists elsewhere), endangered, threatened, special concern. Protected areas — national parks (Algonquin's adjoining Algonquin Provincial Park, Pukaskwa, Bruce Peninsula), Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas, marine protected areas — provide the strongest legal protection. Ex-situ conservation in zoos, botanical gardens, and seed banks (the Svalbard Global Seed Vault) preserves genetic material as a back-up.

Sustainable use and traditional knowledge are central to Canadian conservation. Many Indigenous Nations have stewarded biodiversity for thousands of years; modern co-management of fisheries, forestry, and parks integrates Indigenous knowledge with Western science. Individual actions also matter: reducing meat consumption, planting native species, avoiding single-use plastics, and supporting certified sustainable products (FSC for wood, MSC for fish) all reduce the ecological footprint.

  • HIPPO — Habitat loss, Invasive species, Pollution, Population, Overharvesting.
  • Endangered species — a species facing imminent extinction or extirpation.
  • Extirpation — loss of a species from a region (it may persist elsewhere).
  • In-situ vs ex-situ conservation — protecting species in their natural habitat vs in zoos, botanical gardens, gene banks.
  • Bioaccumulation — accumulation of persistent toxins in an organism over its lifetime.
  • Biomagnification — increase in toxin concentration up the food chain (DDT in fish-eating birds).

Worked example — Diagnosing a population crash

In the 1960s populations of bald eagles, peregrine falcons, and ospreys all crashed across North America. Female birds laid eggs with thin shells that broke under the parents' weight. What ecological mechanism caused this, and what conservation action reversed it?

The cause was biomagnification of the pesticide DDT. DDT applied to crops washed into waterways and entered phytoplankton, then fish, then fish-eating birds at successively higher concentrations. The metabolite DDE disrupted calcium deposition in eggshells. Banning DDT in Canada (1972) and the United States, plus captive breeding and re-introduction programs, allowed populations to rebound dramatically — peregrines were removed from Ontario's endangered list in the 2000s.

  • Treating biodiversity loss as a "future" problem — extinction rates are already orders of magnitude above background.
  • Equating "exotic" with "harmful" — many exotic species are not invasive.
  • Confusing extinction (everywhere) with extirpation (from a region).
  • Assuming a single protected area is enough — fragmented habitats need corridors to maintain gene flow.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "analyse the effects of various human activities (e.g., habitat destruction, introduction of invasive species, overharvesting) on the diversity of living things, with reference to Canadian ecosystems."
