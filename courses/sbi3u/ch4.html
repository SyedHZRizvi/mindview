How organs and organ systems cooperate to keep an animal alive

4.1 — Levels of Organization & Body-System Integration
From cells to tissues to organs to systems; the body as an integrated network maintaining homeostasis.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Multicellular animals are organised into a hierarchy. Cells are the basic functional units. Cells of similar structure and function form tissues; in animals, four major tissue types are recognised. Epithelial tissue covers body surfaces and lines cavities (skin epidermis, gut lining, alveolar lining); it forms protective barriers and is the site of absorption and secretion. Connective tissue supports and binds other tissues — bone, cartilage, blood, adipose, and tendons all belong here, characterised by cells embedded in an extracellular matrix. Muscle tissue generates movement: skeletal (voluntary, striated, multinucleated), cardiac (involuntary, striated, branched, with intercalated discs), and smooth (involuntary, non-striated, walls of hollow organs). Nervous tissue transmits electrical signals — neurons plus their support cells (glia).

Tissues combine into organs, and organs working together form organ systems. The eleven human organ systems are integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular (circulatory), lymphatic/immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary (excretory), and reproductive. Grade 11 focuses on five: digestive, circulatory, respiratory, excretory, and nervous. The systems are not independent; oxygen absorbed in the lungs reaches every cell because the cardiovascular system distributes it, and waste \(CO_2\) reaches the lungs because of the same network. Hormones from the endocrine system regulate processes in the digestive, circulatory, urinary, and reproductive systems. The nervous system coordinates rapid responses across all systems.

The integrated function maintains homeostasis — the dynamic regulation of internal conditions (body temperature, blood pH, blood glucose, blood pressure, water and electrolyte balance). Homeostasis usually relies on negative feedback: a change in a variable triggers a response that opposes the change. When blood glucose rises after a meal, pancreatic β-cells release insulin → cells take up glucose → blood glucose falls back to ~5 mmol/L. When body temperature rises, sweating and vasodilation increase heat loss until temperature returns to ~37 °C. Disease can be understood as failure of homeostasis: diabetes (failed glucose regulation), heart failure (failed circulation), kidney disease (failed waste clearance).

  • Tissue — group of similar cells that share a common function.
  • Organ — structure composed of two or more tissue types working together.
  • Organ system — group of organs that cooperate to perform a major body function.
  • Homeostasis — maintenance of stable internal conditions.
  • Negative feedback — regulatory response that opposes a change.
  • Positive feedback — regulatory response that amplifies a change (childbirth contractions, blood clotting).

Worked example — Tracing a negative-feedback loop

Outline the negative-feedback loop that returns body temperature to 37 °C after exposure to a hot environment. Identify the sensor, integrator, effector(s), and response.

Sensor: temperature-sensitive neurons in the skin and in the hypothalamus detect rising body temperature. Integrator: the hypothalamus compares incoming signals with the set point (~37 °C). Effectors: sweat glands (via cholinergic sympathetic nerves) and blood vessels in the skin. Response: sweat glands release sweat → evaporative cooling; vasodilation of skin vessels increases heat loss to the environment. As body temperature returns to 37 °C, the sensor's signal weakens and the response is reduced — a classic negative-feedback loop.

  • Treating the systems as independent — they are deeply interconnected.
  • Confusing negative feedback (opposes change) with positive feedback (amplifies change).
  • Forgetting that homeostasis is dynamic — internal conditions oscillate within a narrow range.
  • Equating "homeostasis" with "no change" — homeostasis maintains balance, not stasis.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.1 — "explain how internal systems work together to maintain homeostasis, using the four tissue types and the eleven organ systems as a framework."

4.2 — The Digestive System
A long tube optimised for breaking down food and absorbing nutrients — mouth to anus, plus accessory organs.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Animals must take in complex food molecules and reduce them to absorbable units — sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol, vitamins, minerals. The human digestive system is a long tube (about 8 m end-to-end) with specialised regions plus three accessory organs — salivary glands, liver, and pancreas — that secrete enzymes and other fluids without food passing through them.

The journey starts in the mouth, where teeth mechanically grind food and salivary glands secrete saliva containing amylase (which begins starch digestion) and lubricating mucus. The tongue forms food into a bolus that is swallowed through the pharynx. The oesophagus conveys the bolus by waves of smooth-muscle contraction (peristalsis) into the stomach. There, gastric glands secrete hydrochloric acid (pH ~2), the enzyme precursor pepsinogen (activated to pepsin by the acid; begins protein digestion), and protective mucus. The semi-liquid chyme leaves the stomach through the pyloric sphincter.

The small intestine (~6 m: duodenum, jejunum, ileum) is where most digestion and almost all absorption occurs. The duodenum receives bicarbonate-rich pancreatic juice (which neutralises acid), pancreatic enzymes (trypsin, chymotrypsin for proteins; amylase for starches; lipase for fats), and bile produced by the liver and stored in the gall bladder (bile emulsifies fats, increasing the surface area for lipase to act). The small intestinal wall has a vast surface area thanks to circular folds, villi, and microvilli — perhaps 250 m² of absorptive surface. Sugars and amino acids cross into capillaries; fatty acids and glycerol assemble into chylomicrons and enter lymphatic lacteals. Absorbed nutrients reach the liver via the hepatic portal vein for processing.

The large intestine (colon, ~1.5 m) absorbs water and electrolytes from the remaining material, compacting waste into feces. Resident gut bacteria (microbiome) ferment indigestible fibre, producing short-chain fatty acids and vitamin K. The rectum stores feces until elimination through the anus. Diet-related disease (ulcers, lactose intolerance, celiac disease, colon cancer) reflects the diversity of this system's tasks.

  • Peristalsis — wavelike contractions of smooth muscle that propel food along the digestive tract.
  • Bolus / chyme — chewed food ball in mouth/oesophagus / semi-liquid mixture in the stomach.
  • Bile — alkaline liver secretion stored in gall bladder; emulsifies fats.
  • Villi / microvilli — finger-like projections / microscopic folds that amplify intestinal surface area.
  • Hepatic portal vein — vein carrying absorbed nutrients from intestine to liver for first-pass processing.
  • Sphincter — ring of muscle that regulates flow at junctions (cardiac, pyloric, ileocaecal, anal).

Worked example — Predicting the effect of losing gall bladder function

A patient has their gall bladder surgically removed because of gallstones. What dietary change would you recommend, and why?

The gall bladder stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver. Without it, bile drips continuously from the liver into the small intestine instead of being released in concentrated pulses after fatty meals. The patient should reduce the size of fatty meals and spread fat intake throughout the day. Otherwise, large boluses of fat are inadequately emulsified, lipase cannot work efficiently, and the patient experiences diarrhoea, bloating, and reduced absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

  • Calling stomach acid the main "digestive enzyme" — HCl provides the acidic pH that activates pepsin; pepsin is the protein-digesting enzyme.
  • Saying absorption happens primarily in the stomach — most absorption occurs in the small intestine.
  • Forgetting bile is not an enzyme — bile emulsifies fats but does not chemically digest them.
  • Treating the liver and pancreas as part of the digestive tube — they are accessory organs whose secretions enter the duodenum.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.1 — "describe the structure and function of the major organs of the human digestive system and identify the role of accessory glands (liver, pancreas, gall bladder)."

4.3 — The Circulatory System
Heart, blood vessels, blood components, and the two circuits that deliver nutrients and remove wastes.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The human cardiovascular system is a closed double-loop circuit driven by a four-chambered heart. Deoxygenated blood returns from body tissues through the venae cavae into the right atrium, passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, and is pumped through the pulmonary semilunar valve into the pulmonary trunk to the lungs — the pulmonary circuit. There, blood gives up \(CO_2\) and picks up \(O_2\), then returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, through the mitral (bicuspid) valve into the left ventricle, and is pumped through the aortic valve into the aorta — the systemic circuit. The left ventricle has thicker walls than the right because it pumps against the higher resistance of the entire body, not just the lungs.

Blood travels through three classes of vessels. Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure; they have thick, elastic, muscular walls that absorb the pulse from each heartbeat. Capillaries are one-cell-thick microscopic vessels where gas, nutrient, and waste exchange occurs by diffusion between blood and surrounding tissue fluid. Veins return blood to the heart under low pressure; they have thinner walls and one-way valves that prevent backflow as blood moves against gravity in upright postures. Skeletal-muscle contractions during exercise help push venous blood toward the heart (the "muscle pump").

Blood itself is a connective tissue, about 55% plasma (water with dissolved proteins, electrolytes, hormones, gases, wastes) and 45% formed elements: red blood cells (erythrocytes — biconcave discs without nuclei, packed with haemoglobin to carry \(O_2\) and some \(CO_2\)), white blood cells (leukocytes — neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils — immune defence), and platelets (cell fragments essential for clotting). Each heartbeat begins with electrical depolarisation of the sinoatrial (SA) node in the right atrium, propagates through both atria, reaches the atrioventricular (AV) node, and travels down the bundle of His and Purkinje fibres to trigger ventricular contraction. The lymphatic system works alongside the circulatory system — lymphatic capillaries collect interstitial fluid that has leaked from blood capillaries and return it (as lymph) to the venous system, while also serving an immune-surveillance role.

  • Atria / ventricles — upper receiving chambers / lower pumping chambers of the heart.
  • Pulmonary / systemic circuit — loop to the lungs / loop to the rest of the body.
  • Artery / vein / capillary — carries blood away from the heart / back to the heart / site of exchange.
  • SA node — the heart's natural pacemaker located in the right atrium.
  • Haemoglobin — iron-containing protein in red blood cells that binds and releases \(O_2\).
  • Systole / diastole — contraction / relaxation phase of a heartbeat (BP reported as systolic/diastolic).

Worked example — Tracing the path of a blood cell

Trace the journey of an oxygenated red blood cell from the left atrium to a capillary in the brain and back to the right atrium. Name the major vessels and chambers.

Left atrium → mitral (bicuspid) valve → left ventricle → aortic semilunar valve → aorta → carotid artery → smaller cerebral arteries → arterioles → capillary in the brain (gas exchange occurs here). Then capillary → venule → cerebral vein → jugular vein → superior vena cava → right atrium. Note that the cell has passed through the heart only once in this loop; it must still travel through the right ventricle and pulmonary circuit before being pumped systemically again.

  • Saying arteries always carry oxygenated blood — the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
  • Forgetting valves prevent backflow — incompetent (leaky) valves cause heart murmurs and reduced cardiac output.
  • Confusing the SA node (pacemaker) with the AV node (relay station).
  • Saying red blood cells have nuclei — mature mammalian RBCs are anucleate, which makes more room for haemoglobin.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.2 — "describe the structure and function of the human circulatory system, including the heart, blood vessels, blood components, and the pulmonary and systemic circuits."

4.4 — The Respiratory System
Gas exchange between air and blood — from nose to alveoli, breathing mechanics, and \(O_2\)/\(CO_2\) transport.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The respiratory system delivers oxygen to the blood and removes carbon dioxide. Air enters through the nasal cavity, where it is warmed, humidified, and filtered by hairs and mucus. It passes through the pharynx, then the larynx (housing the vocal cords), where the epiglottis diverts swallowed material toward the oesophagus. The air continues down the trachea — reinforced by C-shaped cartilage rings — into the right and left primary bronchi, which branch repeatedly into smaller bronchioles and end in clusters of microscopic alveoli. Each lung contains roughly 300 million alveoli, providing a gas-exchange surface of about 70 m² — comparable to a tennis court.

Gas exchange occurs at the alveolar–capillary interface. The alveolar wall is one cell thick (Type I pneumocytes) and is wrapped by an equally thin pulmonary capillary, so \(O_2\) and \(CO_2\) diffuse across only about 0.5 µm of tissue down their partial-pressure gradients. \(O_2\) moves from alveolar air (~104 mmHg) into pulmonary capillary blood (~40 mmHg), while \(CO_2\) moves from blood (~46 mmHg) into alveolar air (~40 mmHg). Type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant, a phospholipid mixture that reduces surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse during exhalation. Premature infants whose lungs have not yet produced surfactant suffer infant respiratory distress syndrome — treated by exogenous surfactant therapy.

Breathing is driven by changes in thoracic volume. During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and flattens while the external intercostal muscles lift the rib cage — both increase thoracic volume, decrease intrathoracic pressure below atmospheric, and air flows in. Expiration at rest is largely passive: the diaphragm and intercostals relax, the elastic recoil of lungs and chest reduces thoracic volume, and air is pushed out. Breathing rate and depth are controlled by the medulla oblongata, which monitors blood \(CO_2\) (and pH); rising \(CO_2\) is the strongest stimulus to breathe, not falling \(O_2\). Oxygen is carried in blood almost entirely by haemoglobin (~98%); only ~2% is dissolved in plasma. Carbon dioxide is transported mostly as bicarbonate ions (\(HCO_3^-\)) formed in red blood cells by carbonic anhydrase.

  • Alveolus — microscopic air sac where gas exchange occurs.
  • Surfactant — lipoprotein mixture that reduces alveolar surface tension.
  • Diaphragm — dome-shaped skeletal muscle below the lungs; principal muscle of inspiration.
  • Pleura — double-layered membrane surrounding the lungs; pleural fluid lubricates breathing.
  • Tidal volume — air moved in/out per normal breath (~500 mL).
  • Vital capacity — maximum exhaled volume after maximal inspiration; falls with age and lung disease.

Worked example — Explaining the breathing response to exercise

During exercise, breathing rate increases. Identify the primary blood-gas signal that drives this response and the location of the sensing/control centre. Why is increased breathing important during exercise?

Skeletal muscles consume more \(O_2\) and produce more \(CO_2\); blood \(CO_2\) rises and forms more \(H_2CO_3\), lowering blood pH. Central chemoreceptors in the medulla oblongata detect the pH decrease, while peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies detect rising \(CO_2\) and falling \(O_2\). The medulla increases the rate and depth of breathing, exhaling more \(CO_2\) and supplying more \(O_2\). This restores blood pH and oxygen delivery to working muscles. Note: in healthy people, \(CO_2\)/pH is the dominant signal; \(O_2\) only becomes important at high altitude or in lung disease.

  • Saying inspiration "sucks" air in — air flows because of a pressure difference; the active step is muscle contraction.
  • Treating \(O_2\) as the main breathing stimulus — \(CO_2\) (and pH) is the primary stimulus in healthy people.
  • Calling alveoli "thick" — they are about 0.5 µm thick, perfectly suited for diffusion.
  • Saying haemoglobin "stores" \(O_2\) — haemoglobin transports \(O_2\); it releases the gas where partial pressure is low (active tissues).

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.3 — "describe the structure and function of the human respiratory system and the mechanism of gas exchange at the alveolar-capillary interface."

4.5 — The Excretory System & the Nephron
Kidneys, nephron anatomy, urine formation, and the regulation of blood water and ion balance.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The excretory (urinary) system removes metabolic wastes — chiefly urea from protein catabolism — and regulates blood water, ion concentrations, and pH. The main organs are the two kidneys, each receiving about 1.2 L of blood per minute via the renal arteries (a quarter of cardiac output). Inside each kidney, the cortex (outer) and medulla (inner) contain roughly one million functional units called nephrons, where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Urine drains through the ureters into the urinary bladder for storage and is expelled through the urethra.

A nephron has two main parts: a renal corpuscle (Bowman's capsule wrapped around a tuft of capillaries called the glomerulus) and a long tubule (proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct). Urine formation involves three steps. (1) Filtration: high pressure in the glomerular capillaries forces water and small solutes (ions, glucose, amino acids, urea) into Bowman's capsule, while blood cells and large proteins remain in the capillary. The filtrate volume is ~180 L per day — most of which must be reclaimed. (2) Reabsorption: cells lining the proximal tubule actively pump glucose, amino acids, and key ions back into the blood; water follows osmotically. Further reabsorption in the loop of Henle (which creates a concentration gradient using a counter-current system) and the distal tubule fine-tunes the urine. (3) Secretion: cells of the tubule actively transport specific substances (H⁺, K⁺, drugs, toxins) from the blood into the filtrate.

Two hormones tune the final composition. ADH (antidiuretic hormone), released by the posterior pituitary when blood is too concentrated, increases water permeability of the collecting duct so more water is reabsorbed and a small volume of concentrated urine is produced. Aldosterone, from the adrenal cortex, increases Na⁺ (and water) reabsorption in the distal tubule, raising blood pressure when needed. Failure of these systems — dehydration, diabetes insipidus (ADH deficiency), heart failure, kidney disease — disrupts homeostasis dramatically. Severe kidney failure is treated by dialysis (artificial blood filtration) or transplant.

  • Nephron — functional filtering unit of the kidney; ~1 million per kidney.
  • Glomerulus / Bowman's capsule — capillary tuft / cup-like structure forming the filtration unit.
  • Filtration / reabsorption / secretion — three steps of urine formation.
  • Loop of Henle — descending and ascending limbs that create a counter-current concentration gradient enabling water reclamation.
  • ADH — antidiuretic hormone; increases water reabsorption in the collecting duct.
  • Aldosterone — adrenal hormone; increases Na⁺ and water reabsorption to raise blood pressure.

Worked example — Predicting urine output after drinking water

A student drinks 1 L of plain water in 10 minutes. Predict and explain the effects on (a) plasma osmolarity, (b) ADH secretion, and (c) urine production over the following hour.

(a) Plasma osmolarity decreases as water dilutes the blood. (b) Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect the drop and signal the posterior pituitary to reduce ADH secretion. (c) With less ADH, the collecting duct is less permeable to water; less water is reabsorbed, and the student produces a large volume of dilute urine over the next hour, restoring plasma osmolarity to normal. (Compare: in dehydration, ADH rises, water is conserved, and urine is small in volume and concentrated.)

  • Confusing filtration (passive, in the glomerulus) with secretion (active, in the tubule).
  • Saying urine is the same as filtrate — most filtrate is reabsorbed; only ~1% becomes urine.
  • Forgetting that ADH targets the collecting duct water channels (aquaporin-2), not the entire nephron.
  • Saying the kidneys "make" urea — urea is produced by the liver from amino-acid breakdown; the kidneys excrete it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.4 — "describe the structure and function of the human excretory system, including filtration, reabsorption, and secretion at the nephron."

4.6 — The Nervous System: Neurons, Reflex Arcs & Body Coordination
CNS vs PNS, the structure of a neuron, action potentials, synapses, and the reflex arc.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The nervous system is the body's rapid-response coordinator. Anatomically it is divided into two parts. The central nervous system (CNS) — brain and spinal cord — integrates information and decides on responses. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) connects the CNS to the rest of the body through nerves. The PNS itself has two functional divisions: the somatic nervous system (voluntary control of skeletal muscle) and the autonomic nervous system (involuntary control of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands). The autonomic system has antagonistic subdivisions: the sympathetic branch ("fight or flight" — raises heart rate, dilates pupils, mobilises energy) and the parasympathetic branch ("rest and digest" — slows heart rate, stimulates digestion).

The basic functional cell is the neuron. A typical neuron has dendrites (branched receivers of incoming signals), a cell body with the nucleus, and a long axon that conducts the signal away. Many axons are wrapped by an insulating myelin sheath formed from Schwann cells (PNS) or oligodendrocytes (CNS); the gaps between myelin segments are the nodes of Ranvier. Neurons come in three functional kinds: sensory (afferent) neurons carry information from receptors to the CNS, interneurons integrate signals within the CNS, and motor (efferent) neurons carry signals from the CNS to effectors.

A signal travels along a neuron as an action potential. The neuron at rest maintains a membrane potential of about −70 mV by pumping Na⁺ out and K⁺ in (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase). When a stimulus depolarises the membrane to the threshold (~−55 mV), voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ rushes in, reversing the polarity to about +30 mV. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels then open, K⁺ leaves, and the membrane repolarises and briefly hyperpolarises before returning to rest. The action potential is "all or nothing" — a stimulus either reaches threshold and produces a full action potential or it does not. Myelinated axons conduct signals much faster by saltatory conduction, jumping from one node of Ranvier to the next.

At the end of an axon is a synapse. The arriving action potential triggers the release of neurotransmitter molecules (acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, GABA, glutamate) into the synaptic cleft. The transmitter binds receptors on the next cell — exciting or inhibiting it. A reflex arc is the simplest behavioural circuit: receptor → sensory neuron → interneuron(s) in the spinal cord → motor neuron → effector. The classic patellar (knee-jerk) reflex is monosynaptic, with no interneuron between the sensory and motor neuron, which is why it is so fast. Reflexes operate without conscious awareness because they bypass the brain.

  • CNS / PNS — central (brain + spinal cord) / peripheral (nerves outside CNS).
  • Sympathetic / parasympathetic — fight-or-flight / rest-and-digest branches of the autonomic system.
  • Action potential — rapid, all-or-nothing depolarisation that travels along an axon.
  • Myelin sheath — insulating lipid wrapping that speeds signal conduction.
  • Synapse — junction between two neurons, or neuron and effector, where neurotransmitter is released.
  • Reflex arc — neural pathway producing a rapid, involuntary response to a stimulus.

Worked example — Tracing a knee-jerk reflex

A reflex hammer taps the patellar tendon. Outline the neural pathway from stimulus to leg extension, naming the components in order.

Stretching the tendon stretches the quadriceps muscle spindle (receptor) → action potential travels along a sensory neuron with cell body in the dorsal root ganglion → enters the spinal cord and synapses directly on a motor neuron in the ventral horn (no interneuron — this is a monosynaptic reflex) → action potential travels back out the ventral root along the motor neuron's axon → axon terminals release acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction onto the quadriceps muscle (effector) → muscle contracts, extending the leg. The whole loop takes about 50 ms, far faster than any conscious response.

  • Treating action potentials as gradable — they are all-or-nothing; signal strength is encoded by frequency, not size.
  • Confusing dendrites (receive signals) with axons (send signals).
  • Forgetting the Na⁺/K⁺ pump — it is what establishes the resting potential between action potentials.
  • Saying reflexes "go to the brain" — many reflexes are processed entirely in the spinal cord and can occur without brain involvement.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.5 — "describe the structure and function of the human nervous system, including neurons, the reflex arc, and the CNS/PNS divisions."
