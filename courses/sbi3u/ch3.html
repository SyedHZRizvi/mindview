3.1 — Meiosis: Making Gametes & the Roots of Genetic Variation
The two-division process that halves chromosome number and reshuffles alleles.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Most body cells (somatic cells) in humans are diploid (2n = 46): two complete sets of chromosomes, one inherited from each parent. Gametes (eggs and sperm) are haploid (n = 23): one complete set. When two gametes fuse at fertilisation, the diploid number is restored. To produce haploid gametes, animals use a specialised cell division called meiosis — two rounds of division (meiosis I and meiosis II) following one round of DNA replication, producing four haploid daughter cells from one diploid parent. Without meiosis, chromosome number would double every generation.

Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes (pairs that carry the same genes in the same order — one maternal, one paternal). In prophase I, homologs pair up (synapsis) and exchange segments by crossing over at chiasmata, producing recombinant chromatids. In metaphase I, paired homologs line up at the cell's centre; the orientation of each pair is independent of every other pair (independent assortment). In anaphase I, homologs (not sister chromatids) separate to opposite poles. After meiosis I, each daughter cell is haploid in chromosome count but each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids. Meiosis II is essentially mitosis on the haploid cells: sister chromatids separate, producing four genetically unique haploid gametes.

Meiosis differs from mitosis in three key ways. Mitosis produces two genetically identical diploid daughters; meiosis produces four genetically unique haploid daughters. Mitosis has one division; meiosis has two. Mitosis has no homolog pairing or crossing over; meiosis I has both. Meiosis is the source of three of biology's most important genetic shuffling mechanisms (crossing over, independent assortment, and — combined with random fertilisation — the staggering uniqueness of every sexually produced offspring). Errors in meiosis can cause nondisjunction: a chromosome (or chromatid) fails to separate, producing gametes with the wrong number of chromosomes. Fertilisation involving a nondisjunction gamete yields a zygote with trisomy (extra chromosome, e.g., trisomy 21 = Down syndrome) or monosomy (e.g., Turner syndrome, XO).

  • Diploid (2n) / haploid (n) — having two / one complete sets of chromosomes.
  • Homologous chromosomes — paired chromosomes carrying the same genes, one from each parent.
  • Sister chromatids — two identical copies of a single chromosome joined at the centromere after DNA replication.
  • Crossing over — exchange of DNA segments between homologous chromosomes during prophase I.
  • Independent assortment — random alignment of homologous pairs in metaphase I, producing 2n combinations.
  • Nondisjunction — failure of chromosomes (meiosis I) or chromatids (meiosis II) to separate properly.

Worked example — Tracking chromosome number through meiosis

A human cell entering meiosis has 46 chromosomes (2n). State the number of chromosomes (and number of chromatids) in each of: (a) a cell at the end of meiosis I; (b) a cell at the end of meiosis II.

Before meiosis begins, DNA replication produces 46 chromosomes, each consisting of 2 sister chromatids (92 chromatids total). (a) After meiosis I, homologs are separated, so each daughter cell has 23 chromosomes, each still made of 2 sister chromatids (46 chromatids per cell). (b) Meiosis II separates the sister chromatids, so each final daughter cell has 23 chromosomes, each consisting of 1 chromatid (23 chromatids per cell). Four haploid gametes are produced from one diploid parent cell.

  • Saying meiosis "doubles" chromosome number — it halves it; fertilisation restores the diploid count.
  • Confusing meiosis I (separates homologs) with meiosis II (separates sister chromatids).
  • Putting crossing over in meiosis II — crossing over occurs in prophase I only.
  • Drawing four identical daughter cells from meiosis — they are not identical (unlike mitosis).

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "describe the cell cycle and the stages of meiosis I and II, and distinguish meiosis from mitosis."

3.2 — Mendelian Genetics: Monohybrid Crosses & Punnett Squares
Mendel's laws of segregation; how a single gene with two alleles produces predictable ratios.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Gregor Mendel (1822–1884), an Augustinian friar in Brno, performed thousands of crosses with garden peas in the 1860s. His insight — that hereditary information is carried in discrete particles ("factors", now called alleles) and that each parent contributes one factor per trait — was published in 1866 and ignored for thirty-four years. When his work was rediscovered around 1900, it became the foundation of modern genetics. The two key Mendelian laws are the law of segregation (an organism with two different alleles for a gene produces gametes carrying one or the other allele with equal probability) and the law of independent assortment (alleles of different genes are inherited independently, provided the genes are on different chromosomes or are far apart on the same chromosome).

Vocabulary. A gene is a unit of inheritance — a stretch of DNA that codes for a trait. Different versions of a gene are alleles. The pair of alleles in a diploid organism is the genotype (AA, Aa, or aa for a gene with two alleles); the observable trait is the phenotype. Homozygous means two identical alleles; heterozygous means two different alleles. A dominant allele masks a recessive allele in the heterozygote; the recessive phenotype appears only in homozygous recessive (aa) individuals. Mendel used uppercase letters for dominant alleles and lowercase for recessive.

The Punnett square is the standard tool for predicting offspring genotypes. Each gamete's possible alleles label one axis; the offspring boxes show every combination. A monohybrid cross between two heterozygotes (Aa × Aa) yields offspring in the genotypic ratio 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa and the phenotypic ratio 3 dominant : 1 recessive. A test cross (between an individual with a dominant phenotype and a homozygous recessive — Aa × aa or AA × aa) distinguishes a heterozygote from a homozygote: a heterozygote produces some recessive offspring (1:1 ratio), while a homozygote produces only dominant offspring.

  • Allele — alternative form of a gene at a specific locus.
  • Genotype / phenotype — the genetic make-up / the observable trait.
  • Homozygous / heterozygous — two identical alleles / two different alleles for a gene.
  • Dominant / recessive — masking / masked allele in a heterozygote.
  • Punnett square — a grid used to predict offspring genotypes from a cross.
  • Law of segregation — each gamete carries only one allele for each gene.

Worked example — Monohybrid cross

In pea plants, purple flower colour (P) is dominant to white (p). A heterozygous purple plant (Pp) is crossed with a white plant (pp). Predict the genotypic and phenotypic ratios of the offspring.

Pp parent gametes: P, p (each with probability 1/2). pp parent gametes: only p. Punnett square: Pp × pp → 1/2 Pp (purple) and 1/2 pp (white). Genotype ratio 1 Pp : 1 pp; phenotype ratio 1 purple : 1 white. This 1:1 outcome is also the signature of a test cross — if the purple parent were homozygous PP, all offspring would be purple.

  • Confusing genotype (Aa) with phenotype (e.g., purple) — phenotype is the observable result of the genotype.
  • Forgetting that a 3:1 phenotypic ratio requires a heterozygous × heterozygous cross.
  • Treating dominant as "stronger" or "better" — dominant simply means the allele's phenotype appears in the heterozygote.
  • Using ratios as guarantees instead of expectations — small samples deviate from the predicted ratios due to chance.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "describe Mendel's laws (segregation, independent assortment) and use Punnett squares to predict offspring outcomes."

3.3 — Dihybrid Crosses & Independent Assortment
Tracking two traits at once; the 9:3:3:1 ratio and what it tells us about linkage.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Mendel's second great insight came when he tracked two traits at the same time. He crossed pure-breeding pea plants with round, yellow seeds (RRYY) and pure-breeding plants with wrinkled, green seeds (rryy). All F1 offspring were round and yellow (RrYy), confirming dominance for both traits. He then self-crossed the F1 and obtained the famous 9:3:3:1 ratio in the F2: 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green. The reappearance of all four phenotype combinations — including new combinations not seen in the parents — proved that the two traits assort independently. This is the law of independent assortment: during gamete formation, the alleles of one gene segregate independently of the alleles of another gene.

The 9:3:3:1 ratio can be derived without drawing a 16-box Punnett square by using the product rule: the probability of two independent events is the product of their separate probabilities. Each gene in an RrYy × RrYy cross follows a 3:1 ratio independently. Probability of round = 3/4; probability of wrinkled = 1/4; probability of yellow = 3/4; probability of green = 1/4. Then probability of round-yellow = (3/4)(3/4) = 9/16; round-green = (3/4)(1/4) = 3/16; wrinkled-yellow = (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16; wrinkled-green = (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16. The 16-box square gives the same answer but is unwieldy for three or more genes.

Independent assortment only holds when the genes are on different chromosomes (or are far enough apart on the same chromosome that crossing over separates them in nearly every meiosis). Genes that are close together on the same chromosome are linked: the parental combinations of alleles are transmitted together more often than predicted, and the deviation from the 9:3:3:1 ratio is the signature of linkage. Mendel was extraordinarily lucky — every pair of pea traits he studied turned out to be on different chromosomes. This luck saved him from confusing data on linkage and helped him discover the clean law of independent assortment first.

  • Dihybrid cross — a cross tracking two genes simultaneously.
  • 9:3:3:1 ratio — F2 phenotypic ratio from a dihybrid cross of two heterozygotes.
  • Product rule — probability of two independent events = product of individual probabilities.
  • Linkage — tendency of genes on the same chromosome to be inherited together.
  • Recombinant gamete — gamete with a new (non-parental) combination of alleles produced by crossing over.
  • Test cross (dihybrid) — cross with a fully homozygous-recessive partner (aabb) reveals offspring gamete frequencies directly.

Worked example — Predicting a dihybrid offspring

In peas, round seed (R) is dominant to wrinkled (r) and yellow seed (Y) is dominant to green (y). Two heterozygotes (RrYy × RrYy) are crossed. What fraction of the offspring is expected to be wrinkled and green?

Use the product rule. Probability of wrinkled (rr) = 1/4. Probability of green (yy) = 1/4. Probability of wrinkled-and-green = (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16. In a 16-square Punnett, this is the lone "rryy" box. The remaining offspring: 9/16 round-yellow, 3/16 round-green, 3/16 wrinkled-yellow, 1/16 wrinkled-green.

  • Treating linked genes as independent — linked genes deviate from 9:3:3:1.
  • Confusing the F1 (all dominant) with the F2 (9:3:3:1) generations.
  • Drawing 16-box Punnett squares unnecessarily — the product rule is faster for predicting one specific outcome.
  • Forgetting that ratios are expectations — observed counts will fluctuate around the expected ratios.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "analyse data from monohybrid and dihybrid crosses and predict outcomes using Punnett squares and probability rules."

3.4 — Non-Mendelian Inheritance: Codominance, Incomplete Dominance & Multiple Alleles
When the simple dominant/recessive picture breaks down — ABO blood, snapdragons, sickle-cell, and polygenic traits.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Mendel's clean dominant/recessive picture turns out to be one pattern among several. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote shows a blended intermediate phenotype. The classic example is snapdragon flower colour: red (RR) × white (rr) crosses yield all pink (Rr) in the F1, and the F2 from pink × pink gives the genotype ratio 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr and the phenotype ratio 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white — the genotypic and phenotypic ratios match because neither allele dominates. In codominance, both alleles are fully and simultaneously expressed in the heterozygote without blending. Human MN blood-group antigens are codominant: an MN heterozygote produces both M and N antigens on the same red blood cells. Roan coat colour in cattle (red hairs and white hairs mixed visibly together) is also codominant.

The ABO blood group system illustrates a third pattern: multiple alleles. Three alleles for one gene exist in the human population — \(I^A\), \(I^B\), and \(i\). \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) are codominant to each other but both are dominant to \(i\). The six possible genotypes give four phenotypes: \(I^A I^A\) and \(I^A i\) → type A; \(I^B I^B\) and \(I^B i\) → type B; \(I^A I^B\) → type AB; \(ii\) → type O. The clinical importance is huge: a person with type O is a universal donor of red cells (no surface antigen), while type AB is the universal recipient (no antibodies). Blood typing for transfusions and paternity testing depends on these inheritance rules.

Many human traits are polygenic — controlled by many genes — and continuous rather than discrete. Skin colour, height, and birth weight are classic polygenic traits. With each additional gene, the distribution becomes more like a bell-shaped curve as small additive effects combine. Environmental factors (nutrition, sunlight) also influence polygenic traits, producing the complex phenotypic variation we see in human populations. Pleiotropy is the reverse situation — a single gene affects multiple phenotypes (sickle-cell allele changes red-blood-cell shape, oxygen-carrying capacity, malaria resistance, and risk of complications all at once).

  • Incomplete dominance — heterozygote shows a blended intermediate phenotype.
  • Codominance — both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote (MN, AB blood).
  • Multiple alleles — a gene with more than two alleles in the population (ABO).
  • Polygenic inheritance — many genes contribute to one trait, producing continuous variation.
  • Pleiotropy — one gene affects multiple unrelated traits.
  • Universal donor / recipient — type O blood can be given to anyone (red cells); type AB can receive any blood (red cells).

Worked example — ABO blood-group cross

A man with blood type A (genotype \(I^A i\)) has a child with a woman of blood type B (genotype \(I^B i\)). What are the possible blood types of the child, and with what probability?

The man's gametes: \(I^A\) (1/2) and \(i\) (1/2). The woman's gametes: \(I^B\) (1/2) and \(i\) (1/2). Punnett square: \(I^A I^B\) (AB, 1/4), \(I^A i\) (A, 1/4), \(I^B i\) (B, 1/4), \(ii\) (O, 1/4). Probabilities: 1/4 type A, 1/4 type B, 1/4 type AB, 1/4 type O. All four blood types are possible — a famous fact in paternity-testing problems.

  • Treating ABO blood as a simple dominant/recessive trait — three alleles with codominance produce four phenotypes.
  • Confusing codominance (both alleles fully expressed) with incomplete dominance (a blended intermediate).
  • Assuming polygenic traits follow Mendelian ratios — multiple genes plus environment yield continuous distributions.
  • Forgetting that pleiotropy and polygeny are opposites: one gene → many traits vs many genes → one trait.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.3 — "explain non-Mendelian inheritance patterns: incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles, and polygenic inheritance."

3.5 — Sex Linkage & Pedigree Analysis
X-linked traits, why colour blindness and haemophilia run in families, and how to read pedigrees.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In mammals, sex is determined by the X and Y chromosomes. Genetic females are XX; genetic males are XY. The Y is much smaller than the X and carries far fewer genes. Genes on the X chromosome (outside the small "pseudoautosomal" region shared with Y) are X-linked. Because males have only one X, they are hemizygous for X-linked genes — any allele on the X is expressed, dominant or recessive. This single-dose expression is why X-linked recessive disorders such as red–green colour blindness and haemophilia A appear far more often in males than in females. A female must inherit two copies of a recessive X-linked allele (one from each parent) to express the disorder; a male needs only one (from his mother).

Notation. X-linked alleles are written as superscripts on the X chromosome symbol. Using haemophilia: \(X^H\) for the normal allele, \(X^h\) for the haemophilia allele. Possible female genotypes are \(X^H X^H\) (homozygous normal), \(X^H X^h\) (carrier — heterozygous, not affected), and \(X^h X^h\) (affected). Possible male genotypes are \(X^H Y\) (normal) and \(X^h Y\) (affected). A carrier mother (\(X^H X^h\)) and a normal father (\(X^H Y\)) have a 1/4 chance each of: normal daughter (\(X^H X^H\)), carrier daughter (\(X^H X^h\)), normal son (\(X^H Y\)), affected son (\(X^h Y\)). Half the sons are affected; daughters are never affected unless the father also carries the allele.

A pedigree is a family tree that tracks an inherited trait across generations. Squares are males, circles are females; filled symbols are affected, horizontal lines connect mates, vertical lines connect parents to offspring, Roman numerals label generations. Reading a pedigree means inferring the genotypes of family members and the inheritance pattern. Key signatures: an autosomal dominant trait appears in every generation, affected children require an affected parent. An autosomal recessive trait can skip generations — two unaffected (carrier) parents have an affected child. An X-linked recessive trait is much more common in males, never passes from father to son directly, and an affected female has an affected father.

  • X-linked / Y-linked — gene located on the X / Y chromosome.
  • Hemizygous — having only one allele for a gene (males for X-linked genes).
  • Carrier — heterozygous for a recessive allele but not affected (often used for autosomal recessive or X-linked recessive disorders in females).
  • Pedigree — diagram showing inheritance of a trait through a family.
  • Autosomal vs sex-linked — gene on a non-sex chromosome (chromosomes 1–22) vs gene on a sex chromosome.
  • Genetic counsellor — a health professional who helps families understand inheritance risks.

Worked example — X-linked recessive pedigree

An unaffected mother and unaffected father have a son with haemophilia. The maternal grandfather was also affected. Determine the mother's genotype and the probability that a future daughter of this couple will be a carrier.

The affected son must have inherited \(X^h\) from his mother (sons get their X from mom). The mother must therefore carry \(X^h\); since she is unaffected, her genotype is \(X^H X^h\). This is consistent with her own affected father (\(X^h Y\)) — she had to inherit \(X^h\) from him. Father's genotype is \(X^H Y\). For a future daughter: cross \(X^H X^h \times X^H Y\). The daughters' genotypes are 1/2 \(X^H X^H\) (homozygous normal) and 1/2 \(X^H X^h\) (carrier). The probability the daughter is a carrier is 1/2 (= 50%).

  • Forgetting that males never inherit X from their fathers — fathers pass X only to daughters and Y only to sons.
  • Treating carrier females as affected — heterozygous carriers usually do not express the disorder.
  • Confusing X-linked recessive (mostly affects males) with autosomal recessive (affects both sexes equally).
  • Reading a pedigree as a Punnett square — pedigrees show actual family data; genotypes are inferred, not always certain.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "analyse pedigree data to determine the inheritance pattern of a trait (autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive)."

3.6 — Chromosomal Abnormalities & Introductory Biotechnology
Trisomies and monosomies; karyotypes; gel electrophoresis, PCR, and an introduction to genetic engineering.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Errors during meiosis can produce gametes with extra or missing chromosomes — a phenomenon called nondisjunction. If such a gamete fertilises a normal gamete, the resulting zygote has an abnormal chromosome number, called aneuploidy. Trisomy means one extra chromosome (47 instead of 46); monosomy means one missing chromosome (45). Most aneuploidies are lethal in early development, but a few are compatible with life. Trisomy 21 (an extra copy of chromosome 21) causes Down syndrome — characteristic facial features, intellectual disability, and a higher risk of heart defects. Sex-chromosome aneuploidies are usually milder: Turner syndrome (XO, single X) females are short-statured and usually infertile; Klinefelter syndrome (XXY) males show reduced fertility and other developmental features. Larger-scale chromosomal mutations include deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations; some cause cancer (the Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukaemia is a 9–22 translocation).

A karyotype is a photograph of an individual's chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs by size. Pre-natal karyotypes from chorionic villus sampling (~10–13 weeks) or amniocentesis (~15–20 weeks) detect aneuploidies. Modern non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) analyses fetal DNA in the maternal bloodstream and detects common trisomies (21, 18, 13) and sex-chromosome aneuploidies with greater than 99% accuracy.

Biotechnology — the use of biological systems for practical applications — has revolutionised medicine, agriculture, and forensics. At the Grade 11 level, three foundational techniques matter. Restriction enzymes are bacterial proteins that cut DNA at specific sequences ("molecular scissors"). EcoRI, for example, cuts every GAATTC site. They allow scientists to cut a gene out of one organism and paste it into another (recombinant DNA). Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size — DNA is negatively charged and migrates through a gel toward the positive electrode; smaller fragments move farther. It is used in DNA fingerprinting, paternity testing, and diagnosing genetic disease. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) uses repeated heating and cooling cycles with the heat-stable Taq polymerase to make billions of copies of a specific DNA region in a few hours. PCR underlies most modern DNA analysis — from forensics, to medical diagnostics, to COVID-19 testing.

  • Nondisjunction — failure of chromosomes to separate in meiosis, producing aneuploid gametes.
  • Aneuploidy — abnormal chromosome number (e.g., 47, 45).
  • Trisomy / monosomy — one extra / one missing chromosome.
  • Karyotype — visual representation of all chromosomes arranged in pairs by size.
  • Restriction enzyme — bacterial enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific sequence.
  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction) — laboratory technique that amplifies a specific DNA region into billions of copies.

Worked example — Origin of trisomy 21

A child has Down syndrome. Cytogenetic analysis shows that the extra chromosome 21 comes from the egg, and the error occurred in meiosis I. Explain what happened, and predict the chromosomes the egg cell contained at fertilisation.

In meiosis I, the two homologs of chromosome 21 failed to separate (nondisjunction). Both homologs travelled to the same daughter cell, producing one secondary oocyte with both copies of chromosome 21 (n + 1 = 24) and another with no copy (n − 1 = 22). The egg carrying 24 chromosomes — with two copies of chromosome 21 — was fertilised by a normal sperm carrying 23 chromosomes (one chromosome 21), giving a zygote with 47 chromosomes total and three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21).

  • Saying Down syndrome is "inherited" in a simple Mendelian way — most trisomy 21 cases arise from new nondisjunction in the mother's egg; risk rises with maternal age.
  • Confusing karyotyping (chromosome-level analysis) with DNA sequencing (nucleotide-level).
  • Thinking PCR copies the whole genome — it copies a specific region defined by the primers used.
  • Forgetting that recombinant DNA combines pieces from different organisms — the host organism then produces the foreign protein (e.g., bacteria producing human insulin).

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.4 — "describe chromosomal abnormalities such as non-disjunction (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome) and discuss applications of biotechnology (gel electrophoresis, PCR, recombinant DNA)."
