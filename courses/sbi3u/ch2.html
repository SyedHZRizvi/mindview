2.1 — Early Ideas & the Development of Evolutionary Theory
From Aristotle's "scale of nature" through Lamarck to the Darwin–Wallace synthesis.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

For most of recorded history, Western scholars treated species as fixed and arranged on a hierarchical "scale of nature" with humans at the top. Several lines of inquiry slowly eroded this idea. In the late 1700s, James Hutton and later Charles Lyell developed uniformitarianism — the principle that the same slow geological processes operating today (erosion, deposition, volcanism) shaped the Earth over immense time. This long timescale made gradual biological change conceivable. Georges Cuvier, working on fossils in the Paris Basin, demonstrated that some species had gone extinct — also impossible in a fixed-creation view. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1809) proposed the first comprehensive theory of evolution: organisms have an inner drive toward complexity, and traits acquired through use or disuse during life are passed to offspring (the inheritance of acquired characteristics). Although Lamarck's mechanism turned out to be wrong, he made change in species a respectable scientific hypothesis.

Charles Darwin's voyage on HMS Beagle (1831–1836) gave him five years of fieldwork. He noted that finches on different Galápagos Islands had different beak shapes adapted to local food sources, that fossil sloths in South America resembled living sloths, and that closely related but distinct species often lived in neighbouring regions. Back in England Darwin spent twenty years building his argument. The economist Thomas Malthus's observation that populations grow geometrically but resources arithmetically — leading to a "struggle for existence" — gave him the missing piece. Alfred Russel Wallace, working independently in the Malay Archipelago, arrived at the same conclusion in 1858. A joint paper was read to the Linnean Society of London that year, and Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859.

Darwin's argument has four observations and one inference: organisms produce more offspring than can survive; populations show heritable variation; resources are limited (struggle for existence); therefore the variants best matched to local conditions leave more offspring (natural selection). Over generations, advantageous traits accumulate. Darwin lacked a mechanism for inheritance — Gregor Mendel's contemporary work on peas was unknown to him — and so could not explain how variation persists. The reconciliation came in the early 20th century with the modern synthesis, when geneticists (Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky, Mayr) merged Mendelian genetics with Darwinian selection.

  • Uniformitarianism — geological processes operate consistently over long time scales.
  • Lamarckian inheritance — the (incorrect) idea that traits acquired during life can be inherited.
  • Natural selection — differential reproductive success of individuals based on heritable variation.
  • Fitness (biological) — number of offspring an individual contributes to the next generation, not strength or speed.
  • Modern synthesis — the 1930s–40s merger of Mendelian genetics with Darwinian natural selection.
  • Adaptation — an inherited trait that increases an organism's fitness in its environment.

Worked example — Lamarck vs Darwin on giraffe necks

Compare how Lamarck and Darwin would each explain the evolution of long necks in giraffes.

Lamarck (1809): a giraffe's ancestor stretched its neck to reach high leaves, lengthened it during its lifetime, and passed the elongated neck to offspring — repeated for many generations. Darwin (1859): ancestral giraffes already varied in neck length; those with slightly longer necks reached more food, survived droughts better, and had more offspring than short-necked individuals. Because neck length is heritable, the average neck length increased over generations. We now know Darwin's mechanism is correct: traits acquired during life (a stretched neck) do not change germ-line DNA and are not inherited.

  • Saying "individuals evolve" — populations evolve, not individuals; an individual's genome is fixed at conception.
  • Equating fitness with strength — fitness in biology means reproductive success.
  • Calling natural selection "survival of the fittest" without explaining what "fit" means in context.
  • Describing selection as goal-directed — selection has no purpose; it is the statistical outcome of differential reproduction.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "explain the fundamental theory of biological evolution as developed by Darwin and Wallace, and the importance of the modern synthesis."

2.2 — Mechanisms of Natural Selection
Directional, stabilizing, and disruptive selection; sexual selection; the four ingredients required for selection to act.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Natural selection acts whenever four conditions are met: (1) variation exists for a trait within a population, (2) the trait is heritable — passed genetically from parent to offspring, (3) the trait affects survival or reproduction, and (4) more offspring are produced than can survive, so not every individual contributes equally to the next generation. When all four hold, the allele frequencies of the population shift over generations. This shift — the change in genetic make-up of a population over time — is microevolution.

Three classic patterns of natural selection are recognised. Directional selection favours one extreme of a trait; the mean shifts. Examples: peppered moths darkening during the British Industrial Revolution because soot-covered trees made dark moths better camouflaged; antibiotic resistance in bacteria; pesticide resistance in insects. Stabilizing selection favours the average and prunes the extremes; the variance shrinks. Human birth weight is the canonical example — babies that are too small face survival risk, babies that are too large face delivery risk, so the average sits at ~3.5 kg. Disruptive selection favours both extremes against the middle and can split a population. The cichlid fish of African rift lakes show disruptive selection on jaw shape leading to different feeding strategies.

Sexual selection, a form Darwin emphasised in his 1871 work, acts on traits that affect mating success rather than survival. It comes in two flavours: intersexual selection (one sex — usually females — chooses among competing males for "honest signals" of fitness: peacock tails, frog calls, bowerbird nests) and intrasexual selection (males compete directly: elk antlers, ram horns). Sexually selected traits are often costly to survival (a male peacock's tail makes him conspicuous to predators), which is itself a signal of underlying genetic quality. Other modes of selection include kin selection, in which an individual helps a genetic relative, increasing the relative's reproductive success and thereby propagating shared alleles (Hamilton's rule).

  • Microevolution — change in allele frequencies within a population over generations.
  • Directional selection — selection that favours one extreme phenotype.
  • Stabilizing selection — selection that favours the average phenotype and reduces variation.
  • Disruptive selection — selection that favours both extremes and may lead to divergence.
  • Sexual selection — selection for traits that improve mating success.
  • Coevolution — reciprocal evolution between two interacting species (predator–prey, host–parasite, flower–pollinator).

Worked example — Identifying the type of selection

In a Galápagos finch population, drought caused most small seeds to disappear, leaving only large, hard seeds. Beak size was heritable. After one breeding season, the average beak depth in the population had increased by 4%. Which type of selection acted, and on which extreme of the distribution?

Selection favoured one extreme (large, deep beaks better able to crack hard seeds), shifting the population mean upward — this is directional selection. The trait was variable, heritable, and affected survival/reproduction, satisfying the conditions for natural selection. (Peter and Rosemary Grant documented this exact event in Geospiza fortis during the 1977 drought.)

  • Saying "selection acts on the individual" — selection acts on phenotypes within a population, changing allele frequencies; the individual's genotype is fixed.
  • Confusing mutation rate with selection — mutation provides variation, selection sorts it.
  • Calling sexual selection "irrelevant to fitness" — fitness is reproductive success, so mating success is fitness.
  • Forgetting that selection has no foresight — bacteria do not "decide" to develop antibiotic resistance; pre-existing resistant mutants survive and reproduce.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "explain the four principles of natural selection (variation, heritability, differential survival/reproduction, and accumulation across generations)."

2.3 — Evidence for Evolution
Fossils, biogeography, homology, embryology, vestigial structures, molecular data, and observed change.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Evolutionary theory rests on independent lines of evidence that converge on the same conclusion. The fossil record shows that life has changed dramatically over geological time and that transitional forms exist (Archaeopteryx, with feathers and teeth, between non-avian dinosaurs and birds; Tiktaalik, a Devonian "fishapod" with limb-like fins; whale ancestors with hind limbs). Radiometric dating (uranium–lead, potassium–argon, carbon-14) gives reliable ages. The order of fossils in undisturbed strata matches the predicted order based on living relatives' anatomy — simple aquatic forms first, then jawed fish, then tetrapods, amniotes, mammals, and finally hominins.

Biogeography — the geographic distribution of species — provides another line of evidence. Closely related species often live in adjacent areas (Galápagos finches, marsupial diversity in Australia), while distantly related species fill similar roles on different continents (placental wolves vs Tasmanian wolves). Comparative anatomy reveals homologous structures (e.g., the one-bone-two-bones-many-bones limb plan of mammals, birds, reptiles) that share an evolutionary origin even when their functions differ — the human arm, whale flipper, and bat wing all share the same skeletal plan. Analogous structures (insect wing vs bird wing) share function but evolved independently (convergent evolution). Vestigial structures — reduced organs whose functional importance has waned (whale pelvis, human appendix, hind-limb buds in snake embryos) — are the leftover hardware of evolutionary tinkering.

Embryology reveals shared developmental patterns: pharyngeal arches appear in all vertebrate embryos. Molecular biology is now the most precise source of evidence. All organisms use DNA, the same genetic code, and many of the same proteins (cytochrome c, hemoglobin). Sequence differences quantify how long species have been evolving separately. Finally, natural selection has been directly observed: the Grants' Galápagos finch beaks, peppered-moth colour shifts, antibiotic-resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Lenski's long-term E. coli experiment all document evolution in real time.

  • Transitional fossil — a fossil that bridges two groups (e.g., Tiktaalik, Archaeopteryx).
  • Homologous structures — structures inherited from a common ancestor, even if used differently.
  • Analogous structures — structures with similar function but different evolutionary origins (convergent evolution).
  • Vestigial structures — reduced versions of organs that were functional in ancestors.
  • Molecular clock — the use of sequence differences to estimate divergence times.
  • Convergent evolution — independent origin of similar features in unrelated lineages (dolphins, ichthyosaurs, sharks).

Worked example — Homology vs analogy

A bat's wing and a butterfly's wing both serve to power flapping flight. Skeletally, the bat's wing contains a humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, and elongated phalanges. The butterfly's wing has no internal bones; it consists of a chitinous membrane reinforced by hollow tubes called veins. Are these structures homologous or analogous? Justify and identify the type of evolution involved.

They are analogous. The two wings perform the same function but have entirely different underlying anatomy and embryological origins (vertebrate forelimb bones vs arthropod cuticle), and the most recent common ancestor of bats and butterflies was a non-flying invertebrate-like animal. The match in function arose independently and is an example of convergent evolution. Compare with bat wings vs human arms, which are homologous: same skeletal plan, different uses.

  • Treating gaps in the fossil record as evidence against evolution — fossilisation is rare; expected gaps do not invalidate the pattern.
  • Calling all similarities homologous — function-only similarities (analogous) reflect convergence.
  • Saying humans descended from monkeys — humans and other primates share a common ancestor; we are not descended from any living monkey.
  • Confusing radiometric dating with carbon-14 only — carbon-14 is for organic material under ~50 000 years; long-timescale dates use other isotopes (U-238, K-40).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.4 — "describe the lines of evidence (fossils, biogeography, comparative anatomy, embryology, molecular biology) that support the theory of evolution."

2.4 — Sources of Variation: Mutation & Genetic Recombination
Without variation, no evolution — where new variation comes from.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Natural selection sorts existing variation but does not create it. Where does variation come from? Three sources matter. Mutation is the ultimate source — a change in the DNA sequence caused by replication error, chemical damage, or radiation. Point mutations (substitutions) and indels (insertions or deletions) at coding sites can change a protein; mutations in regulatory regions can change when, where, or how strongly a gene is expressed. Mutations occur at a rate of roughly 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁹ per nucleotide per generation in most species. Most are neutral or slightly harmful; rarely, a mutation gives an advantage. Even rare beneficial mutations matter on long timescales — a single advantageous allele can sweep through a population over hundreds of generations.

For sexually reproducing organisms, genetic recombination during meiosis reshuffles existing alleles into new combinations every generation. Three meiotic events drive this. Crossing over in prophase I exchanges segments between homologous chromosomes, mixing maternal and paternal alleles within a chromosome. Independent assortment in metaphase I allows each homologous pair to align independently, generating \(2^{23}\) ≈ 8.4 million possible chromosome combinations in humans (one of each pair). Random fertilization combines one sperm and one egg out of millions, multiplying combinations to about \(7 \times 10^{13}\) for a single human couple. The result: every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.

Gene flow (migration of individuals between populations) and genetic drift (random sampling effects, especially in small populations) further shape variation. Drift is non-adaptive — small populations can lose alleles to chance, as in the bottleneck effect (cheetahs experienced a severe genetic bottleneck thousands of years ago) and the founder effect (small colonising groups carry only a subset of the parent population's alleles, often expressed in higher rates of certain inherited diseases in island populations).

  • Mutation — heritable change in DNA sequence.
  • Crossing over — exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes in prophase I.
  • Independent assortment — random alignment of homologous pairs in metaphase I.
  • Random fertilization — random combination of one sperm and one egg out of millions.
  • Gene flow — movement of alleles between populations via migration.
  • Genetic drift — random change in allele frequencies, important in small populations.

Worked example — Counting possible offspring genotypes

Ignoring crossing over, how many genetically distinct gametes can a human produce by independent assortment alone? How many possible offspring genotypes (combining gametes from both parents) are possible?

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes. Each homologous pair can independently send either the maternal or paternal homolog to a gamete, giving \(2^{23} = 8\,388\,608\) gametes per parent. Combining two parents: \(2^{23} \times 2^{23} = 2^{46} \approx 7 \times 10^{13}\) genotypes — roughly 70 trillion. Crossing over makes the real number astronomically larger.

  • Treating all mutations as "bad" — most are neutral; a few are beneficial.
  • Saying meiosis "creates" new alleles — meiosis shuffles existing alleles; only mutation creates new alleles.
  • Forgetting genetic drift in small populations — chance can swamp selection when N is small.
  • Confusing gene flow (migration of alleles) with genetic drift (random sampling within a population).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.3 — "distinguish among the major sources of variation in populations (mutation, sexual reproduction including independent assortment, crossing over, and random fertilization)."

2.5 — Speciation & Macroevolution
How one species becomes two — allopatric and sympatric mechanisms — and patterns over deep time.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Speciation is the process by which one species splits into two. Under the biological species concept, speciation is complete when two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring — they are reproductively isolated. Reproductive barriers come in two kinds: prezygotic (no fertile zygote forms — habitat, behavioural, temporal, mechanical, or gametic isolation) and postzygotic (a hybrid forms but is inviable or sterile — the mule, a horse × donkey, is sterile because chromosome numbers do not pair correctly in meiosis).

Allopatric speciation (literally "other land") is the most common pattern. A geographic barrier — a new river, a glacier advancing, a mountain rising, a vicariance event — splits an ancestral population. Each isolated subpopulation then experiences different mutations, drift, and selection. After enough time apart, reproductive isolation is complete even if the populations meet again. Classic examples: pocket gophers split by the Grand Canyon; the Galápagos finches radiating from a single ancestral colonist; the Squirrel populations on the rims of the Grand Canyon.

Sympatric speciation (literally "same land") occurs without geographic isolation. It is rarer but documented. Polyploidy, common in plants, instantly creates a reproductive barrier — a tetraploid (4n) plant cannot produce fertile offspring with a diploid (2n) parent because their hybrids are sterile triploids. Many crop species (modern wheat, cotton, strawberries) arose by polyploidy. Disruptive selection on ecological resources (e.g., apple maggot flies that lay eggs preferentially on apples vs hawthorn fruits) and sexual selection (cichlid fish in Lake Victoria diverging by female preference for male colour patterns) can also drive sympatric divergence.

Over very long time scales, the cumulative effect of speciation and extinction produces patterns visible in the fossil record. Adaptive radiation — a single ancestor giving rise to a burst of diverse descendants — has happened repeatedly: mammals after the K-Pg extinction 66 million years ago, the Cambrian "explosion" 540 million years ago, Hawaiian honeycreepers. Speciation is sometimes slow and gradual (Darwinian gradualism) and sometimes rapid (Gould and Eldredge's punctuated equilibrium); both patterns are observed depending on the lineage and environment.

  • Reproductive isolation — barriers that prevent two populations from interbreeding.
  • Allopatric speciation — divergence following geographic separation.
  • Sympatric speciation — divergence within a shared range (often via polyploidy or strong disruptive selection).
  • Polyploidy — having more than two complete sets of chromosomes; common in plants.
  • Adaptive radiation — rapid diversification from a single ancestor (Galápagos finches, Hawaiian silverswords).
  • Punctuated equilibrium — long periods of stasis interrupted by short bursts of rapid speciation.

Worked example — Identifying the speciation mode

A single ancestral plant species had 2n = 14 chromosomes. A meiotic error in one individual produced a viable offspring with 2n = 28 chromosomes (tetraploid). The tetraploid self-fertilises and forms a small reproductively isolated population. Identify the mode of speciation and explain why reproductive isolation is immediate.

This is sympatric speciation via polyploidy. Hybrid offspring between the tetraploid (gametes = 14 chromosomes) and the diploid parent (gametes = 7 chromosomes) would be triploid (3n = 21). Triploid plants have an odd number of chromosomes that cannot pair evenly in meiosis, so they are sterile. The tetraploid population is therefore reproductively isolated from the start — no geographic separation needed.

  • Claiming evolution stops at the species level — speciation is a continuum, and "species" is a useful but imperfect category.
  • Treating gradualism and punctuated equilibrium as mutually exclusive — both occur depending on lineage and timescale.
  • Forgetting prezygotic barriers — most reproductive isolation in nature is prezygotic (no hybrid forms in the first place).
  • Calling all hybrids inviable — many hybrids form but are sterile (mule, liger); reproductive isolation can be partial.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.5 — "distinguish between microevolution and macroevolution, and describe allopatric and sympatric speciation."

2.6 — Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium & Population Genetics
A simple equation that tells us when a population is NOT evolving — and why that matters.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A century after Darwin, biologists wanted to know whether allele frequencies remain stable across generations or change inevitably. In 1908, English mathematician G. H. Hardy and German physician Wilhelm Weinberg showed independently that, under specific assumptions, allele frequencies do not change from one generation to the next. Their result — Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium — provides a null hypothesis: any observed change in allele frequency indicates that one or more of the assumptions has been violated, meaning the population is evolving.

The five assumptions of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium are: (1) very large population size (no drift), (2) no migration in or out (no gene flow), (3) no mutation (or balanced mutation rates), (4) random mating with respect to genotype, and (5) no natural selection — all genotypes have equal survival and reproductive success. When all five hold for an autosomal locus with two alleles, the frequencies of the two alleles \(p\) (for A) and \(q\) (for a), with \(p + q = 1\), give predictable genotype frequencies: \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\), where \(p^2\) is the homozygous-dominant frequency (AA), \(2pq\) is the heterozygous frequency (Aa), and \(q^2\) is the homozygous-recessive frequency (aa).

In practice no real population meets all five assumptions, so Hardy–Weinberg serves as a baseline against which to detect evolution. If we observe genotype frequencies that match Hardy–Weinberg predictions, we cannot detect evolution at that locus during the timescale measured. If we observe deviation — too many homozygotes (inbreeding), too few of one allele (selection or drift), allele frequencies that change between generations (selection, drift, gene flow) — we have evidence that microevolution is occurring. Hardy–Weinberg also gives a practical way to estimate the heterozygous carrier frequency for autosomal recessive disorders: if 1 in 2500 newborns has cystic fibrosis (\(q^2 = 1/2500\)), then \(q = 1/50\), and the carrier frequency \(2pq \approx 2(1)(1/50) = 1/25\) — so about 4% of the population carries one CF allele.

  • Allele frequency — the proportion of one allele among all copies of a gene at a locus in a population.
  • Hardy–Weinberg equation — \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\), the genotype frequencies expected under no evolution.
  • Genotype frequency — proportion of a population with a given genotype (AA, Aa, aa).
  • Gene pool — all alleles for all genes in a population at one point in time.
  • Microevolution — change in allele frequency from one generation to the next.
  • Null model — a baseline expectation used to test whether a process is acting.

Worked example — Calculating heterozygote frequency

In a Canadian population, 9% of people are homozygous recessive (aa) for a recessive allele. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what fraction of the population is heterozygous (Aa)?

Set \(q^2 = 0.09\), so \(q = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.30\). Then \(p = 1 - q = 0.70\). The heterozygous frequency is \(2pq = 2(0.70)(0.30) = 0.42\), or 42%. Note that even though only 9% of the population shows the recessive phenotype, almost half are carriers — an important fact for genetic counselling.

  • Computing \(2pq\) as \(p + q\) — \(2pq\) is the heterozygous frequency, not the sum of allele frequencies.
  • Confusing genotype frequencies with allele frequencies — \(p\) and \(q\) refer to alleles, \(p^2\), \(2pq\), \(q^2\) refer to genotypes.
  • Forgetting that Hardy–Weinberg requires all five assumptions to hold — even one violation makes the population evolve.
  • Applying the equation to X-linked traits without modification — sex-linked loci need a separate formulation.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.6 — "describe the Hardy–Weinberg principle and apply the equation \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\) to a simple population-genetics problem."
