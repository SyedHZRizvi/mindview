How plants are built, how they grow, and how they support life on Earth

5.1 — Diversity of Plants & Plant Tissue Systems
A quick survey of plant evolution, then the three tissue systems that every vascular plant shares.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Plants colonised land about 470 million years ago, evolving from green algae. Four major groups illustrate the evolutionary trajectory. Bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) are non-vascular, lack true roots, and depend on water for sexual reproduction; they remain low-growing. Seedless vascular plants (ferns, horsetails, club mosses) developed xylem and phloem, allowing taller bodies, but still release motile sperm that need water. Gymnosperms ("naked seeds" — conifers, cycads, ginkgo) introduced seeds and pollen, freeing reproduction from standing water; they bear seeds on cones. Angiosperms ("seeds in a vessel" — the flowering plants) introduced flowers and fruits, co-evolved with animal pollinators, and now dominate most ecosystems and all major crop families. Angiosperms split into monocots (one cotyledon — grasses, palms, lilies, orchids) and eudicots (two cotyledons — oaks, roses, beans, most flowering plants).

Every vascular plant is built from three tissue systems. The dermal tissue system covers the plant body. In young plants and herbaceous parts, the epidermis is a single layer covered by a waxy cuticle that minimises water loss; specialised guard cells flank tiny pores called stomata that open and close for gas exchange. In older woody plants, dermal tissue is replaced by bark. The ground tissue system fills most of the plant body and serves storage, support, and photosynthesis. Three cell types are key: parenchyma (the most common — thin-walled, alive at maturity, photosynthesis and storage), collenchyma (thicker walls, flexible support — celery strings), and sclerenchyma (very thick lignified walls, often dead at maturity — fibres and gritty stone cells in pears).

The vascular tissue system moves water, minerals, and sugars. Xylem conducts water and minerals upward from roots to shoots. It is made of tracheids (long thin cells with pitted walls, present in all vascular plants) and vessel elements (shorter, wider, joined into continuous pipes — evolved in flowering plants). Both are dead at maturity; only the cell walls remain. Phloem transports sugars (mainly sucrose) in any direction. It consists of sieve-tube elements — long living cells with pores at their end walls (sieve plates) — supported by companion cells that load and unload sugars. Vascular tissues are organised into vascular bundles: scattered in monocot stems, arranged in a ring in eudicot stems.

  • Vascular plant — plant with xylem and phloem for internal transport.
  • Monocot vs eudicot — angiosperm with one vs two cotyledons; differ in leaf veins, vascular bundles, flower parts.
  • Dermal / ground / vascular tissue — three tissue systems of vascular plants.
  • Stoma / guard cell — pore in the epidermis / specialised cell that opens and closes the pore.
  • Xylem / phloem — water-conducting / sugar-conducting vascular tissue.
  • Parenchyma / collenchyma / sclerenchyma — three principal ground-tissue cell types.

Worked example — Distinguishing monocot and eudicot stems under the microscope

You examine two herbaceous stems in cross section. Stem A has vascular bundles arranged in a single ring near the perimeter. Stem B has vascular bundles scattered throughout the ground tissue. Which is a monocot, which is a eudicot, and what other features could you check to confirm your call?

Stem A (vascular bundles in a ring) is a eudicot; stem B (scattered bundles) is a monocot. To confirm, examine a leaf: eudicots typically have netted (reticulate) veins, while monocots have parallel veins. Look at the flower: eudicot flower parts come in multiples of 4 or 5; monocot flower parts come in multiples of 3. Count cotyledons in the seed: 2 for eudicots, 1 for monocots.

  • Saying all plants have xylem and phloem — non-vascular bryophytes (mosses) do not.
  • Confusing the cuticle (waxy lipid) with the cell wall (cellulose) — different materials, different locations.
  • Drawing stomata on the upper leaf surface in temperate plants — most are on the lower surface to reduce water loss.
  • Calling sclerenchyma cells "alive" — most are dead at maturity; only the reinforced cell walls remain.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F3.1 — "describe the structure and function of the three plant tissue systems (dermal, ground, vascular) and distinguish monocots from eudicots."

5.2 — Roots, Stems & Leaves: Form Meets Function
How each plant organ is shaped to do its job — anchoring, supporting, photosynthesising.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Plants have three vegetative organs: roots, stems, and leaves. Roots anchor the plant, absorb water and minerals, and often store food. Two major root systems are seen: a taproot (one large primary root with smaller laterals — carrots, oak trees, eudicots) and a fibrous root system (many similarly sized roots branching from the stem base — grasses, monocots). Just behind each root tip is the zone of elongation, and behind that the root hair zone, where the epidermis grows tiny single-celled extensions called root hairs that vastly increase surface area for water and ion uptake. Water and ions move into root xylem either through cells (symplast pathway) or between cells (apoplast pathway), but at the endodermis the apoplast pathway is blocked by the waxy Casparian strip, forcing all substances through cell membranes — a quality-control checkpoint.

Stems support leaves and conduct materials. In addition to housing vascular bundles, stems have nodes (where leaves attach), internodes (between nodes), terminal buds (apical growth), and axillary buds (potential lateral growth, suppressed by apical dominance from the terminal bud's auxin). Stems show two kinds of growth. Primary growth (from apical meristems at shoot and root tips) elongates the plant. Secondary growth (from lateral meristems — the vascular cambium and cork cambium) thickens stems and roots in woody plants. The vascular cambium adds secondary xylem to the inside (creating wood) and secondary phloem to the outside; the cork cambium produces bark. Annual rings in temperate woody plants reflect seasonal differences in cell size — wide spring vessels and narrow summer ones — so a ring count gives the tree's age.

Leaves are the plant's solar panels. A typical leaf has a flat blade and a stalk (petiole). Inside, the upper epidermis (with cuticle, no chloroplasts) lets light through to the palisade mesophyll — columnar parenchyma cells packed with chloroplasts — and the spongy mesophyll below, where loose cells with air spaces facilitate \(CO_2\) movement. The lower epidermis carries most of the stomata flanked by guard cells. Veins (vascular bundles) deliver water and remove sugars. Many leaves are modified for special functions: cactus spines (protection, water conservation), tendrils (climbing), bracts (mistaken for flower petals in poinsettias), bulb scales (storage in onions).

  • Apical meristem — region of dividing cells at root and shoot tips; drives primary growth (length).
  • Vascular / cork cambium — lateral meristems that drive secondary growth (thickness) in woody plants.
  • Root hair — single-cell extension of root epidermis that increases absorptive surface area.
  • Casparian strip — waxy band in the endodermis that controls what enters root xylem.
  • Palisade vs spongy mesophyll — densely packed photosynthetic cells / loosely packed cells with air spaces for gas movement.
  • Annual ring — yearly increment of secondary xylem in temperate woody plants.

Worked example — Reading a tree cross section

A tree stump shows 47 rings, of which several near the centre are narrow and several near the edge are very wide. What is the tree's age, and what might the ring widths tell you about its life history?

Each annual ring represents one year of secondary growth, so the tree is about 47 years old. Narrow rings indicate years of slow growth — drought, cold summers, shading by neighbours, or pest damage. Wide rings indicate favourable years — good rainfall, removal of nearby competitors (perhaps when another tree fell), or warmer growing seasons. Dendrochronologists use these patterns to reconstruct past climates and date archaeological wood.

  • Confusing root hairs (single-cell extensions) with lateral roots (multi-cellular branches).
  • Drawing chloroplasts in the upper epidermis — chloroplasts are concentrated in the mesophyll, not the surface epidermis.
  • Treating apical and lateral meristems as the same — apical = primary (length); lateral = secondary (thickness).
  • Saying "wood" is mostly phloem — wood is secondary xylem; secondary phloem is part of the bark.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F3.2 — "describe the structure and function of roots, stems, and leaves of vascular plants, including primary and secondary growth."

5.3 — Transport in Plants: Water, Minerals, and Sugars
Transpiration pull, cohesion-tension theory in xylem, and pressure flow in phloem.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A redwood tree may lift water more than 100 metres against gravity, without a pump. How? The accepted explanation is the cohesion-tension theory. Water at the leaf surface evaporates from mesophyll cells into the substomatal air space and is lost through stomata — a process called transpiration. As water exits the leaf, it pulls a continuous chain of water molecules behind it. Water has unusual cohesion (molecules stick to each other via hydrogen bonds) and adhesion (molecules stick to xylem walls). These properties allow the entire column of water in the xylem to be under tension and to remain continuous from root to leaf. Roots replenish the lost water by absorbing it from the soil — driven ultimately by the leaves, not the roots.

Plants regulate transpiration by opening and closing stomata. When guard cells take in water (osmosis), they swell unevenly because of their thicker inner walls and arc apart, opening the pore. When they lose water, they collapse and close. Open stomata allow \(CO_2\) to enter for photosynthesis but inevitably let water escape; the trade-off between gas exchange and water loss is central to plant ecology. C4 and CAM plants (sugarcane, corn, cacti, succulents) have specialised adaptations that reduce water loss in hot or dry environments. Wilting occurs when transpiration exceeds water uptake — cells lose turgor and the plant droops.

Sugars produced by photosynthesis must be moved from the leaves (the source) to other parts of the plant where they will be used or stored (the sink). The pressure-flow model explains phloem transport. At the source, sucrose is actively loaded into sieve-tube elements; this raises the solute concentration, so water enters by osmosis (from neighbouring xylem) and raises the hydrostatic pressure. At the sink, sucrose is actively unloaded; water exits, pressure falls. The pressure gradient — high at the source, low at the sink — drives sap to flow from source to sink, dragging dissolved sugars along. Phloem transport is bidirectional over the growing season: in spring, sugars stored in roots are mobilised upward to growing buds (root = source, bud = sink); in summer, leaves load phloem for storage downward (leaf = source, root = sink).

  • Transpiration — loss of water vapour from a plant, mostly through stomata in leaves.
  • Cohesion-tension theory — explanation of xylem water transport using cohesion and adhesion of water molecules.
  • Turgor pressure — pressure of cell contents against the cell wall; gives non-woody plants their rigidity.
  • Source / sink — sugar-producing region / sugar-using region of a plant.
  • Pressure-flow model — explanation of phloem transport using osmotic pressure differences between source and sink.
  • Translocation — movement of sugars through phloem.

Worked example — Predicting changes in transpiration rate

A potted tomato plant is moved from a humid greenhouse (relative humidity 95%, no breeze) to a windy outdoor area (relative humidity 30%, steady breeze). Predict how transpiration rate will change and explain why.

Transpiration will increase substantially. Lower humidity creates a steeper water-vapour gradient between the moist leaf interior and the surrounding air, so water exits the stomata more quickly. The breeze removes the thin boundary layer of saturated air that normally sits next to the leaf surface, further steepening the gradient. Both factors increase the rate at which water is pulled up the xylem. The plant will need more frequent watering or it will wilt as transpiration exceeds water uptake.

  • Saying plants "pump" water like animals do — there is no pump; transpiration pulls water passively.
  • Confusing apoplast (through cell walls) with symplast (through cytoplasm via plasmodesmata) pathways in roots.
  • Forgetting that phloem moves two-way — xylem flow is one-way (up), but phloem flow tracks source-to-sink.
  • Treating stomata as constantly open — guard cells close stomata under drought, at night, or in high \(CO_2\).

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F3.3 — "explain transport of water and minerals in xylem (cohesion-tension theory) and translocation of sugars in phloem (pressure-flow model)."

5.4 — Plant Reproduction: Flowers, Fruits & Alternation of Generations
Flower anatomy, pollination, fertilization, seed and fruit formation, vegetative propagation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Plants alternate between a multicellular diploid sporophyte and a multicellular haploid gametophyte — a life cycle called alternation of generations. In angiosperms, the sporophyte is dominant: it is the visible plant body. The gametophytes are tiny and live within the sporophyte's reproductive structures. The male gametophyte is the pollen grain; the female gametophyte is the embryo sac inside an ovule. This contrasts with mosses, where the green plant we see is actually the gametophyte and the sporophyte is a small structure on top.

A typical flower has four whorls of modified leaves. The outer sepals (collectively the calyx) protect the bud. Inside are the petals (corolla), often colourful to attract pollinators. Inside the petals are the stamens — each consisting of a filament topped by an anther where pollen is produced. At the centre is the carpel (or several fused carpels forming a pistil): a stigma at the top that receives pollen, a style connecting to the ovary at the base, and one or more ovules inside the ovary. Each ovule contains the female gametophyte with the egg.

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma. Wind-pollinated plants (grasses, oaks) produce vast quantities of small light pollen and have inconspicuous flowers; animal-pollinated plants (most flowering plants) have showier flowers and produce nectar to attract insects, birds, or bats. Once on the stigma, a pollen grain germinates a pollen tube that grows down the style. Angiosperms use double fertilization: one sperm fuses with the egg to form the diploid zygote (which becomes the embryo); the other sperm fuses with two polar nuclei in the embryo sac to form a triploid endosperm that nourishes the developing seed. The ovule's tissues become the seed coat; the ovary wall becomes the fruit.

Many plants also reproduce asexually by vegetative propagation: strawberry stolons (runners), potato tubers, garlic and onion bulbs, ginger rhizomes, crocus corms, and rooted cuttings used by gardeners. Vegetatively produced offspring are clones of the parent. Asexual reproduction is fast and lets a successful genotype spread, but offers no genetic variation against changing conditions or new pathogens — the Irish potato famine (1845–49) struck a population of nearly identical potato clones.

  • Alternation of generations — life cycle alternating between haploid gametophyte and diploid sporophyte stages.
  • Stamen — male reproductive part: filament + anther (produces pollen).
  • Carpel / pistil — female reproductive part: stigma + style + ovary (contains ovules).
  • Pollination — transfer of pollen from anther to stigma.
  • Double fertilization — angiosperm phenomenon: one sperm forms the embryo, the other forms the endosperm.
  • Vegetative propagation — asexual reproduction producing clonal offspring (stolons, tubers, rhizomes, cuttings).

Worked example — Predicting consequences of removing the carpel

You remove all carpels from a tomato flower before pollination. The flower's other parts are intact. Predict what will happen and explain.

The flower can still receive pollen on the petals or filaments, but no fertilisation can occur because the female gametophyte (the embryo sac with egg and polar nuclei) is inside the ovule of the carpel. Without the egg, no zygote forms; without polar nuclei, no endosperm. With no fertilisation, the ovary wall does not develop into a fruit, so the flower will wither and fall without producing a tomato. A bee visiting the flower may still pick up some pollen, but cannot deliver it productively at this plant.

  • Confusing pollination (pollen lands on stigma) with fertilization (sperm fuses with egg) — these are sequential events.
  • Calling fruit "the seed" — a fruit is the matured ovary; seeds are inside the fruit.
  • Saying double fertilization produces two embryos — it produces one embryo and the endosperm food supply.
  • Treating clones as identical to ageless individuals — vegetative propagation produces genetic clones, but environmental conditions still cause phenotypic differences.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F3.4 — "describe plant reproduction including alternation of generations, flower anatomy, pollination, fertilization, and vegetative propagation."

5.5 — Plant Growth, Hormones & Environmental Responses
Auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin, ABA, ethylene; phototropism, gravitropism, photoperiodism.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Plants cannot move, but they grow and respond. Growth and environmental responses are coordinated by small organic molecules called plant hormones. Five classes are foundational at the Grade 11 level. Auxin (chiefly indole-3-acetic acid, IAA) drives cell elongation, sets apical dominance (the terminal bud's auxin suppresses lateral bud growth), and mediates phototropism and gravitropism. Gibberellins stimulate stem elongation (think of bolting lettuce), promote seed germination, and trigger flowering in many species. Cytokinins promote cell division (cytokinesis) and counteract apical dominance, encouraging lateral branching when used in tissue culture. Abscisic acid (ABA) is the "stress hormone" — it closes stomata under drought, maintains seed dormancy, and protects plants from cold. Ethylene is a gas hormone that ripens fruit, promotes leaf and fruit drop (abscission), and is produced in response to wounding and stress; this is why one ripe apple speeds the ripening of others in a paper bag.

Hormones drive directional responses called tropisms. Phototropism is growth toward light: in a shoot, auxin redistributes to the shaded side, elongates those cells more than cells on the lit side, and the shoot bends toward the light. Gravitropism is growth in response to gravity: shoots grow up (negative gravitropism), roots grow down (positive gravitropism). The mechanism involves statoliths (dense starch-filled organelles) that sediment to the lower side of cells in the root cap, triggering asymmetric auxin transport. Thigmotropism is directional growth in response to touch — climbing vines coiling around supports.

Plants also respond to photoperiod — the length of light/dark periods over 24 hours. Photoperiodism regulates flowering in many species. Long-day plants (spinach, lettuce, irises) flower when day length exceeds a critical value (usually in late spring/summer). Short-day plants (chrysanthemums, soybeans, poinsettias) flower when day length falls below a critical value (in late summer/autumn). Surprisingly, plants actually measure the length of uninterrupted darkness, not light. A brief flash of light in the middle of the night will prevent flowering in short-day plants and induce it in long-day plants. The pigment phytochrome is the molecular sensor of day length.

  • Auxin — hormone driving cell elongation, apical dominance, phototropism.
  • Gibberellin — hormone promoting stem elongation, seed germination.
  • Cytokinin — hormone promoting cell division and lateral branching.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA) — stress hormone: closes stomata, maintains seed dormancy.
  • Ethylene — gaseous hormone that ripens fruit and promotes abscission.
  • Phototropism / gravitropism — directional growth toward light / in response to gravity.

Worked example — Explaining phototropism on a windowsill

A seedling on a sunny windowsill bends toward the window over 48 hours. Explain the cellular and molecular mechanism, naming the relevant hormone and tissue.

Light is detected by photoreceptors near the shoot apex. Auxin, made at the shoot tip, is redistributed laterally so that more auxin moves to the shaded side of the stem. Auxin loosens cell walls on the shaded side, allowing those cells to elongate more than the cells on the lit side. Because one side of the stem elongates more, the stem curves toward the light. The result is greater leaf exposure to sunlight for photosynthesis — adaptive in mixed-light environments.

  • Saying all plant hormones promote growth — ABA inhibits growth and maintains dormancy.
  • Calling phototropism a "movement" — plants do not move; they grow directionally.
  • Forgetting that ethylene is a gas — its mobility through air is why one ripe banana ripens its neighbours.
  • Saying long-day plants need long days of light — they actually require short uninterrupted darkness; phytochrome senses the dark period.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F2.3 — "investigate factors that affect plant growth, including the roles of plant hormones (auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin, abscisic acid, ethylene) and tropisms."

5.6 — Plants in Society: Agriculture & Sustainability
From wild ancestors to modern crops — and why plants are central to the future.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Plants are the foundation of human civilization. About 10 000 years ago, humans in several regions independently began the Neolithic agricultural revolution: wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent, rice and millet in East Asia, maize in Mesoamerica, potato in the Andes, sorghum and yam in Africa. Through millennia of artificial selection, wild ancestors were transformed: teosinte (a wild grass with eight tiny kernels) became modern maize with hundreds of large soft kernels; wild mustard was selected into broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, kale, and kohlrabi. Today, about a dozen crop species supply roughly 80% of human calories — a narrow base that makes food security vulnerable to climate change and emerging diseases.

Plants give us more than food. Fibres from cotton, flax, hemp, and jute make clothing and rope. Wood from pines, oaks, maples, and many other species builds shelter and supplies most of the world's paper. Medicines derived from plants include aspirin (originally from willow bark), digoxin (foxglove, for heart failure), morphine (opium poppy), quinine (cinchona, for malaria), and the cancer drug Taxol (Pacific yew). Plants also provide essential ecosystem services: oxygen production through photosynthesis, removal and storage of atmospheric \(CO_2\) (with potential to mitigate climate change), soil stabilisation, water filtration, and habitat for animals. Forests are major carbon sinks; protecting them is central to climate policy.

Modern agriculture faces real tensions. Monocultures (single-crop fields) maximise short-term yields but increase vulnerability to pests and require heavy pesticide and fertilizer inputs. Genetically modified crops such as Bt corn (carrying a bacterial gene that kills certain pest larvae) and Roundup Ready soybeans (resistant to a specific herbicide) can boost yields and reduce pesticide use; they also raise questions about resistance evolution, biodiversity loss, and the economics of seed ownership. Sustainable approaches include crop rotation, cover crops, integrated pest management, polyculture, agroforestry, and organic farming, all of which aim to maintain soil health and biodiversity over the long term. Indigenous knowledge of native plants — for food, medicine, and ecological management — has been recognised as a critical complement to scientific agriculture in Canada and elsewhere.

  • Artificial selection — selective breeding by humans for desired traits over many generations.
  • Monoculture — agricultural system planting a single crop species over a large area.
  • Genetically modified crop (GMO) — plant whose genome has been altered using recombinant DNA techniques.
  • Sustainable agriculture — farming that meets present food needs without compromising future productivity.
  • Ecosystem services — benefits ecosystems provide (oxygen, water filtration, pollination, carbon storage).
  • Carbon sink — natural reservoir that absorbs more carbon than it releases (forests, oceans, healthy soils).

Worked example — Evaluating a real agricultural choice

A Canadian farmer is considering switching half her wheat acreage to a diverse rotation including legumes (peas, beans) and cover crops. List two ecological benefits she could expect and one economic risk she should plan for.

Ecological benefits: (i) legumes host nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, enriching soil nitrogen and reducing fertilizer demand for subsequent crops; (ii) crop rotation breaks pest and disease cycles, lowering pesticide need and improving long-term soil structure. Economic risk: short-term yield or revenue may drop while the soil rebuilds and the farmer develops markets for the new crops, particularly if equipment, knowledge, or distribution channels are tailored to monoculture wheat. Government programs and crop-insurance options can buffer the transition.

  • Treating GMO crops as monolithically "good" or "bad" — every modification must be evaluated for its specific risks and benefits.
  • Forgetting that all crops are products of selection — humans have been modifying plant genomes through breeding for 10 000 years.
  • Ignoring soil health — modern agriculture's biggest long-term threat is degraded soil; cover crops and reduced tillage help rebuild it.
  • Equating "sustainable" with "organic" — sustainability is a broader concept that includes economic and social viability.

Curriculum expectation: Strand F, Expectation F1.1 — "evaluate the importance of plants to Canadian society and the economic and ecological costs and benefits of agricultural and biotechnological practices."
