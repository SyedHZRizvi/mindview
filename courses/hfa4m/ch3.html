❤️ Cardiovascular Disease
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the second leading cause of death in Canada (after cancer), with about 1 in 12 adults living with diagnosed heart disease. CVD encompasses several related conditions — coronary artery disease, hypertension, stroke, heart failure — and shares a common underlying process: atherosclerosis, the gradual build-up of cholesterol-rich plaques inside artery walls. Plaques narrow arteries, restricting blood flow, and can rupture to trigger clots that cause heart attacks (myocardial infarction) or ischaemic strokes.

Lipoproteins transport cholesterol in the blood. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) delivers cholesterol to tissues; oxidised LDL is the precursor to plaque formation, hence its label "bad cholesterol." HDL (high-density lipoprotein) retrieves cholesterol from tissues for excretion — "good cholesterol." A healthy ratio matters more than total cholesterol. Diet strongly influences these: saturated fats (animal fats, palm oil, coconut oil) and trans fats (now banned in Canada as of 2018) raise LDL; soluble fibre (oats, legumes, psyllium), plant sterols, monounsaturated fats (olive oil, avocado), and omega-3 fatty acids (fatty fish, flax, walnuts) lower LDL or raise HDL.

Sodium is a key dietary contributor to hypertension. Canadians consume about 2760 mg/day on average — well above the AI of 1500 mg/day, and most of this sodium comes from packaged and restaurant foods rather than the salt shaker. The DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet emphasises vegetables, fruits, whole grains, low-fat dairy, lean protein, nuts/seeds, and limits sodium to 2300 mg/day or less. A meta-analysis of DASH trials showed systolic blood-pressure reductions of about 11 mm Hg. The Mediterranean diet — high in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, fish, olive oil, nuts, and moderate wine — is the most extensively studied for cardiovascular protection (PREDIMED 2013 trial showed a 30% reduction in major cardiovascular events).

  • Atherosclerosis — progressive build-up of cholesterol-rich plaques in artery walls.
  • LDL vs HDL — delivers cholesterol to tissues (atherogenic) vs returns cholesterol to liver (cardioprotective).
  • Hypertension — systolic ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic ≥80 mm Hg (current Canadian thresholds).
  • DASH diet — sodium-restricted, mineral-rich pattern shown to lower blood pressure.
  • Mediterranean diet — plant-forward pattern with olive oil, fish, nuts; strong evidence for CVD protection.
  • Soluble fibre — viscous fibre (oats, legumes, psyllium) that binds bile acids and lowers LDL.

Worked example — Counting hidden sodium

A teenager's lunch: a deli ham sandwich (14 g ham = 600 mg Na; 2 slices bread = 400 mg Na; 1 slice cheddar = 180 mg Na; mustard 1 tsp = 60 mg Na); a bowl of canned chicken-noodle soup (790 mg Na); a granola bar (110 mg Na); a bottled green tea (15 mg Na). Calculate total sodium and compare to AI.

Sandwich: 600 + 400 + 180 + 60 = 1240 mg Na. Soup: 790 mg Na. Granola bar: 110 mg Na. Tea: 15 mg Na. Total = 2155 mg Na — already exceeds the 1500 mg/day AI for adolescents, and approaches the chronic-disease-risk-reduction value of 2300 mg/day, in a single meal. Most sodium came from processed components, not added salt.

  • Equating "low-fat" with "heart-healthy" — low-fat ultra-processed foods often replace fat with sugar; quality of fat matters more than quantity.
  • Avoiding all eggs because of cholesterol — for most healthy adults, dietary cholesterol from eggs has modest effect; saturated fat is a larger driver of blood LDL.
  • Forgetting potassium — potassium-rich diets (fruits, vegetables, legumes) are independently protective against hypertension.
  • Thinking only "table salt" raises sodium — about 80% of Canadian sodium intake comes from processed and restaurant foods.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "explain the relationship between diet and cardiovascular disease, including the role of saturated and trans fats, sodium, fibre, and omega-3 fatty acids."

🩸 Type 2 Diabetes
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounts for about 90% of diabetes cases in Canada and now affects roughly 3.7 million Canadians (about 10% of adults), with First Nations communities bearing a disproportionately high burden. Unlike type 1 (autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells, requiring lifelong insulin), type 2 is fundamentally a problem of insulin resistance combined with eventual β-cell failure. It is largely preventable and substantially modifiable through lifestyle.

Insulin, secreted by pancreatic β-cells, signals cells (especially muscle, liver, adipose) to take up glucose, store it as glycogen or fat, and reduce hepatic glucose output. In insulin resistance, cells respond sluggishly; the pancreas compensates by secreting more insulin (hyperinsulinaemia) until eventually it cannot keep up — blood glucose rises (prediabetes: A1C 6.0–6.4%, then diabetes: A1C ≥6.5% or fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L). Chronic hyperglycaemia damages capillaries (microvascular complications: retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and accelerates atherosclerosis (macrovascular: heart attack, stroke, peripheral artery disease leading to amputation).

Diet plays a central role in both prevention and management. The glycemic index (GI) ranks carbohydrate-containing foods by their effect on blood glucose: low-GI foods (legumes, intact whole grains, most non-starchy vegetables, most fruits) cause gentler rises; high-GI foods (white bread, white rice, sugar-sweetened beverages) cause spikes. Diabetes Canada now emphasises portfolio patterns over single nutrients: vegetable-and-fibre-rich plates, plant proteins, intact whole grains, healthy fats, and limited refined sugar. The Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP, 2002) showed that lifestyle intervention (modest weight loss, 150 min/week activity) reduced progression from prediabetes to diabetes by 58% — outperforming metformin.

  • Insulin resistance — reduced cellular response to insulin signalling; precursor of type 2 diabetes.
  • HbA1c (A1C) — glycated haemoglobin; reflects average glucose over the prior 2–3 months.
  • Glycemic index (GI) — ranking of carbohydrate foods (0–100) by their blood-glucose response.
  • Glycemic load (GL) — GI × grams of carbohydrate per serving; accounts for portion size.
  • Prediabetes — impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance preceding diabetes diagnosis.
  • Microvascular complications — diabetes-related damage to small vessels (eye, kidney, nerve).

Worked example — Designing a low-GI lunch

A newly diagnosed person with type 2 diabetes asks for a lunch idea that balances blood glucose. Build one and explain how each component contributes.

Suggestion: 1 cup lentil-vegetable soup + 1 small whole-grain pita + 2 oz baked chicken + green salad with olive-oil dressing + small apple. Lentils provide low-GI carbohydrate, plant protein, and soluble fibre that blunts post-meal glucose. Whole-grain pita has lower GI than white bread (intact grains slow digestion). Chicken provides protein with minimal glycemic effect. Olive oil supplies monounsaturated fat. Apple supplies fibre and antioxidants. Total carbohydrate ~ 50 g — within typical meal range — and glycemic load is moderate.

  • Equating diabetes with "no sugar at all" — moderate, portion-controlled carbohydrate at meals is acceptable; quality and quantity matter.
  • Treating "diet drinks" as a magic fix — they reduce sugar but evidence on metabolic outcomes is mixed; water and unsweetened beverages are preferred.
  • Believing type 2 diabetes only affects older adults — paediatric type 2 cases are rising sharply, especially in high-risk populations.
  • Forgetting that physical activity has insulin-like effects independent of weight — muscle contraction increases glucose uptake.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "explain the relationship between diet and type 2 diabetes, including the role of refined carbohydrates, fibre, glycemic index, and physical activity."

🎗️ Cancer & Nutrition
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

About 4 in 10 Canadians will develop cancer in their lifetime, and the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF) estimates that 30–40% of cancers are linked to lifestyle factors including diet, body weight, and physical activity. While no single food causes or cures cancer, dietary patterns and specific exposures meaningfully shift risk.

Strong evidence (WCRF and the International Agency for Research on Cancer, IARC) links the following to increased risk: processed meat (Group 1 carcinogen as of IARC 2015 — sausages, ham, bacon, hot dogs; about an 18% increased colorectal-cancer risk per 50 g/day); red meat (Group 2A probable carcinogen — beef, pork, lamb); alcohol (Group 1 — even moderate intake raises breast-, oral-, oesophageal-, liver-, and colorectal-cancer risk; the 2023 Canadian Guidance on Alcohol and Health states no amount is safe); high BMI/excess body fat (linked to at least 13 cancers); high consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages (via obesity); aflatoxin (mold contamination of poorly stored grains, peanuts — major risk in some low-income settings).

Protective dietary factors include: fibre-rich foods (whole grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables; lower colorectal cancer risk); non-starchy vegetables and fruit (rich in fibre, vitamins, polyphenols, glucosinolates in cruciferous vegetables); physical activity; breastfeeding (protects mother against breast and ovarian cancer); and maintaining healthy body weight. The Cancer Care Ontario MyCancerIQ tool helps Ontarians estimate their personal modifiable risk.

"Antioxidant" supplements have not lived up to early hopes: the CARET and ATBC trials of β-carotene supplements actually increased lung-cancer rates in smokers. Lesson — antioxidants from whole foods within complex food matrices (broccoli, berries, tea) appear protective, but isolated mega-doses do not.

  • IARC carcinogen classification — Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans), 2A (probably), 2B (possibly), 3 (not classifiable).
  • Processed meat — meat preserved by smoking, curing, salting, or chemical preservatives (Group 1 colorectal carcinogen).
  • Cruciferous vegetables — broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, kale; rich in glucosinolates linked to lower cancer risk.
  • Aflatoxin — toxin from Aspergillus mold; potent liver carcinogen.
  • BMI — body mass index (kg/m²); a population-level screening tool, imperfect at the individual level.
  • Polyphenols — diverse plant compounds (flavonoids, stilbenes, lignans) with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

Worked example — Evaluating cancer-risk claims

A social-media post claims "drinking lemon water every morning cures cancer." Evaluate this claim using evidence-based reasoning.

(1) Source: anonymous social-media post — no peer-reviewed citation. (2) Plausibility: lemon contains vitamin C, citric acid, and polyphenols; pleasant but no curative mechanism is biologically plausible. (3) Evidence: no randomised controlled trials show lemon prevents or cures cancer. (4) Risk of harm: delays effective treatment, may cause dental erosion. (5) Conclusion: claim unsupported. Reliable sources: Canadian Cancer Society, WCRF, IARC. Encourage skepticism toward simple "miracle food" claims.

  • Calling any food "cancer-causing" in isolation — risk depends on pattern and dose, not single exposures.
  • Confusing Group 1 (carcinogenic) with "guaranteed to cause cancer" — IARC classification is hazard, not magnitude of risk.
  • Believing supplements equal whole foods — high-dose antioxidant supplements have caused harm in trials.
  • Forgetting alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen — the breast-cancer risk increases linearly with intake.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.3 — "explain the relationship between diet and cancer, including the influence of fibre, processed meat, alcohol, and antioxidants."

⚖️ Obesity, Osteoporosis & Other Diet-Related Issues
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Obesity is defined clinically by BMI ≥30 kg/m² and increasingly by health complications rather than weight alone. The 2020 Canadian Adult Obesity Clinical Practice Guideline reframes obesity as a complex chronic disease driven by genetic, biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors — not a personal failing. Sustained intentional weight loss of 5–10% improves blood pressure, glucose, lipids, and quality of life. Effective treatment is multimodal: nutrition therapy (registered dietitian, ideally), physical activity, cognitive-behavioural therapy, pharmacotherapy (e.g., GLP-1 agonists like semaglutide), and bariatric surgery for severe obesity. The guideline also urges health-care providers to reduce weight stigma, which itself harms physical and mental health.

Osteoporosis is the loss of bone density and structural deterioration of bone tissue, leading to fragility fractures. About 1 in 4 women and 1 in 8 men over 50 in Canada have osteoporosis; hip fractures alone carry a 20% one-year mortality. Diet contributors to prevention: calcium (1000 mg/day adults 19–50; 1200 mg/day women >50, men >70), vitamin D (600 IU 1–70 years, 800 IU >70 years; Health Canada recommends a daily supplement of 400 IU for everyone over 50), protein (resistance for muscle and bone), vitamin K, magnesium, potassium, and limited sodium and excessive caffeine. Weight-bearing and resistance exercise build and maintain bone. Modifiable risk factors include smoking, excess alcohol, sedentary lifestyle, and low body weight.

Other diet-related issues: iron-deficiency anaemia (the world's most common micronutrient deficiency, affecting menstruating individuals especially); dental caries (frequent sugar exposure feeding cariogenic bacteria like Streptococcus mutans); non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (linked to excess fructose, sugar-sweetened beverages, abdominal obesity); gout (hyperuricaemia worsened by alcohol, fructose, and high-purine foods).

  • BMI thresholds — underweight <18.5; normal 18.5–24.9; overweight 25–29.9; obesity ≥30 kg/m².
  • Visceral adiposity — fat around abdominal organs; more metabolically harmful than subcutaneous fat.
  • Osteoporosis vs osteopenia — bone density T-score ≤−2.5 vs −1.0 to −2.5 (less severe).
  • Bone mineral density (BMD) — measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA).
  • Weight stigma — discrimination based on body weight; itself associated with worse health outcomes.
  • GLP-1 agonist — class of drugs (semaglutide, liraglutide) used for type 2 diabetes and obesity; mimics gut hormone.

Worked example — Estimating BMI and counselling appropriately

An adult, 1.65 m tall, weighs 78 kg. Calculate her BMI, interpret cautiously, and outline next steps.

BMI = \(\frac{78}{1.65^2} = \frac{78}{2.7225} \approx 28.7 \text{ kg/m}^2\) — within the "overweight" range. Cautions: BMI does not distinguish muscle from fat; ethnic-specific cutoffs differ (lower thresholds for many Asian populations); does not account for fat distribution. Next steps: review her overall health (BP, A1C, lipids, function, mental health), discuss her own goals, focus on health behaviours (movement, sleep, balanced eating, social connection) rather than on weight as the only target. Refer to a registered dietitian if dietary change is the priority. Avoid weight-stigmatising language.

  • Treating BMI as a precise individual diagnosis — it is a population screening tool with significant individual variability.
  • Equating "thin" with "healthy" — metabolically unhealthy normal-weight (MUNW) is a recognised pattern.
  • Recommending dairy-heavy diets without considering lactose tolerance or cultural fit — calcium can come from many sources (fortified plant milks, leafy greens, tofu, sardines).
  • Believing "willpower" determines weight outcomes — biological and environmental factors are dominant; framing matters for both effective care and dignity.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "describe the relationships between diet and various health issues, including obesity, osteoporosis, and other chronic conditions."

🦠 Foodborne Illness & Food Safety
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Public Health Agency of Canada estimates about 4 million cases of foodborne illness in Canada each year (1 in 8 Canadians), causing 11,600 hospitalisations and 238 deaths. Foodborne illness is largely preventable through correct handling, storage, and cooking. The most common pathogens in Canada are Norovirus (the leading cause), Campylobacter, non-typhoidal Salmonella, and pathogenic Escherichia coli (especially O157:H7). Vulnerable populations include pregnant women (listeriosis), infants, older adults, and the immunocompromised.

The "temperature danger zone" — between 4 °C and 60 °C — is the temperature range in which most pathogenic bacteria multiply rapidly. The food-safety mantra "keep cold foods cold (≤4 °C) and hot foods hot (≥60 °C)" minimises time in this zone. Specific cooking temperatures matter: poultry to internal 74 °C; ground beef to 71 °C; whole cuts of beef/pork to 63 °C (with 3-minute rest); fish to 63 °C; reheated leftovers to 74 °C. The "2-hour/4-hour rule" guides perishable food at room temperature: under 2 hours — safe to refrigerate; 2–4 hours — use immediately; over 4 hours — discard.

The four key principles for safe food handling are clean, separate, cook, chill. (1) Wash hands for 20 s before and after handling food; sanitise surfaces. (2) Keep raw meat separate from ready-to-eat foods — separate cutting boards and storage. (3) Cook to recommended internal temperatures, verified with a thermometer. (4) Refrigerate within 2 hours. HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is the systematic food-industry framework for identifying biological, chemical, and physical hazards at each step and setting critical control limits. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) administers Canada's food-safety regulations, and provincial public-health units inspect restaurants.

  • Temperature danger zone — 4–60 °C, the range in which bacteria multiply rapidly.
  • Cross-contamination — transfer of pathogens from one food (often raw) to another (often ready-to-eat).
  • HACCP — Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points; systematic food-safety framework.
  • Pasteurisation — heat treatment (typically 72 °C × 15 s) that kills most pathogens in milk and juice.
  • Listeriosis — serious infection from Listeria monocytogenes, especially dangerous in pregnancy.
  • Botulism — paralytic toxin produced by Clostridium botulinum; risk in improperly canned low-acid foods and honey for infants.

Worked example — Diagnosing what went wrong at a family barbecue

Six guests develop diarrhea and abdominal cramps 12 hours after a backyard barbecue. They ate chicken kebabs, potato salad, and brownies. Outline plausible causes and which best fits.

Suspect: Campylobacter or Salmonella from undercooked chicken, OR Staphylococcus aureus toxin from potato salad left in the danger zone. Symptoms appearing 12 h after meal fit both — Campy/Salmonella incubation is typically 12–72 h. The chicken cooked from a single grill while raw on a shared cutting board is suspicious for cross-contamination. Potato salad sitting on a hot patio for 3 h is also suspicious. Recommend: report to local public-health unit; preserve any leftovers for testing; investigate handling timeline; rehydrate affected guests; medical attention if bloody stool, high fever, or dehydration.

  • Believing visible cleanliness equals safety — pathogens are microscopic; appearance, smell, and taste are unreliable indicators.
  • Trusting "thaw on the counter" — surface thaws into the danger zone while interior is still frozen; thaw in the fridge or in cold water.
  • Saying "I'll just heat it well, it's fine" — pre-formed toxins (Staph, botulinum) survive cooking.
  • Forgetting hand-washing is the single most effective preventive measure.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.4 — "describe the causes and consequences of foodborne illness and the safe handling, preparation, and storage practices that prevent it."
