🤰 Pregnancy & Lactation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Maternal nutrition is one of the strongest predictors of pregnancy outcome. The principle is "nutrition for two — quality, not quantity, doubled": while energy needs only modestly rise (about +340 kcal/day in the second trimester and +452 kcal/day in the third trimester for a healthy-weight woman), needs for several specific nutrients climb sharply, and a deficit during sensitive developmental windows can have permanent effects on the fetus.

Folate (vitamin B9) is the headline nutrient. Adequate folate before conception and during the first 28 days post-conception reduces neural-tube defects (spina bifida, anencephaly) by roughly 70%. Because the neural tube closes before most women know they are pregnant, Health Canada recommends that all women who could become pregnant take a daily supplement of 0.4 mg folic acid in addition to a folate-rich diet (dark green leafy vegetables, legumes, fortified grain products — Canada has mandated folic-acid fortification of white flour since 1998). The RDA jumps to 600 µg DFE during pregnancy.

Iron needs nearly double during pregnancy (to 27 mg/day) to support maternal blood-volume expansion and fetal iron stores; iron-deficiency anaemia in pregnancy is linked to preterm birth and low birth weight. Calcium and vitamin D are critical for fetal skeletal development; current recommendations are 1000 mg calcium and 600 IU vitamin D/day. Iodine supports fetal brain development (severe deficiency causes cretinism, a preventable form of intellectual disability); RDA rises to 220 µg/day. DHA (an omega-3 from fatty fish) supports retinal and cognitive development. Foods to avoid: unpasteurised dairy (listeria), undercooked meat or fish (toxoplasmosis, parasites), high-mercury fish (shark, swordfish, king mackerel), raw eggs (salmonella), excess vitamin A from supplements (teratogenic), and any alcohol (FASD — fetal alcohol spectrum disorder is preventable only by abstaining).

Lactation raises energy needs to about +330 kcal/day above pre-pregnancy. Breast milk composition is remarkable: it changes from colostrum (first 3–5 days, rich in antibodies and oligosaccharides) to mature milk that adapts to the infant's age. The WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months. Maternal nutrient deficiencies generally affect the mother first (her stores deplete to maintain milk quality), but some nutrients (iodine, B12, vitamin D, DHA) do vary with maternal intake.

  • Neural-tube defect (NTD) — failure of the embryonic neural tube to close in the first weeks of pregnancy; folate-preventable.
  • Folic acid fortification — mandatory addition of synthetic folate to white flour, enriched pasta, and corn meal in Canada since 1998.
  • FASD — fetal alcohol spectrum disorder; no safe amount of alcohol is established during pregnancy.
  • Colostrum — first breast milk, dense in immunoglobulin A and lactoferrin.
  • Gestational diabetes — glucose intolerance first recognised during pregnancy; managed by diet, activity, and (when needed) insulin.
  • Pica — craving for non-food substances (ice, clay, starch) sometimes associated with iron deficiency in pregnancy.

Worked example — Recommending a prenatal nutrient plan

A 28-year-old woman is planning pregnancy. Her current diet includes 2 servings of whole-grain bread, a salad of spinach and chickpeas, salmon twice a week, and dairy daily. List three pre-conception nutrient recommendations and justify each.

(1) Begin a daily 0.4 mg folic acid supplement at least 2–3 months before conception to reduce NTD risk (her diet is decent in folate but supplementation provides reliable protection during the critical pre-tube-closure window). (2) Confirm iron status with bloodwork — if ferritin is low, supplement to build stores before pregnancy demands rise. (3) Choose low-mercury fish (her salmon choice is excellent — <0.04 ppm mercury) and continue twice-weekly for DHA. Also: limit caffeine to <200 mg/day, avoid alcohol entirely, and ensure adequate vitamin D (especially over a Canadian winter).

  • "Eating for two" interpreted as doubling kilocalories — caloric needs rise only modestly; nutrient density matters far more.
  • Starting folate when pregnancy is confirmed — by then the critical window is over.
  • Believing all fish is bad in pregnancy — low-mercury fish (salmon, sardines, light canned tuna) are recommended; only high-mercury species should be avoided.
  • Treating breast milk as a constant — composition changes minute-to-minute (foremilk vs hindmilk) and day-to-day.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "describe the nutritional needs of women during pregnancy and lactation, including those of teenage mothers."

🍼 Infant & Toddler Nutrition
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The first 1000 days of life — from conception to the second birthday — are a window during which nutrition has lifelong effects on growth, cognitive development, and chronic-disease risk. Health Canada, the Canadian Paediatric Society, the Dietitians of Canada, and Breastfeeding Committee for Canada jointly recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, with continued breastfeeding alongside complementary foods for up to two years and beyond.

Breast milk is uniquely suited to the human infant. It supplies easily-digested whey-dominant protein, lactose (which supports calcium absorption and gut flora), DHA (brain development), human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs that feed beneficial bifidobacteria), and protective factors including secretory IgA, lactoferrin, lysozyme, and live immune cells. It also adapts: colostrum in the first days is high in immunoglobulins; transitional and mature milk provide ongoing immune protection. Breastfeeding reduces the infant's risk of otitis media, gastroenteritis, respiratory infections, SIDS, and later development of obesity and type 1 diabetes, and confers long-term maternal benefits (reduced ovarian and pre-menopausal breast cancer risk).

When breastfeeding is not possible, commercial iron-fortified infant formula based on cow's milk is the alternative. Cow's milk itself should not be a main beverage until 9–12 months — it has too much sodium and protein, too little iron and vitamin C, and can cause occult intestinal blood loss. Vitamin D supplementation of 400 IU/day is recommended for all breastfed infants (and double for infants in the far north or born in winter), because breast milk supplies very little vitamin D.

Complementary feeding begins around 6 months, signalled by developmental readiness (good head control, sitting with support, showing interest in food, loss of tongue-thrust reflex). The 2019 Health Canada guidance moved away from rigid food-by-food schedules: iron-rich foods first (meat, poultry, fish, well-cooked egg, fortified cereal, legumes), and a variety of textures introduced gradually. Allergenic foods (peanut, egg, dairy, wheat, soy, tree nuts, fish, shellfish, sesame) should be introduced around 6 months rather than delayed — the LEAP study (NEJM 2015) showed early peanut introduction in high-risk infants reduced peanut allergy at age 5 by ~80%. Avoid: honey before 12 months (botulism), choking hazards (whole nuts, grapes, popcorn, hard raw vegetables), juice and sugar-sweetened drinks (no nutritional advantage), and added salt.

  • Exclusive breastfeeding — only breast milk, with no other liquids or solids (except medications, vitamin drops).
  • Complementary feeding — introduction of foods alongside continued breast milk or formula, beginning around 6 months.
  • Iron-fortified infant formula — commercial formula meeting Canadian regulatory standards; the recommended alternative when breast milk is unavailable.
  • LEAP study — landmark trial showing early peanut introduction reduces allergy.
  • Failure to thrive — inadequate growth in infancy, often nutritional in origin.
  • SIDS — sudden infant death syndrome; risk reduced by breastfeeding and safe-sleep practices.

Worked example — Evaluating infant feeding at 4 months

A 4-month-old breastfed infant is on track developmentally. The mother asks whether she should introduce solids "to make him sleep through the night." Counsel her.

Early introduction of solids is not recommended before 6 months. Research does not support that solids improve sleep, and early introduction can displace breast milk (lowering immune protection), expose immature kidneys and gut to inappropriate foods, and increase obesity and chronic-disease risk. Continue exclusive breastfeeding to 6 months and introduce complementary foods at 6 months when developmental signs appear. Vitamin D 400 IU/day supplement remains essential.

  • Recommending cow's milk before 9–12 months — it displaces iron-rich foods and can cause iron-deficiency anaemia.
  • Restricting allergenic foods to delay them past 12 months — current evidence supports early introduction (around 6 months) for most infants.
  • Giving honey to infants under 12 months — risk of infant botulism.
  • Using "diet drinks" or skim milk for a toddler — fat is essential for brain development in early years.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "describe the nutritional needs of infants and young children, including the role of breastfeeding and the introduction of solid foods."

🧒 Children, Adolescents & Adults
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

After infancy, nutritional needs continue to evolve. Growth slows in early childhood and accelerates again at adolescence; activity and habits laid down in these years often carry into adulthood. The Ontario School Food and Beverage Policy (PPM 150) regulates what schools may sell, an example of policy responding to nutritional needs in this stage.

Children (1–8 years) grow steadily but at a slower pace than infancy. Iron and calcium remain priorities; iron-deficiency anaemia among toddlers transitioning off iron-rich foods to whole cow's milk is common (cow's milk has very little iron and inhibits absorption from other foods). Vitamin D supplementation continues for breastfed toddlers in winter. Picky eating is normal: appetite varies day to day, food preferences require repeated exposure (10–15 tastings on average), and a child's role is to choose whether and how much to eat from what is offered (Satter's Division of Responsibility). Encourage family meals; limit ultra-processed snacks and sugar-sweetened beverages; limit fruit juice to ≤125 mL/day.

Adolescents (9–18 years) experience a growth spurt requiring increased energy, protein, calcium, and iron. RDA for calcium peaks at 1300 mg/day in adolescence to support skeletal accretion — 90% of peak bone mass is reached by age 18. Iron RDA rises to 15 mg/day for menstruating females. Adolescent eating patterns become more variable: irregular meals, skipped breakfasts, increased away-from-home eating, more independence. Concerns include energy drinks (excess caffeine), sugar-sweetened beverages, and the influence of social media on body image and disordered eating. The Canadian Paediatric Society advises no more than 100 mg caffeine/day for adolescents (about one cup of coffee).

Adults (19–50 years) need to maintain rather than build. BMR slowly declines, so energy needs fall while micronutrient needs persist. Pregnancy and lactation overlay specific needs (Strand C1.1). Common issues include weight creep (sedentary lifestyle, takeaway/restaurant meals), insufficient fibre and vegetable intake, and inadequate physical activity. The Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity per week. The combination of healthful diet and activity prevents most chronic disease; conversely, the Global Burden of Disease Study consistently ranks dietary risk among the leading causes of preventable death globally.

  • Peak bone mass — the maximum amount of bone tissue achieved, typically by age 18–25.
  • Division of Responsibility (Satter) — parents decide what, when, and where; the child decides whether and how much to eat.
  • School Food and Beverage Policy (PPM 150) — Ontario regulation governing food and beverages sold in schools (effective 2011).
  • Sedentary behaviour — waking activities with low energy expenditure while sitting, reclining, or lying.
  • Adolescent growth spurt — rapid increase in height and weight typically beginning at 10–12 in females and 12–14 in males.
  • Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines — integrated recommendations for physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep across the lifespan.

Worked example — Evaluating a Grade 9 student's lunch

A 14-year-old male brings: a bottled iced tea (350 mL, 35 g sugar), a sausage roll, a chocolate bar, and an apple. Identify two strengths and three areas to improve. Suggest one swap that improves nutrient density without restricting the student's autonomy.

Strengths: includes a fruit (apple — fibre, vitamin C, water); includes some protein (sausage). Areas to improve: ~70 g added sugar between iced tea and chocolate bar exceeds WHO's 10% of daily energy target; very low calcium; sausage roll high in saturated fat and sodium. Swap: replace iced tea with white milk or water (calcium, hydration, eliminates ~35 g sugar). Avoid moralising the chocolate; offer choices.

  • Treating children's small appetites as a problem — appetite is self-regulating and varies with growth rate and activity.
  • Counting fruit juice as "fruit" servings — it lacks fibre and is energy-dense; whole fruit is preferable.
  • Forgetting that adolescents need more calcium than adults — peak bone mass is built now.
  • Equating "eating clean" with rigid restriction — adolescents are vulnerable to disordered eating from extreme food rules.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "describe the nutritional needs of children, adolescents, and adults at various life stages and identify the implications of meeting or not meeting those needs."

👵 Older Adult Nutrition
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

By 2030 about one in four Canadians will be over 65, and meeting older adults' nutritional needs is a public-health priority. Energy requirements decline (less lean tissue, lower activity, lower BMR), but most nutrient requirements stay the same or even rise — a phenomenon called increased nutrient density requirement. Older adults need to eat less but better.

Several physiological changes alter nutrition. Sarcopenia — age-related loss of muscle mass and strength — begins around age 30 and accelerates after 60. Resistance exercise and adequate protein (1.0–1.2 g/kg/day, more than the 0.8 g/kg of younger adults) help slow it. Reduced gastric acid impairs vitamin B12 absorption, so the RDA shifts to recommend B12 from fortified foods or supplements rather than from animal foods alone. Reduced thirst sensation raises dehydration risk; the Mifflin–St Jeor and Holliday–Segar approaches both apply to fluid needs. Reduced skin synthesis of vitamin D and less sun exposure mean RDA rises to 800 IU/day for adults over 70 (and Health Canada now recommends a supplement of 400 IU/day for everyone over 50). Calcium RDA also rises (1200 mg/day for women >50 and men >70) to slow osteoporosis. Loss of taste and smell, dental issues, polypharmacy (drug–nutrient interactions), depression, social isolation, fixed income, and difficulty cooking all influence intake.

The Canadian Malnutrition Task Force estimates that 45% of hospitalised older adults are malnourished on admission; in long-term care, the figure is 30–60%. Screening tools like the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) identify risk; interventions include enriched menus, family meal participation, social meal programs (e.g., Meals on Wheels), and aspiration-safe textures (IDDSI framework) for those with dysphagia.

  • Sarcopenia — progressive age-related loss of muscle mass, strength, and function.
  • Nutrient density — concentration of nutrients per kilocalorie; rises in importance as energy needs fall.
  • Polypharmacy — concurrent use of multiple medications, common in older adults; raises risk of drug–nutrient interactions.
  • Dysphagia — difficulty swallowing; common in stroke, Parkinson's, dementia; requires modified-texture diets.
  • Osteoporosis — loss of bone density leading to fragility fractures; vitamin D, calcium, and weight-bearing activity are protective.
  • IDDSI framework — International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative; common terminology for modified-texture foods and thickened liquids.

Worked example — Designing a nutrient-dense breakfast for a 78-year-old

An older adult with a small appetite reports skipping breakfast. Design one breakfast that meets ~1/3 of her protein needs (assume 65 kg × 1.2 g/kg = 78 g protein/day, so target ~26 g) and provides calcium, vitamin D, fibre, and B12.

Option: 200 mL fortified soy milk + ½ cup Greek yogurt with berries + a slice of whole-grain toast with peanut butter + a vitamin D supplement (400 IU). Protein: soy milk 7 g + Greek yogurt 12 g + bread 4 g + peanut butter 4 g = 27 g. Calcium from soy milk (300 mg) + yogurt (~200 mg). Fibre from berries + whole grain + peanut butter ~6 g. B12 fortified in soy milk; dairy yogurt provides additional B12. Small portions but high nutrient density.

  • Treating older adults as a homogeneous group — needs vary widely with health, activity, and living situation.
  • Recommending the same protein RDA (0.8 g/kg) as younger adults — current evidence supports 1.0–1.2 g/kg for older adults.
  • Forgetting B12 absorption issues — chronic low B12 causes irreversible neurological damage in older adults.
  • Equating "low appetite" with "low needs" — most nutrient needs persist; energy density and nutrient density must be balanced carefully.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "describe the nutritional needs of children, adolescents, and adults at various life stages and identify the implications of meeting or not meeting those needs."

🧠 Eating Disorders
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Eating disorders (EDs) are serious mental-health conditions with the second-highest mortality rate of any psychiatric illness (after opioid use disorder). They affect roughly 1 million Canadians at any given time and can begin at any age, although most onset is in adolescence and early adulthood. The DSM-5 recognises several distinct disorders: anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), and other specified feeding or eating disorders (OSFED).

Anorexia nervosa is characterised by restriction of energy intake relative to needs leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, and disturbance in the way one's body weight or shape is experienced. Physical sequelae include amenorrhea, lanugo (fine body hair), bradycardia, hypothermia, osteoporosis, and electrolyte disturbance — refeeding must be cautious to avoid refeeding syndrome (dangerous shifts in phosphate, potassium, magnesium when carbohydrate is reintroduced). Bulimia nervosa involves recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by compensatory behaviours (self-induced vomiting, laxative misuse, fasting, excessive exercise); body weight may be normal, making detection harder. Complications include parotid swelling, dental erosion, oesophageal tears, and hypokalemia. Binge-eating disorder involves recurrent binge eating without regular compensatory behaviour; it is the most common ED and is associated with overweight/obesity and metabolic complications.

Causes are multifactorial: genetic susceptibility, psychological factors (perfectionism, low self-esteem, trauma), sociocultural pressures (thinness as ideal, weight stigma), and family factors. The internet and social-media exposure to "thinspiration" or extreme dietary content is a documented modern risk factor. Treatment is multidisciplinary — medical stabilisation, nutritional rehabilitation by a registered dietitian, psychotherapy (CBT-E, family-based therapy for adolescents), and pharmacotherapy when indicated. Early intervention dramatically improves outcomes. The National Initiative for Eating Disorders (NIED) and the National Eating Disorder Information Centre (NEDIC) at Toronto General Hospital are Canadian resources.

  • Anorexia nervosa — restriction of intake, significantly low weight, intense fear of weight gain.
  • Bulimia nervosa — recurrent binges with compensatory behaviour (purging, fasting, exercise).
  • Binge-eating disorder — recurrent binges without regular compensatory behaviour.
  • ARFID — avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder; not driven by body-image concerns but by sensory aversion, fear of consequences, or lack of interest.
  • Refeeding syndrome — potentially fatal electrolyte and fluid shifts when carbohydrate is reintroduced to a malnourished patient.
  • Weight stigma — discrimination and stereotyping based on body weight; itself a risk factor for disordered eating.

Worked example — Recognising warning signs

A high-school dance student begins skipping lunch, weighs herself daily, posts before-and-after photos on social media, and explains her tiredness as "training hard." She has lost 8 kg in 3 months. Identify three warning signs and outline next steps.

Warning signs: significant rapid weight loss; meal restriction (skipping lunch); preoccupation with weight and body image (frequent weighing, social-media posts); over-exercise framed as discipline. Next steps: a non-judgmental conversation focused on her well-being (not her weight); referral to her physician for medical screening (including vitals, electrolytes, ECG); connection to a registered dietitian and a mental-health professional with eating-disorder training; access to school supports; share contact info for NEDIC (1-866-NEDIC-20) and Kids Help Phone.

  • Believing eating disorders only affect thin young women — they affect all genders, all body sizes, and all ages.
  • Praising weight loss when the cause is unclear — comments on body size can reinforce disordered behaviour.
  • Treating eating disorders as a "phase" — they have one of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric illnesses.
  • Refeeding aggressively — refeeding syndrome can be fatal; start at low energy and increase gradually under medical supervision.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.3 — "explain the warning signs and impact of eating disorders (e.g., anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder) and identify community resources and prevention strategies."
