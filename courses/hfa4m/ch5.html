🌍 Global Hunger & Malnutrition
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The 2024 UN State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI) report estimates that 733 million people — about 1 in 11 worldwide and 1 in 5 in Africa — faced chronic hunger in 2023. The number rose sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic and Russia's invasion of Ukraine and has plateaued at levels seen 15 years ago. Hunger is highly unevenly distributed: sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South Asia, and conflict-affected regions of the Middle East bear the heaviest burden, while in many high-income countries hunger is hidden inside otherwise food-rich societies.

Undernutrition takes several forms. Stunting (low height-for-age) reflects chronic undernutrition during the first 1000 days and affects 148 million children under five globally; stunted children have impaired cognitive development, reduced productivity, and higher risk of chronic disease as adults. Wasting (low weight-for-height) reflects acute undernutrition and affects about 45 million children. Underweight (low weight-for-age) combines both. Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) appears in two clinical forms: kwashiorkor (severe protein deficit despite some calorie intake, characterised by oedema — swollen belly — and reddish hair colour change) and marasmus (severe overall energy deficit, characterised by extreme thinness and muscle wasting). Hidden hunger — micronutrient deficiency in the absence of overall energy deficit — affects roughly 2 billion people globally. The "big three" are iron-deficiency anaemia (1.6 billion people), vitamin A deficiency (250 million preschool children; leading preventable cause of childhood blindness), and iodine deficiency (results in cretinism in the absence of fortification).

The drivers of global hunger are well-understood. Conflict is the largest single driver — wars in Syria, Yemen, South Sudan, Ukraine, Sudan, and Gaza have displaced people, destroyed harvests, and broken supply chains. Climate shocks — droughts, floods, cyclones — destroy crops and infrastructure. Economic inequality within countries means food is unaffordable for the poorest. Gender inequality matters because women produce up to 80% of food in many low-income agricultural communities but face systematic barriers to land, credit, education, and market access. The FAO Food Price Index tracks international prices of cereals, oils, dairy, meat, and sugar — and 2022 set records, contributing to a surge of food insecurity.

  • Chronic hunger / undernourishment — habitual food intake insufficient to provide enough energy for a healthy life.
  • Stunting / wasting / underweight — chronic undernutrition / acute undernutrition / combined indicator.
  • Kwashiorkor / marasmus — protein-predominant deficit (oedema, hair changes) / overall energy deficit (muscle wasting).
  • Hidden hunger — micronutrient deficiency (iron, vitamin A, iodine, zinc) even when calorie intake is adequate.
  • Famine — extreme food scarcity affecting a region, formally declared at IPC Phase 5; rare but devastating.
  • IPC (Integrated Food-Security Phase Classification) — global five-phase scale used by FAO and UN agencies.

Worked example — Reading the IPC scale

A region is classified as IPC Phase 4 (Emergency). What does this mean, what response is needed, and how does it differ from Phase 5 (Famine)?

IPC Phase 4 means at least 20% of households face an extreme food shortage with very high acute malnutrition and excess mortality. Households cannot meet food needs without resorting to emergency strategies (selling assets, depending on humanitarian aid). Response: urgent humanitarian food assistance, treatment for severe acute malnutrition, livelihood support, address conflict/access constraints. Phase 5 (Famine) raises the threshold: at least 20% of households face an extreme lack of food, severe acute malnutrition ≥30%, and crude mortality ≥2 per 10,000 per day. Famine is rare and represents catastrophic system failure — typically requires conflict, blocked access, or environmental collapse acting together.

  • Believing global hunger is mainly a food-supply problem — the world produces enough kilocalories; distribution, conflict, and inequality drive shortages.
  • Equating undernutrition with low BMI alone — stunting can be missed if only weight is checked; height-for-age catches it.
  • Treating hidden hunger as a minor issue — micronutrient deficiency causes huge cognitive and health losses despite adequate kilocalories.
  • Forgetting that famine is technically declared — most acute crises are Phase 3–4, not yet Phase 5.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "explain the difference between hunger, undernutrition, and malnutrition, and analyse global patterns and trends."

🌡️ Climate Change & Food Systems
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The food system is both a victim and a driver of climate change. The IPCC (2019, 2023) estimates that food systems contribute roughly 21–37% of total global anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions, when production, land-use change, processing, transport, packaging, retail, consumption, and waste are all counted. Agriculture is the largest single user of land and freshwater on Earth. At the same time, agriculture is one of the most exposed sectors to climate change: heat waves, drought, floods, shifting growing seasons, pest range expansion, and ocean changes (warming, acidification, deoxygenation) are already cutting yields and disrupting fisheries.

Within food, emissions vary by orders of magnitude across products. The most-cited synthesis is Poore & Nemecek (Science 2018), a meta-analysis of about 40,000 farms in 119 countries. Per kg of food, beef from beef herds emits about 60 kg CO₂-equivalent; lamb 24; cheese 21; pork 7; poultry 6; eggs 4.5; rice 4; tofu 2.0; nuts 0.3; root vegetables 0.4; peas 0.9. Ruminant livestock (cattle, sheep) are uniquely high because of enteric methane (a potent short-lived greenhouse gas) and the land required for grazing or feed. The EAT-Lancet Commission (2019) proposed a "planetary health diet": about half the plate vegetables and fruits, mostly whole grains, modest plant proteins, modest fish and poultry, low red meat, and very limited added sugar — designed to feed 10 billion people sustainably within planetary boundaries.

Adaptation in agriculture includes drought-tolerant crops, water-efficient irrigation, agroforestry, soil-health practices to retain moisture, livestock breed change, and shifting growing zones. Mitigation strategies include reducing methane (improved feed, additives like 3-NOP/Bovaer, manure management), restoring soil carbon, reducing food waste (the third-largest emissions category if it were a country), reducing emissions from synthetic nitrogen fertiliser (production and N₂O release), and reducing land-use change (deforestation for soy and palm oil). Consumers in high-income countries can shift toward plant-forward eating, reduce food waste, and choose seasonal/local where the production method warrants.

  • Greenhouse gas (GHG) — gases that trap heat in Earth's atmosphere (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O); methane is ~80× as potent as CO₂ over 20 years.
  • Enteric methane — methane produced by microbial fermentation in ruminant digestive systems (mostly cattle).
  • EAT-Lancet planetary health diet — reference diet (2019) for human and planetary health within boundaries.
  • Carbon footprint — total greenhouse-gas emissions associated with a product or activity, expressed as CO₂-equivalent.
  • Food miles — distance food travels from production to consumer; one (often minor) component of total carbon footprint.
  • Agroforestry — integration of trees into farming systems to provide shade, nitrogen, soil stabilisation, and additional income.

Worked example — Estimating dietary carbon savings

A student eats beef three times a week (each meal contains 150 g beef). She is considering swapping two of those meals for legume-based meals (150 g cooked lentils). Estimate her weekly carbon-emission reduction using Poore & Nemecek factors: beef ≈ 60 kg CO₂eq/kg, lentils ≈ 0.9 kg CO₂eq/kg.

Current beef emissions: \(3 \times 0.15 \text{ kg} \times 60 \approx 27 \text{ kg CO}_2\text{eq/week}\). New plan: \(1 \times 0.15 \text{ kg} \times 60 + 2 \times 0.15 \text{ kg} \times 0.9 \approx 9 + 0.27 \approx 9.3 \text{ kg CO}_2\text{eq/week}\). Savings: about \(27 - 9.3 \approx 17.7 \text{ kg CO}_2\text{eq/week}\), or roughly 920 kg/year — roughly equivalent to driving 3,700 km in a gasoline car. The change also typically reduces saturated fat and adds fibre.

  • Believing local food is always lower-carbon — production method usually outweighs transport; greenhouse-grown local tomatoes can have a higher footprint than imported field tomatoes.
  • Conflating organic with low-carbon — organic systems may use more land per kg of output.
  • Ignoring food waste — wasted food represents wasted emissions, water, and land; reducing waste is among the highest-leverage individual actions.
  • Treating individual diet change as the only lever — policy (carbon pricing, agricultural subsidies, methane regulations) and industry change are equally critical.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "analyse the impact of climate change on agriculture and food security and evaluate strategies that the food system can adopt to mitigate and adapt."

🌱 Sustainable Agriculture & Food Waste
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Sustainable agriculture seeks to meet today's food needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. The FAO (1989) frames sustainability across three dimensions: ecological soundness, economic viability, and social justice. In practice, sustainable agriculture works toward maintaining or rebuilding soil, water, biodiversity, and ecosystem services while supporting fair livelihoods for farmers and food workers.

Key practices include: crop rotation (alternating legumes that fix nitrogen with grains that draw it down); cover cropping (planting between cash crops to prevent erosion and add organic matter); integrated pest management (IPM) (biological controls, monitoring, targeted spraying rather than calendar-based blanket applications); conservation tillage / no-till (leaving soil undisturbed to preserve structure and carbon); agroforestry (integrating trees into farmland); silvopasture (cattle, trees, and forage together); polyculture (multiple species on one field, like the Indigenous Three Sisters); precision agriculture (GPS, soil sensors, variable-rate technology to apply inputs exactly where needed). The SAFA framework (FAO Sustainability Assessment of Food and Agriculture systems) and the True Cost Accounting movement aim to make hidden costs (pollution, biodiversity loss, public health) visible in food pricing.

Food waste is staggering. The FAO and UNEP Food Waste Index 2024 estimate that 19% of food available at retail and consumer levels is wasted globally — about 1.05 billion tonnes per year. In Canada, the 2019 National Zero Waste Council and Second Harvest reports estimate that 58% of food produced is lost or wasted across the supply chain (about 35.5 million tonnes), worth $49 billion annually. Households account for about 28% of avoidable waste. Globally, if food waste were a country, it would be the third-largest greenhouse-gas emitter after China and the United States. Reducing waste yields ecological, financial, and food-security benefits simultaneously. Strategies: buying realistically (planning meals, lists, smaller portions), using everything (broths from scraps, root-to-leaf eating), storing well (cold for most, dark/dry for grains, refresh container etiquette), and composting unavoidable scraps to keep methane out of landfill.

  • Crop rotation — sequenced planting of different crops on the same field to manage pests, soil fertility, and disease.
  • Integrated pest management (IPM) — pest-control strategy combining biological, cultural, mechanical, and (when needed) chemical methods.
  • Cover crop — non-harvested crop planted to protect soil between cash-crop seasons.
  • Conservation tillage — practices that reduce soil disturbance to preserve structure and carbon.
  • Food loss vs food waste — losses occur upstream (production, post-harvest, processing); waste occurs downstream (retail, consumer).
  • Composting — controlled aerobic decomposition of organic matter into a soil amendment.

Worked example — Cutting household food waste

A household of four throws away on average 4 kg of food per week. Estimate the annual cost (using average grocery price ~$6/kg) and propose three changes likely to cut the waste in half.

Current annual waste: \(4 \times 52 = 208\) kg/year × $6/kg = $1,248/year — substantial. Three changes likely to halve the waste: (1) Plan meals weekly before shopping and write a list; this alone reduces "panic purchases" that go bad. (2) Use a "first in, first out" fridge system and check the back of the fridge weekly. (3) Cook with leftovers intentionally — a "use-it-up" night once a week; freeze portions of cooked meals for later. Bonus: compost unavoidable scraps to prevent methane release from landfill.

  • Believing best-before dates indicate safety — they indicate quality; many foods are safe past best-before (use sensory checks).
  • Composting only — composting is great but reducing at the source is higher in the waste hierarchy.
  • Treating sustainability as a single technology — it is a portfolio of practices fitted to context.
  • Forgetting workers — sustainability requires fair wages and safe conditions for farm and processing workers.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "evaluate the role of sustainable agriculture and food-waste reduction in supporting global food security."

🤝 Food Aid & the Sustainable Development Goals
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In 2015, all 193 UN member states adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development with 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) aims to "end hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture" by 2030. Progress has stalled or reversed since 2019 due to COVID-19, conflict, and climate shocks. As of 2024 the world is not on track to meet SDG 2.

Food aid takes several forms. Emergency food assistance responds to acute crises (refugee camps, famine, post-disaster), delivered by the UN World Food Programme (WFP) and partners. Programme food aid ties food deliveries to developmental goals (school meals, mother-and-child nutrition). Cash and voucher transfers increasingly replace in-kind food because they support local markets, are more dignifying, and are typically more cost-effective. Canada's Global Affairs Canada is among the largest donors to WFP. Concerns about food aid include: dependency creation; undermining of local farmers when free food competes with markets; politicisation; corruption; and the need for medical-grade ready-to-use therapeutic foods (RUTFs like Plumpy'Nut) for severe acute malnutrition.

Long-term solutions focus on the root causes: peace and good governance; women's empowerment and education; secure land tenure; access to credit, seeds, irrigation, and markets for smallholder farmers; social safety nets; climate adaptation; addressing global trade rules that disadvantage low-income countries. Programs like the African Green Revolution (AGRA), 1000 Days partnership (focused on nutrition during pregnancy and the first two years), and the UN Decade of Action on Nutrition emphasise systemic change. Other relevant SDGs intersect with food: SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 3 (Good Health), SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 6 (Clean Water), SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), SDG 13 (Climate Action), SDG 14 (Life Below Water), SDG 15 (Life on Land).

  • SDG 2 — Zero Hunger — UN target to end hunger and ensure food security by 2030.
  • World Food Programme (WFP) — UN agency providing food assistance; 2020 Nobel Peace Prize laureate.
  • Cash transfer — providing money rather than food; often more efficient and dignified.
  • Ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) — energy-dense, micronutrient-fortified paste (e.g., Plumpy'Nut) used to treat severe acute malnutrition.
  • 1000 Days — the period from conception to a child's second birthday; the critical window for nutritional interventions.
  • Smallholder farmer — small-scale farmer (typically <2 ha); produces about a third of the world's food.

Worked example — Cash versus food assistance

A donor must decide between sending grain shipments (in-kind food) and cash transfers to a region with functioning local markets. List two advantages of each and recommend a default choice.

In-kind food: (1) reliable when local markets have collapsed (war, blockade); (2) visible donor branding can be politically valuable. Cash transfers: (1) preserve local food markets and farmer livelihoods; (2) allow recipients to choose foods that match dietary preferences and cultural norms. Default recommendation: cash, when markets function and access is safe. WFP's recent guidance and IFRC (Red Cross) increasingly default to cash for this reason. In-kind food remains essential during acute supply disruption.

  • Treating food aid as a solution rather than a stop-gap — long-term food security depends on systemic change.
  • Assuming donor charity is always positive — poorly designed aid can undermine local markets and farmer livelihoods.
  • Ignoring trade rules — agricultural subsidies in high-income countries depress prices for low-income farmers.
  • Forgetting gender — women are the primary food producers in many regions; gender-responsive policy multiplies returns.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.3 — "evaluate UN Sustainable Development Goal 2 (Zero Hunger) and analyse the effectiveness of food-aid and development programs."

⚖️ Ethical Food Choices
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

What we eat is never just a personal decision — it touches farmworker livelihoods, animal welfare, biodiversity, climate, and global hunger. Studying food ethics means weighing competing values when choices conflict.

Animal welfare is central to many food debates. Concerns include intensive confinement (battery cages for laying hens — banned in the EU since 2012 and being phased out in Canada by 2036; gestation crates for sows; veal crates), painful procedures without anaesthesia (debeaking, dehorning, tail-docking), high-density broiler-chicken operations, fish farming practices, and slaughter standards. Certifications include Certified Humane, SPCA Certified (BC), and tiered standards from Global Animal Partnership. The plant-based movement responds in part to welfare concerns and in part to climate considerations. Fair Trade certification addresses farmworker welfare in coffee, cocoa, bananas, tea, and sugar — guaranteeing minimum prices, premiums for community investment, and prohibition of child and forced labour. The Rainforest Alliance and UTZ provide alternative certifications.

The plant-based diet spectrum includes flexitarian (occasional meat reduction), pescatarian (no land animals), vegetarian (no meat or fish), vegan (no animal products at all), and within those: lacto-vegetarian, ovo-vegetarian. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (2016 position paper) and Dietitians of Canada concur that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are appropriate at all life stages including pregnancy, lactation, infancy, childhood, and athletic performance — provided attention to specific nutrients of concern: protein (achievable with legumes, tofu, tempeh, nuts, seeds), vitamin B12 (supplement required for vegans), iron (more bioavailable from animal sources but achievable with pairing iron-rich plants with vitamin C), zinc, calcium, vitamin D, omega-3 (DHA/EPA from algae supplements), iodine. Religious and cultural food practices matter: halal (Islamic), kosher (Jewish), Hindu and Buddhist vegetarian traditions, Sikh langar, Indigenous ceremonial foods. Respectful nutrition counselling works with these practices rather than around them.

An ethical food framework for a Grade 12 student might ask of each food: Who grew/raised it, and were they fairly compensated? What were the conditions for the workers and animals involved? What's the environmental cost? What's the nutritional value? Does it support or undermine community food sovereignty? Few foods score perfectly on every axis, but consistently asking the questions builds a habit of conscientious eating. As Michael Pollan summarised, "Eat food. Not too much. Mostly plants." The Dietitians of Canada add: "in a way that fits your culture, your health, and your community."

  • Animal welfare — concern for the well-being of animals raised for food, including living conditions and humane slaughter.
  • Fair Trade — certification ensuring minimum prices and labour standards for farmers in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Vegan — excludes all animal-derived foods (meat, fish, dairy, eggs, honey).
  • Vegetarian — excludes meat and fish; subcategories may include or exclude dairy/eggs.
  • Flexitarian — primarily plant-based with occasional meat.
  • Locavorism — emphasis on foods produced within a defined local radius, often invoked for community and emissions reasons.

Worked example — Building an ethical-food rubric

You are buying coffee for the family. Choose between (A) generic supermarket coffee $9/kg; (B) Fair Trade Certified Organic coffee $18/kg; (C) Single-origin direct-trade coffee from a coop, $26/kg. Apply an ethical framework and recommend.

Recommendation depends on weighting. (A) cheapest but no certification of worker or environmental conditions; commodity coffee historically pays farmers below cost of production. (B) Fair Trade Organic certifies floor price + premium + organic farming standards — strongest combined social and environmental case at moderate cost. (C) Direct trade can pay farmers even more, but requires trust in the importer (no third-party certification). For most households, (B) Fair Trade Organic is the best balance of accountability, environmental practice, and price. If budget is limited, look for store-brand Fair Trade or co-op blends.

  • Believing "natural" certifications mean ethical — most "natural" labels have no rigorous social standards.
  • Assuming a vegan diet is automatically more ethical — vegan foods can be ultra-processed, climate-impactful (almond water demand), or socially problematic (forced-labour palm oil).
  • Dismissing cost as the only factor — true-cost accounting shows hidden environmental and social costs.
  • Generalising about "what's ethical" — ethics depends on values, context, and constraints; individual choices are part of a larger system that requires policy reform too.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.3 — "analyse the ethical dimensions of food choices, including fair trade, animal welfare, and plant-based diets."
