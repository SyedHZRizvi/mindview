CPW4U — Canadian and World Politics, Grade 12 University Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Canadian and World Studies (2018, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CPW4U  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: Any university or university/college preparation course in Canadian and World Studies, English, or Social Sciences and Humanities

1. Course Description

This course explores various perspectives on issues in Canadian and world politics. Students will explore political decision making and ways in which individuals, stakeholder groups, and various levels of government respond to political issues at the domestic and international level. They will also explore political ideologies and types of governance; the protection of human rights and the achievement of human welfare; and the causes of conflict and war and the conditions that promote cooperation. Students will apply the concepts of political thinking and the political inquiry process to investigate issues, develop and communicate informed opinions about them, and assess ways in which citizens can have influence in a political system, including in a democracy.

3. The Concepts of Political Thinking

Political thinking in CPW4U is organised around four interrelated concepts. Every unit, assessment, and inquiry in this course returns to one or more of them.

ConceptDefinition (CPW4U Application)
Political SignificanceWhat makes an issue, action, decision, or development politically significant? How does its significance vary across time, place, and stakeholder? (e.g., the 1982 patriation, the 2015 TRC Calls to Action, the 2022 invocation of the Emergencies Act)
Objectives and ResultsWhat are the stated and unstated goals of a political actor or institution, and how do actual outcomes compare to those goals? (e.g., the 1990s structural-adjustment programmes; Canada's R2P advocacy and post-Libya outcomes; the Paris Agreement's NDC mechanism)
Stability and ChangeWhat conditions support stability in political systems, and what causes change? How are continuity and transformation balanced? (e.g., the durability of Westminster norms; the rise and decline of multilateral order from 1945)
Political PerspectiveReconstructing how different actors (liberal, conservative, socialist, Indigenous, populist, feminist, realist, idealist) interpret the same political issue. Avoiding both anachronism and false neutrality. (e.g., comparing realist and liberal-institutionalist readings of NATO enlargement)

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Political Inquiry & Skill Development (embedded): Disciplined political analysis requires deliberate inquiry, evidence triangulation, and the explicit use of the four concepts of political thinking.
  • Strand B — Power, Governance, and Citizenship: Political systems distribute power among institutions, levels of government, and citizens; the design choices made (Westminster vs. presidential, unitary vs. federal, written vs. unwritten constitution) have enduring effects on who governs, how, and with what limits.
  • Strand C — Human Rights and Human Welfare: Modern political legitimacy increasingly rests on the protection of human rights and the provision of basic welfare; the gap between proclaimed rights (UDHR 1948, ICCPR, ICESCR, the Charter) and lived experience is a central political problem.
  • Strand D — Power and Conflict: War, civil conflict, and political violence arise from structural conditions (security dilemma, resource competition, ethnic nationalism), human choices, and the limits of international law; states and citizens face moral as well as strategic questions.
  • Strand E — Cooperation, Conflict Resolution, and Global Issues: International institutions, treaties, and civil-society movements provide imperfect but real mechanisms for cooperation on issues (climate, health, trade, migration) that no single state can solve alone; the durability of multilateralism is itself a political question.

5. Strand A — Political Inquiry & Skill Development (embedded)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Political Inquiry: use the political inquiry process and the concepts of political thinking when investigating issues, events, and developments of national and international political importance;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills developed through political investigation, and identify careers in which a background in politics might be an asset.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues of national and international political importance;
  • A1.2 select and organise relevant evidence and information from primary and secondary sources, including statistical data, news media, and government documents;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources, recognising bias, framing, propaganda, and disinformation;
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence using appropriate tools (data visualisation, comparative tables, primary-source analysis, content analysis of party platforms);
  • A1.5 use the concepts of political thinking (significance; objectives and results; stability and change; political perspective) when analysing political issues;
  • A1.7 communicate ideas, arguments, and conclusions using various formats (policy memo, op-ed, briefing note, position paper, parliamentary speech, oral debate);
  • A2.1 describe several ways in which political investigation develops skills, including those listed in the Ontario Skills Passport (e.g., critical thinking, information management, advocacy, public speaking).
Cross-curricular focus: CPW4U is a senior university-preparation course; the inquiry skills explicitly target the analytical writing demanded by first-year university programs in political science, law, international relations, public policy, sociology, and history.

6. Strand B — Power, Governance, and Citizenship (Unit 1 + 2)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Political Systems: explain the political characteristics of selected systems of government and analyse the relationships between people, the state, and various centres of power;
  • B2. Political Ideologies and Decision Making: analyse various political ideologies and how they influence political decision making, both in Canada and internationally;
  • B3. Citizenship and Participation: analyse various ways in which citizens, including youth, can participate effectively in the political process, both in Canada and internationally.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 analyse the political characteristics of democratic, authoritarian, and totalitarian systems, using contemporary case studies (Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, China, Russia, North Korea);
  • B1.2 explain the structure and functioning of the Canadian political system, including federalism, the Westminster parliamentary model, the division of powers (sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867), and the principle of responsible government;
  • B1.3 analyse the role of constitutional documents and the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982), including the role of judicial review and the section 33 "notwithstanding" clause;
  • B2.1 describe the key principles of major political ideologies (liberalism, conservatism, socialism, fascism, populism, anarchism, Indigenous political thought);
  • B2.3 analyse the relationship between political ideologies and political parties in Canada and internationally;
  • B3.1 analyse the rights and responsibilities of citizenship in Canada;
  • B3.2 describe several ways in which citizens can participate in political life beyond voting (campaigning, civil society, social movements, public consultation, direct action).
Anchor topics: sovereignty; the Charter; judicial review (R. v. Oakes, R. v. Morgentaler, Vriend v. Alberta); the section 33 override; populism; the 2025 Canadian political landscape.

7. Strand C — Human Rights and Human Welfare (Unit 3)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Human Rights: analyse efforts to advance and protect human rights at the national and international level, and assess the effectiveness of these efforts;
  • C2. Human Welfare: analyse efforts to improve human welfare, including efforts to alleviate poverty and provide social-safety nets;
  • C3. Rights and Welfare in Canada: analyse the protection of human rights and the provision of human welfare in Canada, with attention to First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 analyse the development of international human-rights instruments, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), the ICCPR, the ICESCR, and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007);
  • C1.2 analyse the international refugee-protection regime (1951 Convention, 1967 Protocol, UNHCR);
  • C2.1 analyse the development of welfare-state institutions in liberal democracies (universal healthcare, public education, unemployment insurance, retirement income);
  • C3.1 analyse the protection of human rights in Canada through the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) and provincial human-rights codes;
  • C3.2 analyse the legacy of residential schools, the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (1996), the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–15), and the National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (2016–19).
STSE focus: data-driven welfare measurement (HDI, Gini coefficient, the Multidimensional Poverty Index); the right-to-housing debate; digital privacy and surveillance.

8. Strand D — Power and Conflict (Unit 4)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Causes of Conflict: analyse the causes of conflict and war, with reference to both historical and contemporary cases;
  • D2. Conflict and the International System: analyse the legal and institutional frameworks that govern armed conflict, including international humanitarian law and the responsibility to protect;
  • D3. Canada's Role: analyse Canada's role in armed conflict, peacekeeping, peacemaking, and related international cooperation.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 analyse the structural causes of war (security dilemma, resource competition, ethnic nationalism, ideological hostility, state failure);
  • D1.2 examine the principles of just war theory (jus ad bellum, jus in bello, jus post bellum) and the limits of those principles in contemporary conflicts;
  • D2.1 analyse international humanitarian law (the Geneva Conventions and Additional Protocols), the International Criminal Court (Rome Statute, 1998), and the doctrine of the responsibility to protect (R2P, 2005);
  • D2.2 examine selected armed conflicts (Rwanda 1994, Bosnia 1992–95, Syria 2011–, Russia–Ukraine 2014–);
  • D3.1 analyse Canada's role in NATO (1949–), NORAD (1958–), UN peacekeeping (1956–), and post-2001 Afghanistan operations;
  • D3.2 examine Canada's contemporary defence and foreign-policy posture (Strong, Secure, Engaged 2017; the 2024 Defence Policy Update; Arctic security).
Anchor topics: the Rwandan genocide; Bosnia and Srebrenica; the failure to act in Syria; Canada's role in NATO's Operation Reassurance; the 2024 Indo-Pacific Strategy.

9. Strand E — Cooperation, Conflict Resolution, and Global Issues (Unit 5)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. International Cooperation: analyse various forms of international cooperation, including treaties, institutions, and civil-society initiatives;
  • E2. Conflict Resolution: analyse approaches to the resolution of conflict, including diplomacy, sanctions, and post-conflict reconciliation;
  • E3. Global Issues: analyse selected global issues (climate change, global health, migration, trade, digital governance) and the international responses to them.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 analyse the structure and functioning of the United Nations system (General Assembly, Security Council, ICJ, ECOSOC, specialised agencies including WHO and UNHCR);
  • E1.2 analyse the structure and functioning of regional and economic institutions (G7, G20, WTO, IMF, World Bank, NATO, OECD);
  • E2.1 analyse Canada's diplomatic tools (multilateralism, "middle-power" doctrine, sanctions regimes, development assistance);
  • E3.1 analyse global climate governance, including the UNFCCC, the Kyoto Protocol (1997), and the Paris Agreement (2015) with its NDC mechanism;
  • E3.2 analyse global health governance, including the WHO, the IHR (2005), and the international response to COVID-19 (2020–23);
  • E3.3 analyse global migration patterns and Canadian immigration and refugee policy (Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, 2002; private-sponsorship model; 2015–16 Syrian resettlement).
STSE focus: climate diplomacy; pandemic preparedness; AI governance and digital sovereignty; the geopolitics of critical minerals.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, source analyses, position papers, simulated parliamentary debates, model-UN sessions, briefing notes, and a culminating policy proposal. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a culminating political-inquiry project (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, ideology-self-placement instruments, current-events trackers, policy-memo drafts).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., pre-unit diagnostic, mid-inquiry conferencing, draft thesis feedback).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, formal essays, simulations, oral presentations, final exam).

14. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

Multiple means of representation (primary documents in transcript and audio; captioned parliamentary footage; data visualisations; political cartoons); multiple means of engagement (choice of inquiry topic, choice of country comparison, choice of advocacy issue); multiple means of expression (policy memo, podcast, debate speech, infographic, video op-ed, model-UN position paper). The course centres First Nations, Métis, and Inuit political thought throughout, not as an addendum, in alignment with the TRC's Calls to Action 62 and 63 and UNDRIP Article 14.

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organisation, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CPW4U for sustaining multi-week inquiry projects, engaging respectfully across the political spectrum in classroom debate, and developing the disciplined civic posture expected of senior university-bound students.
