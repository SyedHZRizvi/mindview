CPC3O — Politics in Action: Making Change, Grade 11 Open

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 to 12: Canadian and World Studies (2018, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CPC3O  |  Credit Value: 0.5  |  Hours: 55  |  Type: Open  |  Prerequisite: CHV2O Civics and Citizenship, Grade 10 Open

1. Course Description

This course enables students to develop their understanding of political processes and active citizenship and explore the dimensions of social and political change. Students will examine various contemporary political and social issues, including issues that are affecting youth and their communities. They will analyse the influence of political organizations, ideologies, citizens, and stakeholders, and they will design and implement a plan to address an issue that they personally care about. Students will apply the concepts of political thinking and the political inquiry process to investigate, design, evaluate, and act upon issues of significance — moving beyond awareness (the Grade-10 Civics focus) into purposeful, planned action.

2. Overall Goals of the Course

By the end of the course, students will:

  • investigate political and social issues using the political inquiry process and the concepts of political thinking;
  • analyse the influence of power, ideology, and democratic principles on contemporary issues;
  • compare different methods of civic engagement and their effectiveness for different types of issues;
  • design, implement, and evaluate a civic-action initiative that addresses an issue of personal and community significance;
  • reflect on their growth as politically engaged citizens and on the contributions of others.

3. Concepts of Disciplinary Thinking (Politics)

ConceptQuestion it asks
Political SignificanceWhy does this issue, event, or action matter politically? Significant for whom, and at what scale?
Objectives & ResultsWhat did participants set out to achieve? What were the intended and unintended consequences — short, medium, and long term?
Stability & ChangeWhat did the action seek to change? What conditions made change possible (or prevented it)?
Political PerspectiveWhose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue and the proposed action? Whose are absent?

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Civic Awareness (B): Healthy democracies depend on citizens who understand the political dimensions of the issues facing their communities and the rights frameworks that protect them.
  • Civic Engagement & Action (C): Civic change is rarely accidental; it is the result of deliberate, sustained, ethically-grounded engagement strategies designed by people who care.
  • Political Inquiry & Skill Development (A): A disciplined inquiry process — questions, evidence, reasoning, communication, and reflection — is the engine of every successful civic action.

5. Strand A — Political Inquiry and Skill Development (embedded across all units)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Political Inquiry: use the political inquiry process and the concepts of political thinking when investigating political and social issues, events, and developments;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills, including spatial-technology and media-literacy skills, developed through investigations related to politics and civic action, and identify some careers and post-secondary pathways in which these skills are an asset.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into political and social issues, events, and/or developments;
  • A1.2 select and organize relevant evidence, data, and information from a variety of primary and secondary sources;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information, including online and social-media sources, used in their investigations;
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence using a range of strategies appropriate for political inquiry (e.g., case study, stakeholder mapping, public-opinion analysis);
  • A1.5 use the concepts of political thinking (political significance, objectives and results, stability and change, political perspective) when analysing and evaluating evidence and formulating conclusions;
  • A1.6 evaluate evidence and draw conclusions about political and social issues, events, and/or developments;
  • A1.7 communicate conclusions, judgements, and plans of action clearly and logically using appropriate forms (e.g., infographic, op-ed, formal speech, deputation, social-media campaign, podcast, digital story);
  • A1.8 use accepted forms of documentation to reference sources;
  • A1.9 use appropriate terminology when communicating the results of their investigations (e.g., advocacy, civil disobedience, coalition, lobby, policy, polarization, stakeholder).
Cross-curricular focus: Inquiry products in CPC3O may take the form of a deputation to school board or municipal council, a media campaign, a policy brief, a community organizing plan, or a multimedia digital story. Source-checking habits transfer to History, English, Geography, and senior Politics (CPW4U).

6. Strand B — Civic Awareness

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Political and Social Issues: identify issues of significance in Canada and globally and analyse their political dimensions;
  • B2. Power, Influence, and Democracy: assess how political and economic power is exercised in Canada and globally, and the effect of various forms of influence on democratic life;
  • B3. Civic Rights and Responsibilities: analyse rights and responsibilities of citizens at local, national, and global scales, including the rights frameworks that protect them.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 identify political and social issues of significance in Canada and globally (e.g., housing affordability, climate, Indigenous rights, healthcare access, AI governance, online disinformation);
  • B1.2 analyse the political dimensions of an issue, including the actors involved and the policy levers available to address it;
  • B1.3 explain how an issue is constructed differently by different stakeholders and how those constructions shape what counts as an "acceptable" response;
  • B2.1 describe various forms of political and economic power and their sources (e.g., coercive, persuasive, structural, soft, network);
  • B2.2 explain how decisions are made at different levels of government in Canada and assess where influence is concentrated;
  • B2.3 analyse the role of political parties, interest groups, media, social movements, and lobbyists in Canadian democracy;
  • B2.4 evaluate the effects of digital media and social platforms on political participation and on democratic norms;
  • B3.1 describe key rights protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and by international instruments (e.g., UDHR, UNDRIP);
  • B3.2 analyse responsibilities associated with citizenship rights at local, national, and global scales;
  • B3.3 describe how rights are protected and what citizens can do when rights are infringed.
Anchor texts: Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982); Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948); UNDRIP (2007); Truth and Reconciliation Commission Calls to Action (2015); current public-policy documents and Hansard / municipal-council transcripts.

7. Strand C — Civic Engagement and Action

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Engagement Strategies: examine ways in which individuals and groups can effect political and social change;
  • C2. Civic Action: design, implement, and evaluate a plan of action to address an issue of civic importance;
  • C3. Civic Contributions: assess the contributions of citizens to political and social change at the local, national, and global level.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 analyse a variety of engagement strategies (e.g., petitioning, lobbying, peaceful protest, civil disobedience, voting, voluntary work, social-media campaigning, deputations, running for office) and assess their relative effectiveness for different types of issues;
  • C1.2 analyse the role of ethical reasoning in choosing engagement strategies (e.g., honesty, non-violence, inclusion, accuracy);
  • C1.3 describe how engagement strategies have changed over time and across cultures;
  • C2.1 select a civic issue of personal interest and formulate a research question;
  • C2.2 gather, interpret, and analyse information about the issue using the concepts of political thinking;
  • C2.3 develop a plan of action (SMART goals, target audience, message, channels, timeline, resources, success measures);
  • C2.4 implement (or simulate, where implementation is not possible) the plan, documenting actions taken;
  • C2.5 evaluate the implementation against intended outcomes and reflect on lessons learned;
  • C3.1 assess contributions of Canadian and global citizens to political and social change (e.g., Mary Simon, Cindy Blackstock, Malala Yousafzai, Greta Thunberg, Jagmeet Singh-Olympian Hayley Wickenheiser-style local examples);
  • C3.2 explain why citizen action is essential for sustaining democracy.
Civic Action Project (signature task): Each student designs and implements (or simulates) a civic-action initiative tied to a real local, national, or global issue. Examples: a school-board deputation on climate policy, a voter-registration drive for new 18-year-olds, an op-ed in the local paper, a community fundraiser, a youth-policy submission to municipal council, a multimedia campaign on online safety. The Project synthesises Strand A inquiry, Strand B awareness, and Strand C engagement.

10. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprising unit tests, source-evaluation tasks, position papers, deputations, ongoing Civic Action Project artefacts. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprising the Civic Action Project final submission and presentation (15%) and a Final Examination (15%).

11. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes on rights / engagement strategies, self-rubrics on action-plan drafts, reflective journaling).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — descriptive teacher feedback that informs next steps (e.g., diagnostic on prior CHV2O knowledge, feedback on inquiry-question draft, mid-project plan review).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, civic-action plan, final exam, final action-project presentation).

12. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (Hansard, CBC podcasts, infographics, captioned video, mapping tools), multiple means of engagement (student-selected civic issue), and multiple means of expression (essay, podcast, deputation, op-ed, video, social-media campaign). Where appropriate, the course centres Indigenous political thought (treaty federalism, consensus governance, UNDRIP) and the perspectives of equity-deserving groups in Canada.

13. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CPC3O for sustaining the multi-week Civic Action Project and for collaborative work in simulations and seminars.
