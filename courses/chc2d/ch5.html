🧭 5.1 The Historical Inquiry Process
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

History is not a recitation of dates. It is a discipline of asking, investigating, and arguing. The Ontario curriculum frames the historian's craft as a five-step process that turns curiosity into evidence-based conclusion: formulate questions, gather and organize, interpret and analyse, evaluate and draw conclusions, communicate. The process is recursive — historians return to earlier steps as new evidence reshapes their question.

Step 1 — Formulate questions. The historical question is not "what happened?" alone; it is "what does it mean that this happened, and why does it matter?" Question quality matters. "Was Vimy Ridge important?" is a closed yes/no. "How did the postwar commemoration of Vimy Ridge construct a particular Canadian national identity, and at whose expense?" is open, defensible, and complex. The shift to historical-thinking questions trains students to investigate rather than recite.

Step 2 — Gather and organize. Locate primary sources (documents from the period: letters, photographs, government records, newspaper accounts, oral testimony) and secondary sources (later analyses by historians, journalists, documentary filmmakers). Library and Archives Canada, the Canadian War Museum, provincial archives, and Library of Parliament hold most of what students will need; CBC Archives and University of Toronto's Toronto Public Library Digital Archive are open-web equivalents. Organise sources by topic, perspective, and date — not just by chronology.

Step 3 — Interpret and analyse. Read each source for content (what does it say?), context (who produced it, when, for whom?), perspective (whose viewpoint is privileged or absent?), and reliability (what can we test against other sources?). The 5W+H questions (who, what, where, when, why, how) are scaffolding, not endpoints. Compare sources for agreement and disagreement; a single source corroborated by another is stronger than the most vivid solo testimony.

Step 4 — Evaluate and draw conclusions. Apply the six historical thinking concepts as analytical lenses (covered in detail in topic 5.2). Construct an argument that names a defensible interpretation, acknowledges counter-interpretations, and shows the weight of evidence. The argument should be specific and falsifiable — "Vimy mattered because it raised Canadian diplomatic standing" is a claim others can contest with evidence; "Vimy was great" is not.

Step 5 — Communicate. Choose a format suited to audience and purpose — essay, podcast, documentary, infographic, oral presentation. Cite sources accurately (Chicago, MLA, or APA — your teacher will specify). Distinguish your interpretation from quoted material; embed direct quotations sparingly and integrate them grammatically; provide page or date or archival-reference numbers so a reader could verify your evidence. History without verifiable citation is not history; it is opinion with footnotes-shaped suggestion of authority.

  • Historical inquiry process — five-step procedure: question, gather, interpret, evaluate, communicate.
  • Primary source — a document or artefact produced during the period studied.
  • Secondary source — a later analysis or synthesis based on primary sources.
  • Tertiary source — reference works (encyclopedias, textbooks, Wikipedia) — useful for orientation, not as primary evidence.
  • Library and Archives Canada — federal repository at Ottawa; principal source for federal documents.

Worked Example — From Topic to Question

Topic: "The 1970 October Crisis." Bad question: "Was Trudeau right to invoke the War Measures Act?" (binary, dependent on the asker's politics). Better question: "How did public opinion on the WMA invocation shift between October 1970 and October 1980, and what does that shift reveal about Canadian civil-liberties consciousness?" — this is bounded (Oct 1970 to Oct 1980), specific (public opinion, civil-liberties consciousness), and defensible by evidence (polling data, parliamentary debates, journalism, court rulings). It also explicitly invites a historical-thinking analysis (continuity-and-change in civil-liberties consciousness across a decade). A Level-4 inquiry would identify three to five primary sources (Gallup polls, the Mackasey speech, the McDonald Commission record, the Pépin-Robarts report, Trudeau's own retrospective), three secondary sources (Reg Whitaker, Dominique Clément, Marc Lalonde retrospectively), and one Quebec-perspective source (Pierre Vallières, Jean-François Cardin) — and would weigh these together.

  • Confusing primary with old — a 2024 historian's analysis is secondary; a 1970 newspaper article is primary.
  • Treating Wikipedia as a primary source — it is tertiary; use it for orientation, not citation.
  • Asking yes/no questions that close inquiry before it begins.
  • Quoting evidence without context — a quotation without a date or author cannot be tested.
  • Skipping evaluation and going straight to conclusion — the entire discipline lives in the evaluation step.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in Canadian history since 1914."

🔍 5.2 The Six Historical Thinking Concepts (Seixas)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The "Big Six" historical thinking concepts, articulated by Peter Seixas (Director, Historical Thinking Project, University of British Columbia) and Tom Morton in their 2013 textbook, are the disciplinary lenses through which Ontario expects students to think historically. The concepts are not "topics" but operations — moves a historian makes on evidence. The Ontario CHC2D curriculum (2018) lists them as: historical significance; cause and consequence; continuity and change; historical perspectives; evidence; ethical dimensions.

1. Historical significance. What makes something "historically significant"? Not popularity at the time, not casualty count, not headline space. Seixas suggests three working criteria: (a) resulting in change (the event caused something visible); (b) revealing (the event illuminates broader patterns); (c) resonant (the event continues to matter in present-day understanding). Significance shifts with time and perspective — what was central to one generation may be marginal to the next. Vimy's significance, for instance, grew between 1917 and 1936 and again between 1986 and 2017.

2. Cause and consequence. Events have multiple, hierarchical causes — long-term, short-term, ideological, structural, contingent. They produce intended and unintended consequences. Strong cause-and-consequence analysis: (a) names multiple causes and ranks them; (b) distinguishes necessary from sufficient conditions; (c) distinguishes foreseen from unforeseen consequences; (d) avoids monocausal explanation ("conscription caused Quebec alienation" omits the imperial-French question, Regulation 17, and the Catholic-Protestant divide).

3. Continuity and change. Not everything changes at the same pace; some things never change at all. Identify what persists across decades and what shifts. Compare specific moments rather than vague "before and after." For instance, the legal framework of the Indian Act shows striking continuity from 1876 to today, even as policy practice changed — that gap (continuity in law, change in practice) is itself the historical finding.

4. Historical perspective. Reconstruct how people in the past understood their world without imposing present values. Why did thousands of Canadians enlist enthusiastically in 1914? Not because they were stupid or warmongering — because they understood themselves as part of an imperial moral community defending civilisation. Historical perspective requires imagining within constraints: what did 1914-Canadians know? What did they believe? What were the moral and intellectual resources available to them? Anachronism (judging the past by present standards) and presentism (assuming people then thought like us) are both failures of historical perspective.

5. Evidence. All historical claims rest on sources. Strong evidential reasoning: (a) identifies the source's origin, context, and audience; (b) corroborates across multiple sources; (c) acknowledges what a source cannot tell us; (d) treats absences and silences as themselves evidence. A 1942 RCMP report on Japanese-Canadian "threat" assessment that concluded no threat existed — and was then ignored — is doubly evidential: evidence about the assessment, and evidence about the politics of overriding it.

6. Ethical dimensions. History involves judgment. Past actors made choices that produced help and harm. Fair historical judgment: (a) recognises that contemporaries had moral resources we may underestimate; (b) avoids both whitewashing and condemnation as default; (c) considers whether the harm was foreseeable; (d) considers what responsibilities present-day Canadians inherit from past conduct (Japanese-Canadian internment, residential schools, the MS St. Louis refusal). The 2018 federal apology for the St. Louis, the 1988 redress agreement, and the 2008 residential-schools apology are state acknowledgments of ethical responsibilities the past created.

  • Peter Seixas — UBC historian; founder of the Historical Thinking Project; co-author of The Big Six (2013).
  • Historical Thinking Project — Canadian-led effort to mainstream the six concepts in K–12 history.
  • Presentism — assumption that people in the past thought like us; a methodological error.
  • Anachronism — applying later concepts or judgments retroactively; a methodological error.
  • Periodisation — the act of breaking history into named eras; itself a historical-thinking choice with consequences.

Worked Example — Applying All Six to One Event (Vimy Ridge)

(1) Significance: Vimy is heavily commemorated but contested in actual strategic importance — the gap between the two is itself the finding. (2) Cause: short-term (Byng's plan, creeping barrage doctrine, Currie's training programme); long-term (industrial-scale warfare, imperial-colonial military structures, Canadian volunteer recruitment). (3) Continuity-and-change: continuity in Canadian Corps' coherence from Ypres 1915 to the Hundred Days 1918; change in Canadian command independence — Byng (British) commanded Vimy, Currie (Canadian) commanded later. (4) Perspective: 1917 Canadian view (national pride); 1917 British view (a tactical success in a larger failed offensive); 1917 French view (relief of pressure on French sectors). (5) Evidence: war diaries, Currie's after-action report, German accounts of the loss, the Imperial War Conference 1917 minutes showing how Canadian battlefield reputation translated into diplomatic leverage. (6) Ethics: was the cost (3,598 dead) justified by the strategic gain? An honest analysis weights the question rather than asserting an answer. A Level-4 response applies all six concepts to the same event and shows that the concepts produce different kinds of insight — not duplicate findings.

  • Treating the six concepts as topics to write about rather than operations to perform on evidence.
  • Using "historical perspective" to justify past conduct rather than to understand it.
  • Confusing "ethical judgment" with personal moral preference — it is structured analysis, not opinion.
  • Mistaking "continuity-and-change" for chronology — it is comparative analysis across time.
  • Treating "evidence" as just "quotes" — evidence is sources interpreted within context.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.5 — "use the concepts of historical thinking when analysing, evaluating evidence about, and formulating conclusions and/or judgements regarding historical issues, events, and/or developments in Canada since 1914."

📜 5.3 Primary Sources — Reading Documents in Context
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A primary source is any source produced in the period being studied. The category is wider than students often expect: government documents, newspapers, photographs, films, advertising, military records, letters, diaries, oral testimony, music, posters, court transcripts, censuses, parish registers, treaty texts, business records, school records, hospital records, audio recordings — all primary. The category is wider still when we recognise that artefacts — a uniform, a recruitment poster, a residential-school dormitory bed frame — are also primary evidence.

Reading a primary source well requires distinguishing what the source says from what the source is. A 1916 recruitment poster says "Your King and Country Need You!" — but it is a piece of state-aligned (or paramilitary, like the Canadian Patriotic Fund) propaganda commissioned to address falling enlistment. The text is the surface; the production context, intended audience, and circulation are also evidence — sometimes more important than the text. The OPVL framework (Origin, Purpose, Value, Limitations), or the more elaborate APPARTS / SOAPSTone, structures this reading.

Multiple sources work better than one. A photograph of Mohawk Warrior Lasagna and Cdn soldier Cloutier at Oka 1990 (Komulainen) tells one piece of the story; the same standoff is also documented by SQ radio logs, Warrior Society interviews afterwards, the federal-Mohawk negotiation transcripts, Canadian military after-action reports, contemporary newspaper accounts (English-Canadian, French-Canadian, Mohawk-community publications), and the testimony at the Erasmus-Dussault commission (RCAP 1991–96). A complete picture requires triangulation — corroborating one source's claims against others. Where they agree, confidence rises; where they disagree, the disagreement itself becomes the question.

Source criticism is also internal: does the source contradict itself? Does the language carry assumptions worth examining? Does the source name what it omits? F. C. Blair's correspondence on Jewish immigration in 1939 is sparse on the moral logic of refusal — the omission of moral reasoning is itself a finding. Mackenzie King's 1937 diary on Hitler reveals a worldview that requires reconstruction. A Level-4 source-criticism notes both what the source says and what the source's silences reveal.

  • OPVL — Origin, Purpose, Value, Limitations; structure for source analysis.
  • APPARTS — Author, Place & time, Prior knowledge, Audience, Reason, The main idea, Significance.
  • SOAPSTone — Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone; literary-rhetorical analysis transferable to history.
  • Triangulation — corroborating claims across multiple independent sources.
  • Provenance — the documented chain of custody of a source from creation to present.
  • Bias — a source's slant; not by itself disqualifying — all sources have bias, but biased sources can still be informative if read carefully.

Worked Example — Reading Borden's 1917 Conscription Speech

Robert Borden, in the House of Commons on 18 May 1917, announced his conversion to compulsory service: "All citizens are liable to military service for the defence of their country, and I conceive that the battle for Canadian liberty and autonomy is being fought today on the plains of France and of Belgium." Apply OPVL. Origin: prime minister's parliamentary statement, on the official record, in May 1917, against the backdrop of the recent Vimy victory and rising enlistment shortfalls. Purpose: to persuade Parliament and English-Canadian opinion that compulsion was necessary and that Canada's autonomy required it. Value: gives the official rationale; reveals Borden's framing strategy (autonomy not empire); is contemporaneous and verbatim. Limitations: cannot tell us how the public received it; cannot tell us what Borden himself privately believed about French-Canadian opposition; omits the political-economic context of Imperial War Conference commitments. A complete reading would pair Borden's speech with Bourassa's response in Le Devoir, with the Quebec Easter riots in April 1918, and with the actual conscription enrolment data. A Level-4 source analysis notes both what the speech achieves rhetorically and what it strategically omits.

  • Treating any document as "neutral" — all documents are produced by people with positions.
  • Reading the text without the context — same text in 1916 means something different than in 1944.
  • Conflating "biased" with "useless" — biased sources are still evidence about the biased writer's perspective.
  • Ignoring photograph captions and metadata — original captions often shape interpretation.
  • Underrating oral testimony — it is primary evidence about memory, even when imperfect as evidence about events.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — "interpret and analyse evidence and information relevant to their investigations, using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for historical inquiry."

🗄️ 5.4 Secondary Sources & Historiography
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Secondary sources are later analyses based on primary evidence. They include historians' monographs, peer-reviewed journal articles, encyclopaedia entries, biographies, documentary films, and museum interpretations. They are essential: they synthesize, contextualise, and disagree with each other. A serious historical inquiry reads both primary and secondary sources, treating the secondary sources not as authorities-to-be-believed but as interlocutors-to-be-engaged.

Historiography — the history of history — is the study of how interpretations of past events have changed. The same event can be interpreted differently in 1925, 1965, and 2015, not because the event changed but because the historians' questions, evidence, and perspectives changed. The story of Canadian historiography illustrates: from 1900–1960, "great-man" political-economy history (Sir George Wrong, Donald Creighton, Arthur Lower) dominated; from 1960–1985, social and labour history (Greg Kealey, Linda Kealey, Bryan Palmer) added "history from below"; from 1985–2005, women's, ethnic, and Indigenous histories (Pamela Sugiman, Constance Backhouse, Olive Dickason) restructured the field; from 2005–present, transnational and environmental history (Tina Loo, Sean Kheraj, Susanne Klausen) further widened the frame. A Vimy book written in 1925, 1965, and 2017 differs not because Vimy changed but because the questions have.

For Grade 10 inquiry, two practical implications follow. First, choose secondary sources from across the historiographical spectrum — do not rely on a single textbook or a single school of interpretation. Pair Tim Cook's recent CWM-affiliated military history with Margaret MacMillan's transnational synthesis, with Pierre Berton's older popular narrative, and notice what each foregrounds and omits. Second, learn to identify the historian's perspective: their argument, their evidence, their unstated assumptions. Strong secondary sources state their argument early and provide footnoted evidence; weak ones blur interpretation with summary. The Canadian Historical Review, the Journal of Canadian Studies, and Canadian Historical Association publications are peer-reviewed; Wikipedia, YouTube history channels, and TV documentaries vary widely in quality.

"Public history" — the work of museums, monuments, commemorations, films, and curricula — is itself a category worth analysing. The Canadian War Museum (Ottawa), Pier 21 (Halifax), the Canadian Museum of History (Gatineau), the National Holocaust Monument (Ottawa), and the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation (Winnipeg) all interpret history for public audiences. Their choices about what to include and exclude, what to emphasise, what visitor experience to create, are historiographical choices. A site visit (real or virtual) followed by reading the institution's own scholarly publications is a productive way to see public history as a constructed interpretation, not neutral display.

  • Historiography — the history of historical interpretation.
  • Schools of history — interpretive traditions (military, social, women's, Indigenous, environmental, transnational).
  • Public history — history as encountered in museums, monuments, films, and curricula.
  • Peer review — academic quality-control process for scholarly publication.
  • Donald Creighton — Toronto historian of the Laurentian thesis; The Empire of the St. Lawrence (1956).
  • Tim Cook — Canadian War Museum historian; multiple works on Canadian WWI/WWII military history.
  • Olive Dickason — Métis historian; Canada's First Nations (1992) — foundational Indigenous-perspective synthesis.

Worked Example — Compare Two Vimy Histories

Compare Pierre Berton's Vimy (1986) with Tim Cook's Vimy: The Battle and the Legend (2017). Berton's popular history emphasises the "birth of a nation" reading, dwelling on the soldiers' experience as proto-Canadian transformation. Cook is at once more military-tactically precise (he engages the operational level in detail) and more historiographically self-conscious (he treats the "birth of a nation" reading as a postwar construction). The same event, the same primary documents (much of them), produce different syntheses because the questions differ. A Level-4 inquiry-comparison would: (1) identify each author's argument; (2) identify the historical-thinking concepts each emphasises (Berton: historical significance + perspective; Cook: significance + continuity-and-change in commemoration); (3) explain what changed between the two books and why; (4) take a position on which interpretation the student finds more persuasive and why. The exercise teaches that historiography is not optional decoration — it is part of doing history.

  • Treating secondary sources as authoritative final word — they are interpretations, contestable.
  • Ignoring publication date — a 1955 book on residential schools is dated by today's research.
  • Using documentary films as sole source — most are heavily curated; pair with peer-reviewed work.
  • Confusing the historian with the historical actor — Donald Creighton wrote about John A. Macdonald in the 1950s; both are subjects, but Creighton is the analyst.
  • Citing Wikipedia in an essay — use it for orientation, then trace its sources and cite those directly.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "select and organize relevant evidence and information from a variety of primary and secondary sources."

📰 5.5 Media Literacy & the Misinformation Era
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The skills of historical-source criticism transfer directly to evaluating contemporary media. Both ask: who produced this? When, for whom, why? What does it leave out? What does it want me to think or feel? Mike Caulfield's SIFT method — Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to original context — is the most-used schoolroom framework. It is essentially the historian's source-criticism applied to social-media velocity.

The contemporary information environment poses three problems historians' methods address. (1) Speed: a TikTok claim travels faster than verification; SIFT urges users to stop before sharing. (2) Decontextualisation: a quotation, image, or clip extracted from its original context can flip its meaning; the historian's habit of demanding "what was the original?" applies. (3) Anonymous or hidden sources: bots, content farms, AI-generated text all create plausibly authoritative material without verifiable origin; the historian's "provenance" question — who made this, when, where, why? — is the entry point for evaluation.

Reverse-image search, public-records lookup, and "lateral reading" (opening multiple tabs to check a claim against independent sources) are the practical operations. Lateral reading is the core skill: when an unfamiliar article makes a striking claim, do not stay on the article and judge its credibility from its presentation. Instead, search across the open web for what other sources say about the article's source and claim. This is exactly what historians do when they discover a new primary document — they do not believe it on its own face; they place it within other documents.

Two examples specific to Canadian history illustrate the stakes. (1) The 2021 Kamloops Indian Residential School announcement of 215 anomalies (later identified as likely unmarked graves) was rapidly distorted in some online accounts to "215 confirmed mass graves" or, in opposite-direction misinformation, to "no graves at all, this is a hoax." The careful reading was always: GPR detected anomalies consistent with unmarked burials; physical excavation was deliberately not pursued in keeping with community wishes; the precise count and the precise burial dates remained being investigated. Historical-thinking source criticism could parse the careful claim from both directions of distortion. (2) The 2022 Freedom Convoy generated a parallel mass of claims about its size, composition, foreign funding, and demands. Both English-language CBC and Radio-Canada reporting could be cross-checked against the parliamentary record, the Rouleau Commission transcripts, and the published affidavit evidence. The historian's habits of patience and triangulation produce better reading than the speed of social media rewards.

  • SIFT — Stop, Investigate, Find better coverage, Trace claims (Caulfield's method).
  • Lateral reading — checking claims against independent sources rather than staying within the original article.
  • Reverse image search — finding the original or other appearances of an image.
  • Provenance — documented origin and chain of custody.
  • Deep fake / generative AI — synthetic media indistinguishable from authentic media without source-trace.
  • Confirmation bias — tendency to favour information that supports prior beliefs.

Worked Example — SIFT a Social-Media Post

Suppose you see a tweet: "Canada's official population was secretly higher than reported in 2023 — Stats Can manipulated the figures by 12%." Apply SIFT. Stop: do not retweet. Investigate the source: the tweet is from an account with no verifiable identity, no historical track record on Canadian statistics. Find better coverage: search "Canada Statistics 2023 population revision" on Globe and Mail, CBC, and Statistics Canada itself. The Statistics Canada website shows annual updates and methodological notes; no "12% manipulation" appears in coverage. Trace claims: the tweet provides no link to a source document. The original claim cannot be located in any official or peer-reviewed publication. The investigation completes in ten minutes, and the tweet is identified as unreliable. The same procedure transfers to any time-sensitive historical claim — "Canada turned away X refugees in Y year" — by checking against the cited Library and Archives Canada records, the parliamentary record, or peer-reviewed history. This is not 21st-century media literacy as separate from history; it is the historian's discipline applied to the moment one inhabits.

  • Believing a story because it appeared in a slick presentation.
  • Failing to lateral-read — staying inside the source instead of cross-checking.
  • Treating all sources as equally credible — peer-reviewed publication has earned a higher prior.
  • Falling for confirmation bias — accepting content that confirms what we already believe without checking.
  • Forgetting that historical-source-criticism skills are exactly the skills needed for contemporary media literacy.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations."

📝 5.6 Citation, Plagiarism & Communicating History
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Citation is not optional decoration on top of historical writing — it is constitutive of the discipline. Without citation, an interpretation cannot be checked, an evidentiary claim cannot be tested, and the chain from source to conclusion breaks. The Ontario CHC2D curriculum requires that students "credit sources accurately to avoid plagiarism" (Strand A). The specific citation style your teacher prefers may be Chicago Notes-Bibliography (most Canadian history journals), MLA (Modern Language Association — English-language humanities), or APA (American Psychological Association — social sciences). All three exist to do the same job: allow a reader to find the source you used.

Three common citation needs in historical writing: (1) direct quotation — "verbatim words" in quotation marks with citation; (2) paraphrase — your own words restating a source's content, with citation (paraphrase still needs a citation); (3) reference to a fact specific to one source — for example, that 916 Canadians died at Dieppe needs a citation to the specific source from which that figure is taken. Common knowledge (Canada Day is 1 July; Trudeau was PM 1968–84 with one interruption) does not require citation. The line between common-knowledge and specific-source claim is judgement-dependent: when in doubt, cite. Strong historical writing cites generously.

Plagiarism is presenting another's words or ideas as one's own. It is academic dishonesty even when unintentional. The most common forms in Grade 10 work: copying a Wikipedia paragraph and lightly rewording (still plagiarism even after the rewrites); citing a textbook for one paragraph and then borrowing freely from elsewhere; using AI-generated text without disclosure or verification. Each of these is treated as plagiarism in current Ontario university and college codes; CHC2D-level work, ideally, learns the discipline now so it is automatic later. The internal logic is consistent with everything else in this unit: history rests on traceable evidence; plagiarism breaks the traceability.

Communicating history well — in essay, podcast, video, or oral presentation — has a few recurring qualities. Clarity: the thesis is identifiable; the argument moves from claim to evidence to interpretation. Voice: the writer is present without being intrusive. Evidence-density: claims are tied to specific sources; vague handwaving is rare. Engagement with counter-evidence: strong work acknowledges opposing interpretations. Honesty about uncertainty: where the evidence is contested or absent, the writer says so. Care with controversial material: residential schools, the Holocaust, Japanese-Canadian internment require particular care to avoid sensationalising suffering or instrumentalising it for argument. These qualities transfer well beyond history — they are markers of careful thinking in any discipline.

  • Chicago Notes-Bibliography — most common citation style in Canadian academic history.
  • MLA — Modern Language Association; favoured in literature and humanities.
  • APA — American Psychological Association; favoured in social sciences.
  • Plagiarism — presenting another's words or ideas as one's own.
  • Common knowledge — facts widely known and uncontested; do not require citation.
  • Generative AI disclosure — increasingly required in Canadian university honour codes (2023–24 wave of revisions).

Worked Example — Citing a Primary Source

Suppose you are citing Borden's 18 May 1917 speech announcing his support for conscription. In Chicago Notes-Bibliography: footnote — Sir Robert Borden, "Statement on Military Service" (speech to House of Commons, Ottawa, 18 May 1917), Canada, House of Commons Debates, 12th Parl., 7th Sess., vol. 2 (Ottawa: King's Printer, 1917): 1530. Bibliography entry — Borden, Sir Robert. "Statement on Military Service." Speech to House of Commons, Ottawa, 18 May 1917. Canada, House of Commons Debates, 12th Parl., 7th Sess., vol. 2, p. 1530. Ottawa: King's Printer, 1917. The point is not memorising one citation style; it is recognising what a complete citation contains — author, type of source, occasion, date, place, publisher, page — so any reader could locate the same source. In APA: Borden, R. (1917). Statement on Military Service [Speech]. House of Commons Debates, vol. 2, p. 1530. Ottawa: King's Printer. Different style, same information.

  • Skipping citations for paraphrased material — paraphrase still needs a citation.
  • Treating "I knew this already" as common knowledge — if it took source-checking to confirm, it needs citation.
  • Using AI-generated text without disclosure — this is a rapidly evolving but increasingly enforced rule.
  • Citing a textbook chapter without engaging the textbook's own footnotes — strong work uses textbooks as routes to primary sources.
  • Confusing footnote style across systems midway through a paper — pick one style and stay with it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.7 — "communicate their ideas, arguments, and conclusions using various formats and styles, as appropriate for the audience and purpose."
