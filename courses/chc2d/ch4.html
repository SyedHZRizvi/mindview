📜 4.1 Patriation & the Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Until 1982 the British North America Act, 1867 was a UK statute that could be amended only by the UK Parliament. Patriation — the formal repatriation of the Canadian Constitution to Canadian legal control — was attempted unsuccessfully under Mackenzie King (1927), Diefenbaker (1960), and Trudeau (1971, the Victoria Charter). The unresolved problem in each attempt was a domestic amending formula on which the provinces and the federal government could agree. Pierre Elliott Trudeau's October 1980 announcement of unilateral federal action — patriating without unanimous provincial consent and entrenching a Charter of Rights — provoked legal challenges from eight provinces. In Reference re Resolution to Amend the Constitution (1981), the Supreme Court ruled (7–2) that unilateral patriation was legal but (6–3) that constitutional convention required "substantial" provincial consent.

The November 1981 First Ministers' Conference produced the famous "Kitchen Accord" agreement, negotiated overnight between federal Justice Minister Jean Chrétien, Saskatchewan attorney-general Roy Romanow, and Ontario attorney-general Roy McMurtry. Nine provinces signed; Quebec under René Lévesque did not. Quebec's exclusion — what Quebec memory calls "la nuit des longs couteaux" (the Night of the Long Knives) — is a foundational grievance that shaped subsequent attempts to bring Quebec formally into the constitutional family. The Constitution Act, 1982 took effect on 17 April 1982; Quebec has never signed.

The Charter of Rights and Freedoms is the constitutional instrument that most reshaped Canadian public life. Its key sections include: section 1 (limitations subject to "reasonable limits prescribed by law" — the Oakes test); sections 2–15 (fundamental freedoms, democratic rights, mobility, legal rights, equality); section 25 (Charter does not derogate from Aboriginal rights); section 27 (multicultural-heritage interpretation); section 33 (the "notwithstanding clause" allowing legislatures to override sections 2 and 7–15 for renewable five-year periods); section 35 (existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, in a separate part of the Constitution Act, 1982). Major Charter cases through the 1980s and 1990s — Oakes 1986 (the section 1 test), Morgentaler 1988 (abortion), Schachter 1992 (remedies), Vriend 1998 (reading in sexual orientation as analogous ground) — built the doctrine.

The notwithstanding clause (section 33) has provoked particular debate. It was Romanow's negotiating concession that brought Western premiers to agreement at the Kitchen Accord. It allows a federal or provincial legislature to enact legislation despite a Charter conflict, with the override lapsing after five years unless re-enacted. From 1982 to about 2017, section 33 was rarely used by federal Parliament (never) or by most provinces. Use has accelerated since: Saskatchewan (1986 back-to-work order; 2017 school-funding case); Quebec (Bill 21, religious-symbol restrictions, 2019; Bill 96, language law, 2022); Ontario (election-spending case, 2021; Ford government's controversial 2018 Toronto-council case, the override threat eventually unused). The growth of section-33 use raises questions about whether the clause is functioning as designed — a rarely-used legislative safety valve — or being normalised as routine, weakening the Charter's role as a check on majority legislation.

  • Patriation — bringing the Constitution under exclusive Canadian legal authority.
  • Constitution Act, 1982 — the patriated instrument, including the Charter and section 35.
  • Charter of Rights and Freedoms — sections 1–34 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
  • Kitchen Accord 1981 — Chrétien-Romanow-McMurtry overnight agreement excluding Quebec.
  • Notwithstanding clause (s.33) — legislative override of certain Charter rights for renewable 5-year periods.
  • Section 35 — "existing aboriginal and treaty rights" entrenched in the Constitution Act, 1982.

Historical Significance — Was Patriation a "New Beginning" or an "Old Wound"?

Apply Seixas's significance criteria. (1) Resulting in change? Yes — patriation ended formal UK constitutional authority over Canada (the last colonial tie); the Charter became the touchstone of all subsequent rights litigation. (2) Revealing? The exclusion of Quebec revealed the persistent strain in Canadian federalism between centralist and decentralist visions. (3) Resonant? The Charter is invoked daily in Canadian public life; Quebec's non-signature remains a live political fact. (4) Whose perspective? From English-Canadian perspective, 1982 is liberation; from Quebec sovereigntist perspective, 1982 is exclusion and humiliation; from Indigenous perspective, section 35 was a partial recognition but the constitutional conferences 1983–87 that were to define its substance largely failed. A Level-4 response holds all three perspectives in view: patriation was both a major Canadian achievement and a fresh source of unresolved constitutional conflict.

  • Calling Trudeau "the author of the Constitution" — the 1982 Act was a multi-party, multi-decade negotiation; Trudeau was its principal driver.
  • Forgetting Quebec's non-signature — it is the defining ongoing constitutional fact.
  • Confusing the Charter (sections 1–34) with section 35 (Aboriginal rights) — they are in different parts of the same Act.
  • Treating section 33 as constitutionally illegitimate — it was part of the bargain; the question is whether use has become excessive.
  • Imagining that 1982 ended constitutional politics — Meech, Charlottetown, and the 1995 referendum all followed directly.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.1 — "analyse the patriation of the Constitution (1982) and the entrenchment of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms."

⚜️ 4.2 Meech, Charlottetown & the 1995 Quebec Referendum
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Meech Lake Accord (1987–90) was Brian Mulroney's attempt to bring Quebec back into the constitutional fold. The five Quebec demands articulated by Premier Robert Bourassa were modest: (1) recognition of Quebec as a "distinct society"; (2) constitutional veto for Quebec on future amendments; (3) provincial role in Supreme Court appointments; (4) restrictions on federal spending power; (5) increased Quebec immigration control. Mulroney secured agreement of all ten premiers in April 1987. The Accord required unanimous provincial ratification within three years.

The Accord collapsed in June 1990 for several reasons. (1) Manitoba's MLA Elijah Harper, the only Indigenous MLA in the legislature, blocked passage by withholding unanimous consent for a procedural motion — explicitly because Indigenous concerns about constitutional reform had been excluded from the Accord's negotiation. Harper's eagle feather is preserved in the Canadian Museum of History. (2) Newfoundland Premier Clyde Wells refused to bring it to a vote after replacing Brian Peckford. (3) Public opinion in English Canada had shifted against the "distinct society" clause, framed as Quebec privilege. (4) Pierre Trudeau, in retirement, intervened with a celebrated Maclean's essay denouncing Meech as undermining Charter equality. The Accord lapsed on 23 June 1990; the Bloc Québécois was founded by Lucien Bouchard immediately after, taking ex-PC Quebec MPs into a sovereigntist federal party.

The Charlottetown Accord (1992) was a more comprehensive reform attempt by Mulroney, the provinces, the territories, and four major national Indigenous organisations (AFN, NCC, ITC, MNC). It included: distinct-society for Quebec; an elected Senate (Triple-E: Equal, Effective, Elected); recognition of Indigenous self-government as a third order of government alongside federal and provincial; a "Canada clause" defining Canadian values. The Accord was put to a national referendum on 26 October 1992. It failed: 54.3% No nationally, with No majorities in BC, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Quebec (55.4% No, but for opposite reasons to the West), Nova Scotia, and the Yukon. Both sovereigntists and the Western Reform Party defeated the Accord. After Charlottetown, federal politics shifted toward avoiding constitutional negotiations.

The 1995 Quebec referendum (30 October 1995) on sovereignty-association produced the closest separation result in Canadian history. The PQ government under Jacques Parizeau asked Quebeckers: "Do you agree that Quebec should become sovereign, after having made a formal offer to Canada for a new Economic and Political Partnership?" The Yes side led in mid-October polls. Federal forces mobilised through the "No" coalition led by Daniel Johnson Jr. and Jean Chrétien; the Sheila Copps "Unity Rally" in Montreal on 27 October drew tens of thousands. The result, declared past midnight on 31 October: 50.58% No, 49.42% Yes — a margin of 54,288 votes out of 4.7 million cast. Parizeau's concession-speech reference to "money and the ethnic vote" produced immediate controversy and his resignation. Lucien Bouchard succeeded him as PQ leader and premier.

  • Meech Lake Accord 1987 — failed constitutional amendment to bring Quebec into the 1982 framework.
  • Elijah Harper — Cree Manitoba MLA who blocked Meech Lake ratification June 1990.
  • Charlottetown Accord 1992 — comprehensive constitutional package; defeated 54.3% No in October referendum.
  • Bloc Québécois — federal sovereigntist party founded 1990 by Lucien Bouchard.
  • 1995 referendum — Quebec sovereignty-association referendum; 50.58% No, 49.42% Yes.
  • Clarity Act 2000 — federal statute defining the requirements for a future Quebec secession referendum.

Cause-and-Consequence — Why Was the 1995 Result So Close?

Map the causes of the near-tie. Long-term causes: Quebec's 1982 non-signature; the failure of Meech (1990) and Charlottetown (1992); the Bloc's success in 1993 federal election (54 seats, Official Opposition); the PQ's 1994 provincial election win. Medium-term causes: economic anxiety in the early 1990s (recession 1990–92); generational shift toward younger sovereigntists; effective PQ campaign messaging on partnership rather than separation. Short-term causes: the September 1995 Bouchard speech "Le Québec, c'est toujours un autre pays" galvanised the Yes side; federal complacency in the early campaign; the leadership-bypassing late entry of Lucien Bouchard as "negotiator" (designating Bouchard as chief negotiator with Canada in event of Yes victory). Consequences: the Clarity Act (2000) defining future-referendum requirements; the Calgary Declaration (1997) recognising Quebec's "unique character"; the federal "Sponsorship Programme" 1996–2004 that ended in the Gomery Commission scandal 2004; a long period of declining Quebec sovereigntist support through the 2000s and 2010s. A Level-4 response identifies short, medium, and long-term causes and weighs them.

  • Saying "Quebec almost left" — the question was sovereignty-association, not bare independence; the precise meaning of a Yes was contested.
  • Forgetting Harper's role in killing Meech — Indigenous opposition was decisive.
  • Treating Charlottetown's defeat as a Quebec rejection alone — the West voted No more decisively.
  • Calling Parizeau's resignation "voluntary" — the "money and ethnic vote" speech made it inevitable.
  • Imagining the issue ended in 1995 — the Clarity Act and Quebec's nation-recognition motion (2006) continued the work.

🏔️ 4.3 Oka, Nisga'a & the Long Road to Reconciliation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Oka Crisis (11 July – 26 September 1990) was a 78-day armed standoff between the Mohawk community of Kanesatake, the Sûreté du Québec, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, and ultimately about 4,500 Canadian Forces troops. The dispute centred on the planned expansion of an Oka, Quebec, golf course onto a Mohawk burial ground (les Pins) the municipality claimed was public land. Mohawks had occupied the disputed land since March 1990 in a peaceful resistance. On 11 July, the SQ assaulted the barricade; in the resulting exchange, SQ Corporal Marcel Lemay was killed. The Mohawk community at Kahnawake closed the Mercier Bridge to Montreal in solidarity, paralysing Montreal's south-shore commute. The federal government deployed the army on 8 August. The crisis ended without further fatalities on 26 September with negotiated withdrawal.

Oka was the most televised Indigenous confrontation in Canadian history to that point. The iconic photograph by Shaney Komulainen of Mohawk Warrior "Lasagna" (Ronald Cross) and Canadian soldier Patrick Cloutier face-to-face on 1 September is one of the most-reproduced images in Canadian visual memory. The federal government's purchase of the disputed land in 1991 was acknowledgement of Mohawk claims but not transfer to Mohawk sovereignty — a partial resolution that left underlying questions unaddressed. The Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (RCAP, 1991–96) was established in direct response to Oka. RCAP's 4,000-page final report (1996) called for a fundamental rebuilding of the Indigenous-Canadian relationship: replacement of the Indian Act, an Aboriginal parliament, treaty modernisation, residential-schools redress. Most RCAP recommendations were not implemented.

The Nisga'a Final Agreement (2000) was the first modern BC land-claims treaty. The Nisga'a Nation in the Nass Valley had pursued their land claim through the Indian agencies (Calder 1968–73), the courts (Calder v. BC 1973), and the BC Treaty Commission process (founded 1992). The 2000 agreement recognised Nisga'a self-government over 1,930 km² of their traditional territory and transferred $190 million in compensation. The Nisga'a Lisims Government replaced the Indian Act bands as the legal authority. The treaty has been operating without major dispute since. It was the first of the "modern treaty" agreements in BC — the Tsawwassen (2007), Maa-nulth (2011), and Tla'amin (2016) followed.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC, 2008–2015) was the central institution of the residential-schools reckoning. Established under the 2007 Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (the largest class-action settlement in Canadian history, totalling about $5 billion in compensation), the TRC was chaired by Justice Murray Sinclair. Between 2010 and 2015 the TRC held statements from over 6,750 survivors, family members, and former staff at hearings across Canada. Stephen Harper's 2008 House of Commons apology preceded the TRC's work; in 2017 Pope Francis declined to issue a Catholic Church apology, but did so in person in Maskwacis, Alberta, on 25 July 2022. The TRC's Final Report (December 2015) included 94 Calls to Action covering child welfare, education, language, health, justice, reconciliation, settlement, equity, business, sports, media, museums, missing children, and Vatican apology. As of 2024 about 13 of the 94 Calls have been fully implemented; tracking is maintained by the Yellowhead Institute.

  • Oka Crisis 1990 — 78-day Mohawk-Quebec-Canada standoff over land at Kanesatake.
  • RCAP 1991–96 — Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples, 4,000-page report.
  • Nisga'a Final Agreement 2000 — first modern BC treaty; created Nisga'a self-government.
  • TRC 2008–15 — Truth and Reconciliation Commission on residential schools.
  • 94 Calls to Action 2015 — TRC's policy recommendations across all sectors.
  • UNDRIP — UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; Canada endorsed unconditionally 2016, federal statute 2021.

Continuity and Change — The Long Path from 1982 Section 35 to 2015 TRC

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 entrenched "existing aboriginal and treaty rights" but left their substance undefined. Trace the definition through court rulings and policy moments. 1990 Sparrow: section 35 rights can be limited only with justified infringement; Crown has fiduciary duty. 1990: Oka Crisis brings Indigenous-federal relations into mass public view. 1995 Vander Peet: defines "integral to distinctive culture" test. 1997 Delgamuukw: oral histories are admissible evidence for aboriginal title; aboriginal title is a sui generis right. 1998: federal "Statement of Reconciliation" by Jane Stewart. 2007: Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement. 2008: Harper's parliamentary apology. 2014 Tsilhqot'in: first declaration of aboriginal title at common law. 2015 TRC Final Report. 2016: Canada endorses UNDRIP without qualifications. 2021 UNDRIP Act. 2021–22: discovery of unmarked graves at Tk'emlúps and Marieval residential schools cemeteries. A Level-4 response shows both continuity (slow, contested, still-incomplete progress) and change (substantive movement on title, residential schools, treaty modernisation).

  • Calling Oka a "Mohawk-Canada" conflict — the immediate parties were Mohawk, Oka municipality, and SQ; federal involvement came later.
  • Treating RCAP as successful policy — most recommendations were never implemented.
  • Confusing the Nisga'a Agreement (a treaty) with the Indian Act bands it replaced.
  • Saying the TRC was "the apology" — the apology was Harper's 2008; the TRC was the truth-finding institution.
  • Forgetting that the Catholic Church's role in residential schools was a long sticking point until the 2022 Maskwacis visit.

🤝 4.4 NAFTA, Globalisation & the Free-Trade Era
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Canada-United States Free Trade Agreement (CUSFTA, 1988, in force 1 January 1989) was the largest Canadian economic-policy reorientation since the postwar settlement. Conservative Prime Minister Brian Mulroney had campaigned in 1984 partly on tighter US relations and partly on caution about formal free trade — the "Free Trade Election" of November 1988 became the explicit referendum on the agreement. Liberal leader John Turner attacked CUSFTA in the leaders' debate: "I happen to believe you've sold us out." The PC majority of 169 seats was reduced but maintained; the Liberals took 83 seats; the NDP 43. CUSFTA passed Parliament; it took effect 1 January 1989.

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA, 1992 negotiated, in force 1 January 1994) extended the framework to include Mexico, creating a 360-million-person free-trade area. NAFTA included controversial chapters on investor-state dispute settlement (Chapter 11), intellectual property, labour, and environment side-agreements. Canada's trade with the US grew from $235 billion (1989) to $735 billion (2017) over the agreement's three decades. Manufacturing-employment statistics are more contested: Ontario's auto industry consolidated and grew; Quebec apparel and footwear declined; Western resource exports expanded.

The 2017–2020 NAFTA renegotiation under U.S. President Donald Trump produced the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA / CUSMA, in force 1 July 2020). Changes include: stricter automotive rules-of-origin (75% North American content); a labour-value content requirement that auto workers earn at least USD $16/hour for content to count; a 16-year sunset clause with 6-year review; modified dispute-settlement; updated digital-trade and intellectual-property chapters. The new agreement's auto-sector provisions were partially responsible for the relocation of some automotive investment into Canada and away from Mexico in the early 2020s.

The historical-thinking question is whether free trade was the "abandonment of the national project" some critics named, or the necessary adaptation to globalisation it became. Three honest observations: (1) Canadian wages and productivity have grown more slowly than the US economy's since 1989, complicating the "lifted by free trade" narrative; (2) Canadian export concentration toward the US has risen from about 75% (1989) to about 75% (2024) — i.e., remained roughly constant, but at higher absolute volume; (3) free trade has not produced uniform regional outcomes — Ontario manufacturing benefited; Quebec light-manufacturing declined; Western resource exports thrived. Whether free trade has been a net good for "Canada" depends substantially on which region and which sector one looks at.

  • CUSFTA 1989 — Canada-US Free Trade Agreement; subject of the 1988 federal election.
  • NAFTA 1994 — North American Free Trade Agreement; Canada, US, Mexico.
  • USMCA / CUSMA 2020 — renegotiated NAFTA under Trump-Trudeau-AMLO governments.
  • Brian Mulroney — Conservative PM 1984–93; signed CUSFTA, GST, Meech, NAFTA, redress agreements.
  • Investor-state dispute settlement — mechanism allowing foreign investors to sue host governments under trade-agreement protections.
  • Auto Pact 1965 — earlier US-Canada sector-specific free-trade agreement; ended 2001 under WTO ruling.

Cause-and-Consequence — Mapping the Trade Realignment

Was CUSFTA inevitable? Causes: (1) post-Bretton-Woods 1971 erosion of global tariff order; (2) Macdonald Commission Report 1985 ("Macdonald Plan") recommended free trade; (3) US tariff threats under Reagan; (4) Mulroney's political philosophy of closer US engagement; (5) Canadian business-community advocacy through the Business Council on National Issues. Counterfactual: could Canada have continued the "National Policy" tariff approach? Some economists argue yes, at the cost of slower growth; others argue no, because US protectionism would have hardened. Consequences: economic integration accelerated; manufacturing geography shifted; the federal government's industrial-policy tools were partly constrained (Auto Pact ended 2001; investor-state suits accumulated); GDP grew but wage growth was uneven. Foreseeable consequences: the realignment was substantially predicted by 1985 forecasters, including the regional distributional asymmetries. Unforeseen consequences: the rise of "deep" trade agreements covering services, intellectual property, and dispute settlement; the 2017 reversal of US trade liberalism. A Level-4 response weights causes by their explanatory power and distinguishes foreseen from unforeseen consequences.

  • Saying "Canada joined NAFTA in 1988" — CUSFTA in 1989; NAFTA in 1994; USMCA in 2020. Distinguish the three.
  • Treating free trade as uniformly beneficial or uniformly harmful — regional and sectoral outcomes differ significantly.
  • Forgetting the 1988 election was effectively a free-trade referendum — its result settled the policy.
  • Confusing the Auto Pact (1965, sectoral) with CUSFTA (1989, comprehensive).
  • Overstating Trump's redesign — USMCA preserved most of NAFTA's structure while tightening rules of origin and adding sunset.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.3 — "describe Canada's role in the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA 1994 / USMCA 2020)."

🪖 4.5 9/11, Afghanistan & the Iraq Decision (2001–2014)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The 11 September 2001 al-Qaeda attacks on the World Trade Center and Pentagon killed about 2,977 people, including 24 Canadians. Closed US airspace diverted some 33,000 passengers in 224 transatlantic aircraft to Canadian airports; "Operation Yellow Ribbon" coordinated their housing and feeding, most famously at Gander, Newfoundland (population 9,000, hosting 38 aircraft and 6,700 passengers — later memorialised in the musical Come from Away). The Chrétien government invoked NATO Article 5 on 12 September. Canada deployed special forces (JTF-2) to Afghanistan in late 2001 alongside US operations against the Taliban regime.

The Canadian commitment to Afghanistan expanded dramatically in 2003. Operation Athena committed the Canadian Forces to ISAF (International Security Assistance Force) in Kabul as the primary military commitment. From 2005 onward, Canadian forces relocated to Kandahar Province — the most dangerous deployment, in the Taliban heartland — under successive task forces. About 40,000 Canadians served over the 2001–2014 deployment. The combat mission ended in July 2011; the training mission ended in March 2014. Canadian war dead totalled 158 military, one diplomat, one journalist, two civilian contractors, and many wounded. The Highway of Heroes — the Ontario 401 stretch between CFB Trenton and Toronto coroner's office — became the public-mourning route for the returned fallen.

The 2003 Iraq decision was perhaps the most consequential foreign-policy decision of the Chrétien era. Despite intense US pressure and the formal US-UK case at the UN Security Council, Prime Minister Chrétien announced in the House of Commons on 17 March 2003 that Canada would not participate in the Iraq invasion. His stated grounds: the UN Security Council had not authorised force; weapons-of-mass-destruction claims were unproven; the case had not met the international-law threshold for war. The decision required quiet but firm pushback against US Ambassador Cellucci's public displeasure. The decision is now generally cited — including by US strategists in retrospect — as the right one. Iraqi WMD did not exist; the war's costs to all parties were vast.

The longer war-on-terror policy framework reshaped Canadian law. The Anti-Terrorism Act 2001 expanded preventive-detention and security-certificate provisions. The Maher Arar case (2002–06) — in which a dual Canadian-Syrian citizen was rendered to Syria for torture based on faulty RCMP information — exposed the costs of post-9/11 security cooperation. The 2006 O'Connor Inquiry's findings led to a Canadian apology and $10.5M settlement. The 2015 Anti-terrorism Act (Bill C-51, Harper) expanded CSIS powers, generating significant civil-liberties criticism; the 2017 National Security Act (Bill C-59, Trudeau) modified some provisions while preserving the broader framework. The historical-thinking continuity is the post-9/11 shift toward expanded national-security state authority across both Conservative and Liberal governments — a shift comparable in scope to the WWII expansion of state power, and similarly under-reversed at the conflict's end.

  • 9/11 — 11 September 2001 al-Qaeda attacks on US targets.
  • Operation Yellow Ribbon — Canadian reception of diverted US-bound aircraft.
  • Operation Athena — Canadian Forces operation in Afghanistan 2003–11.
  • Kandahar — Province where most Canadian combat operations and deaths occurred.
  • Highway of Heroes — Ontario 401 stretch used to repatriate Canadian war dead.
  • Maher Arar case — wrongful rendition of dual Canadian citizen to torture in Syria.

Historical Perspective — Why Did Chrétien Refuse Iraq but Commit to Afghanistan?

Apply "historical perspective": reconstruct the 2003 logic. Afghanistan (2001) had multiple legitimating factors: (1) NATO Article 5 invocation after a direct attack on a NATO member; (2) UN Security Council Resolution 1373 supporting counter-terrorism response; (3) the Taliban regime's sheltering of al-Qaeda; (4) broad international consensus on the operation. Iraq (2003) had none of these: (1) no Article 5; (2) UN Security Council split and inspection process ongoing; (3) no clear evidence linking Saddam to al-Qaeda; (4) major Western allies (France, Germany) opposed. From Chrétien's perspective, the relevant test was multilateral legal authority, not US bilateral request. The decision aligned with Pearsonian multilateralism: Canada supports international law and the UN when force is at stake. The retrospective vindication — Iraqi WMD did not exist — confirms the analytical framework Chrétien applied. A Level-4 response shows that "the same government" could rationally make different decisions on different operations.

  • Calling Afghanistan "America's war" — it was a NATO operation; Canada was a full participant.
  • Confusing Iraq (no Canadian troops) with Afghanistan (Canadian combat 2001–14).
  • Treating the security-state expansion as bipartisan accident — it was deliberate policy under both Conservative and Liberal governments.
  • Forgetting Maher Arar — the case is a key civil-liberties moment.
  • Underrating the long Kandahar mission — Canada's combat there was sustained and costly relative to the deployment size.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.2 — "analyse Canada's role in post-9/11 conflicts including Afghanistan 2001–2014 and the decision not to join the Iraq invasion 2003."

🌐 4.6 The 21st Century — Climate, Pandemic, Reconciliation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The contemporary period (roughly 2000–present) sees three policy domains shaping Canadian politics: climate, reconciliation, and global crisis response. Climate-policy history begins with the 1992 Rio Convention on Biological Diversity and the 1997 Kyoto Protocol (ratified by Canada under Chrétien 2002; withdrew under Harper 2011); the 2015 Paris Agreement (signed by Trudeau, embedded into law via the Canadian Net-Zero Emissions Accountability Act 2021); the 2018 federal carbon pricing (the "backstop" applied to provinces without their own); the 2024 review of net-zero progress showing Canada off-track for 2030 targets. Climate policy has been the most consistently bitter inter-provincial fight of the post-2010 period: Alberta and Saskatchewan have opposed federal pricing on jurisdictional and economic grounds; the Supreme Court upheld federal pricing in References re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act (2021) by a 6–3 majority.

The COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020 – approximately 2023) was the largest mass-mortality event in Canada since the 1918 influenza pandemic. Canadian deaths totalled approximately 60,000 by early 2024. The federal Canada Emergency Response Benefit (CERB) and Canada Emergency Wage Subsidy (CEWS) injected roughly $230 billion into the economy in 2020 alone; the federal-debt-to-GDP ratio rose from about 30% (2019) to 47% (2022). The vaccine roll-out from December 2020 onward made Canada one of the most-vaccinated populations globally (over 85% double-dosed in adult population by late 2021). The 2022 Freedom Convoy and the federal government's first peacetime invocation of the Emergencies Act (14–23 February 2022) represented a parallel domestic-civil-disorder crisis. The 2024 Public Order Emergency Commission's Rouleau Report concluded the invocation was legally justified but on narrow grounds, with some criticism of preparation and execution.

Reconciliation work after 2015 produced both progress and visible incompleteness. The 2008 residential-schools apology, the 2015 TRC, and the 2019 National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (with its finding of "race-based genocide" — controversial but accepted by the Trudeau government) framed federal policy. The Kamloops Indian Residential School discovery of 215 anomalies (later identified as likely unmarked graves) in May 2021 — followed by similar discoveries at Cowessess, Cranbrook, and others — produced national mourning, the addition of 30 September as the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (federally observed since 2021), and intensified pressure for federal action on the 94 Calls to Action. As of 2024, about 13 Calls were fully implemented. The 2021 UNDRIP Act brings UN principles into federal law. Progress on individual Calls remains contested.

Foreign policy under Trudeau (Justin, 2015–) has been characterised by "Canada is back" multilateral re-engagement (Paris Agreement, return to UN peacekeeping commitments — substantially unrealised), G7 hosting, complicated relations with China (the 2018 Meng Wanzhou case and the related detention of Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor 2018–21), the 2022 invasion of Ukraine and Canadian sanctions/weapons support, and complicated US relations under both Trump (NAFTA renegotiation, 2017 tariffs on Canadian aluminum and steel) and Biden. The 2025 federal election under Mark Carney's Liberal government and Trump's second-term tariff threats represent the current chapter — too recent for historical synthesis, but worth tracking. The course concludes with an honest acknowledgment that the present is, by definition, the not-yet-historical: students should learn the tools of historical analysis precisely so they can apply them as the present passes into history.

  • Kyoto Protocol — 1997 climate treaty; Canada ratified 2002, withdrew 2011.
  • Paris Agreement — 2015 climate treaty; Canada signed and incorporated 2016, codified in 2021.
  • Federal carbon pricing — 2018 backstop; upheld by Supreme Court 2021.
  • COVID-19 pandemic — 2020–2023; major Canadian fiscal and public-health response.
  • Emergencies Act 2022 — first peacetime invocation; in response to Freedom Convoy.
  • National Day for Truth and Reconciliation — 30 September; federally observed since 2021.
  • MMIWG Inquiry 2019 — Final Report on Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls.

Application — Doing Contemporary History

The Grade 10 student learning this course will live the rest of "since World War I" — that is, will participate in its making. Apply the six historical thinking concepts to a current event of your choosing (climate policy, pandemic response, reconciliation progress, an election, a war). (1) Historical significance: how would a future historian rank this event? Which actors and consequences will matter in 20 years? (2) Cause and consequence: distinguish long, medium, and short-term causes; foreseen and unforeseen consequences. (3) Continuity and change: what does this event continue from earlier patterns? What does it change? (4) Historical perspective: how do the participants understand the event differently from how observers will later? (5) Evidence: what are the sources for this event — and which will survive into the historical record? (6) Ethical dimensions: what fair judgment can be made about actor conduct, and what responsibilities does this judgment create? The exercise transfers Grade 10 history into civic literacy.

  • Treating "recent" history as outside the discipline — historians do analyse contemporary events using the same tools.
  • Reading the present as endpoint — every "present" becomes the past of some future student.
  • Forgetting that climate policy has been Canadian federal-provincial controversy for over 30 years.
  • Imagining the TRC completed reconciliation — it did not; it created a framework.
  • Calling COVID-19 a "natural disaster" — its mortality patterns were profoundly shaped by policy choices, demographic vulnerability, and pre-existing inequalities.
