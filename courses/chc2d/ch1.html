⚔️ 1.1 Canada Enters the Great War (1914–1916)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

When Britain declared war on Germany at 11 p.m. on 4 August 1914, Canada was automatically at war — a colonial reflex of the British North America Act. Canada had no separate foreign policy, no independent army, and no diplomatic representation abroad. Prime Minister Sir Robert Borden's government had only to decide the scale of contribution, not whether to contribute. Within six weeks 30,000 men had assembled at Valcartier, Quebec, under Minister of Militia Sam Hughes's chaotic organisation; the first contingent of the Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF) sailed for Britain on 3 October 1914 in the largest fleet to that point ever to cross the Atlantic.

The early enthusiasm was real and remarkable. Recruitment posters promised "King and Country," and within a year over 150,000 had volunteered. Most were British-born immigrants who saw themselves as defending the empire. French-Canadian enlistment was much lower — only about 35,000 of the eventual 619,000 — for reasons that included Bishop Bourassa's opposition, the Ontario Schools Question (Regulation 17 of 1912 had restricted French-language education), and the fact that the CEF was almost entirely English-speaking and commanded in English. This early divide foreshadowed the 1917 conscription crisis.

The Second Battle of Ypres (April–May 1915) was Canada's first major engagement and its first encounter with chlorine gas. The French colonial troops on the Canadians' left broke under the gas cloud; the Canadians held a four-mile front for 48 hours, urinating on handkerchiefs as makeshift respirators. They suffered 6,500 casualties of 18,000 troops engaged. The battle established a Canadian reputation for tenacity but, in tactical terms, was a defensive holding action — not the offensive victory that the Vimy myth would later require.

On the home front, Borden's government passed the War Measures Act (22 August 1914), giving Cabinet sweeping powers to act by Order-in-Council. Ukrainians, Germans, and other "enemy aliens" (those born in the Austro-Hungarian or German empires) were required to register; about 8,579 were interned in 24 camps, many in Canada's emerging national parks. They were unpaid labour. This first use of the Act established a template — invoked again in 1939 and 1970 — for the suspension of civil liberties in a declared emergency.

  • Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF) — the army Canada raised for overseas service in WWI; eventually over 619,000 served.
  • War Measures Act (1914) — federal legislation granting emergency executive powers to the Cabinet.
  • Enemy aliens — citizens of nations at war with Britain (Germany, Austria-Hungary, later Ottoman Empire) living in Canada.
  • Sam Hughes — controversial Minister of Militia 1911–16; championed the Canadian-made Ross rifle, which jammed in mud and was withdrawn 1916.
  • Regulation 17 (1912) — Ontario directive restricting French-language education in primary schools; a major French-English grievance.

Source Analysis — Recruitment Poster Reading

A 1915 recruitment poster ("Why don't they come?") depicts a soldier in the trenches reaching backward toward a civilian in a Canadian small town. The composition forces the viewer to identify with the implied gap: the soldier looks weary; the civilian sits idle. The text reaches the reader through guilt, not argument. A historical-thinking analysis would ask: Who produced this (the Canadian Patriotic Fund, a private organisation, not the federal government — meaning private actors were already pressuring enlistment); What does it leave out (the casualty rate of CEF battalions like the 22nd "Van Doos" was over 30%, but the poster shows no graves); What perspective is missing (French-Canadian, Indigenous, and pacifist Christian objections were widely held but absent from public visual culture). A Level-4 source analysis would link the poster to the broader phenomenon of "moral coercion" that, by 1917, made voluntary recruitment insufficient and required conscription.

  • Treating the war as something Canada "chose" to enter — it was automatic.
  • Calling Ypres a "victory" — it was a costly defensive stand, not an offensive triumph.
  • Lumping all "enemy aliens" together as German — most internees were Ukrainian (over 5,000).
  • Ignoring the religious and ethnic structures of recruitment (Anglican, British-born vs. French-Catholic, Indigenous).
  • Reading 1914 enthusiasm through the lens of 1918 disillusionment.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "describe some key social, economic, and political events of Canada's participation in World War I."

🏔️ 1.2 Vimy Ridge & the Hundred Days (1917–1918)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Vimy Ridge (9–12 April 1917) is the most-mythologised battle in Canadian history, and the gap between the event and the myth is the substance of any honest Grade 10 inquiry. The tactical facts: for the first time in the war, all four Canadian divisions fought as one corps under a Canadian-born commander (Lt-Gen Arthur Currie, who took over later in 1917; the Vimy planner was British Lt-Gen Julian Byng). The 100,000-strong Canadian Corps captured a seven-kilometre escarpment that had defeated French and British attacks since 1914. They used innovative methods: precise reconnaissance via aerial photography; a "creeping barrage" that moved 100 metres every three minutes ahead of the advancing infantry; underground subways from which the troops emerged onto the German lines; and platoon-level small-unit training that gave NCOs the authority to act in their officer's absence. Canadian casualties were 10,602 in four days — 3,598 dead. They took the ridge by 12 April.

The strategic significance is more contested. Vimy was part of the larger Battle of Arras, a British operation that overall failed. Holding the ridge did not change the war's outcome that year. The battle's symbolic significance, however, was constructed deliberately over the next two decades. Pierre Berton's 1986 book Vimy and the 1936 dedication of the Walter Allward memorial both emphasised the "birth of a nation" reading. Historians like Tim Cook and Jonathan Vance have shown that this reading was a postwar construction — a way of giving the appalling casualty list (66,000 dead overall) retrospective meaning. The historical thinking question is therefore not "did Vimy birth Canadian nationhood?" but "how and why did this become the dominant story?"

Currie's leadership during the Hundred Days Offensive (8 August – 11 November 1918) is the more substantive tactical achievement. The Canadian Corps, by then at full strength, spearheaded the breakthrough at Amiens (the "Black Day of the German Army" per Ludendorff), broke the Drocourt-Quéant line, crossed the Canal du Nord, and liberated Cambrai and Mons (where Canadian Pte. George Price was killed at 10:58 a.m. on 11 November, two minutes before the armistice took effect). Of the 100 days, the CEF was in continuous offensive operations for the final 96. Canadian casualties were 45,830 in those three months — three times higher per day than even Vimy.

The cumulative cost — 60,661 Canadian war dead from a population of about 8 million — produced a generation marked by absence. Almost every Canadian small town raised a cenotaph between 1919 and 1925; the list of names on those monuments is the most accessible primary source for the war's local impact and a key entry point for student inquiry.

  • Canadian Corps — the four-division Canadian formation in the British Expeditionary Force.
  • Creeping barrage — artillery technique of advancing the shell-curtain ahead of attacking infantry at timed intervals.
  • Arthur Currie — first Canadian-born commander of the Canadian Corps from June 1917; knighted 1917.
  • Hundred Days Offensive — the Allied offensive 8 Aug – 11 Nov 1918 that ended the war; the Canadian Corps was the spearhead.
  • Walter Allward — Toronto sculptor who designed the Vimy Memorial (unveiled 1936) — two soaring pylons of Trau limestone.

Historical Significance — Was Vimy a "Birth of a Nation"?

Apply Seixas's significance criteria. (1) Resulting in change? Yes — the Canadian Corps' performance bolstered Borden's argument at Imperial War Conferences in 1917–18 for a Canadian voice in war policy; this contributed to Canada's separate signature on the Treaty of Versailles 1919 and separate seat at the League of Nations. (2) Revealing? The battle reveals the cost of empire participation and the political weight that battlefield reputation could carry. (3) Resonant? Vimy remains heavily commemorated — Canadian banknotes, the memorial restoration of 2007, classroom emphasis. But significance also depends on perspective: Indigenous veterans (estimated 4,000+, including snipers Henry Norwest and Francis Pegahmagabow with 378 confirmed kills) and Black battalions (No. 2 Construction Battalion, segregated, restricted to labour) were excluded from the postwar "birth of a nation" framing well into the 1990s. A nuanced answer therefore says: significant for shaping a (partial) national myth, less significant strategically, and the significance changes depending on whose nation we mean.

  • Calling Vimy "the most important battle of WWI" — it was not the war's most important battle, only the most Canadian.
  • Treating "the birth of a nation" as historical fact rather than a historiographical claim made in retrospect.
  • Crediting Currie with Vimy — the plan was Byng's; Currie commanded the 1st Division within it.
  • Forgetting the Hundred Days, which was militarily more decisive.
  • Ignoring Indigenous, Black, and immigrant Canadian service in the CEF.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "explain how some events from World War I and the postwar period contributed to a developing sense of Canadian identity."

⚖️ 1.3 The Conscription Crisis & the 1917 Election
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

By 1917, voluntary enlistment had collapsed. Casualties at the Somme (24,029 Canadian) had exceeded enlistments; the 50,000-troop minimum Borden had promised the Imperial War Cabinet in February 1917 could not be met by recruiting alone. Borden returned from London in May 1917 convinced that conscription — compulsory military service — was the only path. The Military Service Act received Royal Assent on 29 August 1917, requiring registration of all male British subjects between 20 and 45.

The political consequences split the country along its deepest fault lines. In Quebec, opposition was nearly unanimous. Bishop Bourassa argued in Le Devoir that Quebec had no quarrel with Germany; that the British Empire was not Quebec's empire; that Regulation 17 had shown Anglo-Canada's willingness to attack the French language; and that French Canadians had filled their share of the volunteer ranks proportional to their willingness to defend a state that recognised them. Riots broke out in Quebec City over Easter 1918, killing four civilians when troops opened fire. In English Canada, especially among Anglican and Protestant churches, the press, and farm/labour groups in the West, conscription was framed as fair distribution of sacrifice.

Borden's government took two extraordinary measures to ensure conscription's electoral survival. The Military Voters Act (September 1917) gave the vote to all overseas soldiers and allowed the government to direct their votes to constituencies of its choosing. The Wartime Elections Act (September 1917) extended the franchise to women who were close female relatives of soldiers — the first federal female enfranchisement — while simultaneously disenfranchising naturalised citizens from "enemy" countries who had become Canadian after 1902. These were the first federal women's votes, but they were instrumental — women were granted the franchise because they were expected to vote for conscription, not because of a principled commitment to equality.

Borden then formed a Union Government with English-speaking Liberals who supported conscription, and crushed Wilfrid Laurier's anti-conscription Liberals in the December 1917 election: Unionists 153 seats, Laurier Liberals 82. Of the 82 Liberal seats, 62 were in Quebec. Conscription was implemented, but it produced only about 47,000 men to the front and tore Quebec from federal political loyalty for a generation. Mackenzie King would build his Liberal coalition on the lesson — promising in 1939 "no conscription for overseas service," and only breaking that promise in 1944 after a national plebiscite.

  • Conscription — compulsory military service required by law.
  • Military Service Act 1917 — the federal law mandating conscription.
  • Henri Bourassa — Quebec nationalist, editor of Le Devoir, leading conscription opponent.
  • Wartime Elections Act 1917 — gave the vote to female relatives of soldiers; disenfranchised post-1902 immigrants from enemy nations.
  • Union Government 1917 — Borden's wartime coalition of Conservatives and pro-conscription Liberals.

Cause-and-Consequence — Map of the 1917 Election

Long-term causes: imperial-colonial structure of Canadian foreign policy; English-French linguistic and religious divide; the unresolved Regulation 17 dispute; falling 1917 voluntary enlistment from over 30,000/month in 1915 to under 5,000/month by spring 1917. Short-term causes: spring 1917 Imperial War Conference commitments; spring 1917 Vimy casualties; Borden's personal conversion to compulsion. Intended consequence: 50,000 conscripts to the front. Unintended consequences: the Easter 1918 Quebec City riots; the decades-long alienation of Quebec from the federal Conservative party; the precedent of instrumentalising women's enfranchisement; the cementing of King's later "conscription if necessary, but not necessarily conscription" formula. A Level-4 response would distinguish intended from unintended consequences and weigh them against each other.

  • Believing conscription was a partisan issue when in fact pro-conscription Liberals joined Union government.
  • Forgetting that women's federal enfranchisement began under conscription pressure, not democratic conviction.
  • Treating Bourassa as merely "pro-German" — his argument was about imperial structure, not German sympathy.
  • Underestimating the effect on Quebec's federal political alignment for the next sixty years.
  • Imagining that conscription produced large numbers at the front — only about 24,000 conscripts actually reached the front lines before armistice.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "analyse the conscription crisis of 1917 and its impact on Canada."

💥 1.4 The Halifax Explosion (6 December 1917)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

At 9:04:35 a.m. on 6 December 1917, two ships collided in the Halifax Narrows: the Norwegian-registered SS Imo, sailing empty to New York, and the French munitions ship SS Mont-Blanc, loaded with 2,653 metric tonnes of TNT, picric acid, benzol, and guncotton bound for the Western Front. The Mont-Blanc caught fire after the collision and drifted toward Halifax's Richmond district. Crowds gathered to watch from windows and rooftops. At 9:04:35 the fire reached the cargo and detonated.

The explosion released energy equivalent to roughly 2.9 kilotonnes of TNT — the largest human-made explosion before Hiroshima 1945. The blast wave levelled 2.5 km² of Halifax, killed approximately 1,782 people instantly, injured 9,000, and blinded an estimated 600 (mostly from window-glass shrapnel). A 28-metre tsunami rolled up the harbour. A church bell from St. Joseph's Church landed 4 km away. The shock was felt at Cape Breton, 270 km distant. Half-tonne fragments of the Mont-Blanc's anchor flew 4 km inland.

Two contextual factors made the disaster denser. First, the explosion occurred during wartime mobilisation, when the harbour was at peak military traffic and skilled firefighters and pilots were stretched thin. Second, a winter blizzard hit Halifax the following morning, dropping 41 cm of snow and freezing the rescuers, the homeless, and the wounded. The Massachusetts Relief Committee — sending a train of doctors, nurses, and supplies from Boston within hours of news reaching the United States — became the basis for the Christmas tree Halifax has sent to Boston annually since 1971.

The explosion also exposes the way race and class shaped wartime tragedy. Africville, the Black community on the harbour's north shore, was devastated. Mi'kmaq communities on the Dartmouth side were displaced. Reconstruction was uneven: middle-class south-end Halifax recovered quickly; Richmond and Africville did not. The Halifax Relief Commission, established in January 1918, paid compensation, but Indigenous and Black claimants received systematically less. The disaster therefore is a useful case study not only of the war's reach into Canadian life, but of how disasters magnify pre-existing inequities — a lens that transfers to twenty-first-century disaster studies.

  • SS Mont-Blanc — French munitions ship that exploded; cargo destined for the Western Front.
  • SS Imo — Norwegian relief ship; collided with Mont-Blanc; later refloated.
  • Richmond District — north-end Halifax neighbourhood largely destroyed.
  • Halifax Relief Commission — federal body that compensated victims and rebuilt north-end Halifax.
  • Massachusetts Relief Committee — Boston-based rescue and reconstruction support; origin of the Halifax-Boston Christmas-tree tradition.

Source Comparison — Newspaper Coverage

The Halifax Herald (7 Dec 1917, front page) blamed "German plot" for the explosion in its first hours of coverage; this rumour persisted for weeks despite no evidence. By contrast, the official Wreck Commissioner's inquiry (1918) found the captains of both ships at fault, citing the Royal Navy's wartime use of the harbour without proper traffic control. A historical-thinking comparison would: (1) note the wartime framing — Germany was the all-purpose villain in 1917 Anglo-Canadian newspapers, so the "plot" reading served emotional needs more than evidentiary ones; (2) note that the inquiry's "shared fault" finding was contested for decades, with later courts shifting blame more decisively to the Imo; (3) draw the lesson that proximity to the event is not the same as understanding of it. Primary sources written close to the moment carry the moment's anxieties and may mislead exactly because they feel "live."

  • Confusing the Halifax Explosion with later disasters (e.g., the 1947 Texas City explosion, the 2020 Beirut explosion).
  • Believing the German-sabotage theory — it was disproved by 1918 but lingers in popular memory.
  • Underestimating the secondary blizzard, which doubled the death toll.
  • Treating the disaster as race-neutral when the destruction was concentrated in working-class and Black neighbourhoods.
  • Forgetting that the war's logistics — munitions in a civilian harbour — are what made the disaster possible at all.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "describe some key social, economic, and political events of Canada's participation in World War I (e.g., the Halifax Explosion)."

🗳️ 1.5 Women's Suffrage & the Persons Case
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The path to Canadian women's enfranchisement was provincial and gradual, not federal and singular. Manitoba was first (28 January 1916), followed by Saskatchewan (March 1916) and Alberta (April 1916). Ontario and B.C. extended the provincial franchise in 1917. The federal franchise came in two stages: the Wartime Elections Act (September 1917) enfranchised female relatives of soldiers as a conscription-supporting measure, and the Act to Confer the Electoral Franchise upon Women (24 May 1918) extended the federal vote to all women on the same terms as men — that is, to white women over 21. Status Indian, Inuit, Chinese-Canadian, Japanese-Canadian, and South-Asian-Canadian women were excluded from the federal franchise on racial grounds. (Status Indian women received the federal vote only in 1960.)

The leading suffragists were Nellie McClung (Manitoba), Emily Murphy (Alberta), Henrietta Muir Edwards, Louise McKinney, and Irene Parlby. The "Famous Five" were not a movement; they were the petitioners in the Persons Case. Their wider movement was deeply intertwined with the maternal-feminist and temperance currents — they argued that women's "natural moral authority" entitled them to vote — and with the eugenics movement, particularly in Alberta. McClung in particular championed the 1928 Alberta Sexual Sterilisation Act, under which over 2,800 people (disproportionately Indigenous, immigrant, and intellectually disabled) were forcibly sterilised before the law's 1972 repeal. A historically-honest Grade 10 treatment names both the achievement of the Persons Case and the eugenic and racial exclusions woven into early Canadian feminism.

The Persons Case (Edwards v. AG Canada, 1929) tested whether women were "qualified persons" eligible for appointment to the Senate under section 24 of the British North America Act. In 1928 the Supreme Court of Canada unanimously said no: in 1867 "persons" had meant men, and they construed it as men. The Famous Five appealed to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London — then Canada's highest court of appeal. Lord Sankey's landmark judgment on 18 October 1929 used the now-famous "living tree" doctrine: the BNA Act "planted in Canada a living tree capable of growth and expansion within its natural limits." Women were therefore persons; Cairine Wilson became Canada's first female senator in February 1930.

The "living tree" doctrine matters far beyond the Persons Case. It established the principle that Canadian constitutional language should be interpreted in light of evolving social understandings rather than fixed to the meanings of 1867. This doctrine would later be invoked to extend rights under the Charter (1982) to LGBTQ Canadians, to Indigenous rights, and to disabled Canadians. The Persons Case is therefore both a women's-rights milestone and a foundational constitutional-interpretation decision.

  • Famous Five — Nellie McClung, Emily Murphy, Henrietta Muir Edwards, Louise McKinney, Irene Parlby.
  • Persons Case 1929 — JCPC ruling that women were "qualified persons" under the BNA Act.
  • "Living tree" doctrine — Sankey's principle that constitutional language grows with society.
  • Maternal feminism — argument for women's rights based on women's special moral authority as mothers.
  • JCPC — Judicial Committee of the Privy Council; Canada's highest court of appeal until 1949.

Ethical Judgment — Honouring the Famous Five

Apply Seixas's "Ethical Dimensions" criterion. The Famous Five appear on the back of the 2004 Canadian fifty-dollar bill and as statues on Parliament Hill (since 2000). A fair historical judgment requires both moves: (1) Recognise the achievement — the Persons Case secured Senate access for women, established the living-tree doctrine, and accelerated subsequent women's-rights advances. (2) Recognise the exclusions — their feminism was racialised: it excluded Indigenous and Asian-Canadian women, and McClung's sterilisation advocacy targeted disabled and Indigenous women. A balanced response affirms commemoration while noting that the same women whose statues we raise also championed harms. This is what Seixas means by "fair judgment" — historical actors are neither heroes nor villains, but complex figures whose legacies require continuous re-evaluation.

  • Saying "women got the vote in 1918" — only white women got the federal vote; Indigenous women waited until 1960.
  • Treating the Famous Five as uniformly progressive — they championed sterilisation laws.
  • Confusing the provincial franchise (1916) with the federal franchise (1918) with the Persons Case (1929).
  • Forgetting the JCPC's role — Canada's highest appeals court until 1949.
  • Ignoring the "living tree" doctrine's long downstream effect on Charter interpretation.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "explain how various individuals, groups, and events contributed to the development of identity, citizenship, and heritage in Canada between 1914 and 1929."

✊ 1.6 The Winnipeg General Strike (1919)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Winnipeg General Strike (15 May – 25 June 1919) was the largest general strike in Canadian history. At its peak, 30,000 workers across nearly every sector — including police, firefighters, telephone operators, and bakery workers — walked off the job. The strike was triggered by failed contract negotiations in Winnipeg's metal and building trades but emerged from much deeper conditions: postwar inflation (roughly 75% over four years), wage compression, returning soldiers competing with established workers, the Russian Revolution's recent example (1917), and the founding of the One Big Union (OBU) at the Western Labour Conference in Calgary in March 1919, which advocated industrial unionism on syndicalist lines.

The strike was met by federal intervention. Senator Gideon Robertson and Justice Minister Arthur Meighen travelled to Winnipeg in late May. On 6 June the federal Cabinet amended the Immigration Act to allow the deportation of British-born immigrants without trial — a remarkable suspension of due process aimed directly at the British-born strike leaders. On 21 June 1919 — "Bloody Saturday" — the Royal North-West Mounted Police charged a peaceful crowd on Main Street; two strikers, Mike Sokolowski and Steve Schezerbanowicz, were killed and many injured. The strike leaders were arrested; J. S. Woodsworth (later founder of the CCF) was charged with seditious libel for quoting Isaiah 65 ("they shall build houses, and inhabit them"). The strike collapsed on 25 June. Most strikers returned to work without their gains.

The political consequences were profound. The 1920 provincial election sent strike leaders to the Manitoba legislature. Woodsworth went to the House of Commons in 1921 and helped found the Cooperative Commonwealth Federation (CCF) in 1932 — the direct ancestor of today's NDP. The 1919 events were reframed in three competing ways: (1) by the federal government and most English-Canadian newspapers as a "Bolshevik plot" requiring suppression; (2) by labour historians from the 1970s onward as the high-water mark of pre-Cold-War Canadian industrial militancy; (3) by Indigenous and immigrant historians as a moment whose racial and gendered exclusions matter as much as its class politics. Telephone operators ("Hello Girls"), heavily women and the strike's first walkout, are often missing from popular memory.

The strike is also the first major Canadian case of the federal government weaponising immigration status against political action. The strikers of British origin were threatened with deportation despite years of legal residence; "foreign-born" workers, especially Ukrainians and Russians, were singled out by newspapers regardless of their actual political views. This pattern — using immigration enforcement against domestic dissent — recurs in 1939–45 (Italian-Canadian internment, Japanese-Canadian internment) and in 1970 (FLQ-era arrests under the War Measures Act). Recognising the pattern across a century is exactly what the "Continuity and Change" historical thinking concept asks of students.

  • General strike — coordinated work stoppage by all unions in a city or region.
  • One Big Union (OBU) — Western Canadian syndicalist labour federation founded 1919.
  • J. S. Woodsworth — Methodist minister, strike leader, later first leader of the CCF (1932).
  • Bloody Saturday — RNWMP charge on Main Street, 21 June 1919; 2 killed.
  • Section 98 of the Criminal Code — sedition provision used against strike leaders; repealed 1936.

Continuity and Change — A Century of State Response to Strikes

Compare Winnipeg 1919 with later Canadian state responses to mass labour action. (1) In 1945, the Ford Strike at Windsor produced the "Rand Formula" (P.C. Justice Ivan Rand's arbitration award), which institutionalised the union dues check-off and formalised mid-century industrial peace. State response: institutional negotiation. (2) In 1957 the Nova Scotia coal-mining strikes saw federal-provincial responses focused on industrial restructuring rather than suppression. (3) In 1995–96 the Ontario "Days of Action" against the Harris government were met with restraint. (4) In 2022 the "Freedom Convoy" was met with the first peacetime invocation of the (renamed) Emergencies Act. The continuity is the existence of a state response; the change is the move from physical suppression and deportation (1919) to legal-economic frameworks (1945) and back to emergency powers (2022). The change in which protests trigger which responses is itself a question of political economy and racial coding worth interrogating. This is what continuity-and-change analysis looks like at Level 4: not "things changed" but a structured pattern across decades.

  • Believing the strike was "Bolshevik" — most leaders were British-born Methodists and Labour-party social gospel reformers.
  • Forgetting the role of women — telephone operators walked out first.
  • Treating "Bloody Saturday" as the start; the strike had been running for five weeks.
  • Missing the legal innovation — deportation of British-born residents without trial via Cabinet order.
  • Failing to trace the strike's institutional legacy: Woodsworth → CCF 1932 → NDP 1961.
