CHV2O — Civics and Citizenship, Grade 10 Open

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 and 10: Canadian and World Studies (2018, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CHV2O  |  Credit Value: 0.5  |  Hours: 55  |  Type: Open  |  Prerequisite: None

1. Course Description

This course explores rights and responsibilities associated with being an active citizen in a democratic society. Students will explore issues of civic importance such as healthy schools, community planning, environmental responsibility, and the influence of social media, while developing their understanding of the role of civic engagement and of political processes in the local, national, and/or global community. Students will apply the concepts of political thinking and the political inquiry process to investigate, and express informed opinions about, a range of political issues and developments that are both of significance in today's world and of personal interest to them. CHV2O is paired with CHV2N Civics (Open, Non-Academic) and is normally taught alongside the half-credit CGC1D Issues in Canadian Geography or a half-credit Career Studies (GLC2O) to fulfil the Grade 10 compulsory pairing.

3. Overall Goals of the Course

By the end of the course, students will:

  • understand the responsibilities, rights, and roles of citizens in democratic societies;
  • investigate civic issues, events, and developments using the political inquiry process;
  • apply the concepts of political thinking (political significance; objectives and results; stability and change; political perspective);
  • analyse some beliefs, values, and viewpoints that influence civic action;
  • plan and engage in a civic-action initiative in response to a civic issue of importance to them.

4. Concepts of Disciplinary Thinking (Civics)

ConceptQuestion it asks
Political SignificanceWhy does this civic event, person, or development matter? Significant for whom, and why now?
Objectives & ResultsWhat did the people involved set out to achieve? What were the intended and unintended consequences?
Stability & ChangeWhat has remained constant in our political system and what has been transformed? Why?
Political PerspectiveWhose voices, beliefs, and values shape this issue? How do worldview and lived experience inform a citizen's stance?

5. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Civic Awareness (B): Healthy democracies depend on citizens who understand the structures, principles, and people that shape their political community.
  • Civic Engagement & Action (C): Active citizens contribute to the common good through informed, ethical, and inclusive participation in democratic life.
  • Political Inquiry & Skill Development (A): Investigating civic issues using a deliberate inquiry process develops the dispositions and tools necessary for lifelong civic engagement.

6. Strand A — Political Inquiry and Skill Development (embedded across all units)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Political Inquiry: use the political inquiry process and the concepts of political thinking when investigating issues, events, and developments of civic importance;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills, including spatial-technologies skills, developed through investigations related to civics and citizenship education, and identify some careers in which civics and citizenship education might be an asset.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments of civic importance;
  • A1.2 select and organize relevant evidence, data, and information on issues of civic importance from a variety of primary and secondary sources;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations;
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence, data, and information relevant to their investigations, using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for political inquiry;
  • A1.5 use the concepts of political thinking (i.e., political significance, objectives and results, stability and change, and political perspective) when analysing and evaluating evidence, data, and information and formulating conclusions and/or judgements about issues of civic importance;
  • A1.6 evaluate evidence and draw conclusions about issues, events, and/or developments of civic importance;
  • A1.7 communicate judgements, decisions, conclusions, predictions, and/or plans of action clearly and logically, using appropriate forms (e.g., editorial, essay, formal speech, infographic, podcast);
  • A1.8 use accepted forms of documentation to reference different types of sources (e.g., articles, books, films, websites);
  • A1.9 use appropriate terminology when communicating the results of their investigations (e.g., bill, bylaw, civic action, common good, democracy, federalism, riding).
Cross-curricular focus: Source-checking habits transfer to media-literacy and democratic-citizenship tasks across History, English, and Geography. Inquiry products in CHV2O may take the form of a formal speech, a deputation, an op-ed, a public-service media campaign, or a multimedia presentation.

7. Strand B — Civic Awareness

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Civic Issues, Democratic Values: describe beliefs and values underlying democratic citizenship in Canada and explain how they are related to civic action and to one's position on civic issues;
  • B2. Governance in Canada: explain, with reference to a range of issues of civic importance, the roles and responsibilities of various institutions, structures, and figures in Canadian governance;
  • B3. Rights and Responsibilities: analyse key rights and responsibilities associated with citizenship, in both the Canadian and global context, and some ways in which these rights are protected.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 describe various civic issues of local, national, and/or global significance;
  • B1.2 describe some civic engagement and various democratic values, and explain how they are related to civic action;
  • B1.3 describe some ways in which beliefs and values can influence people's positions on civic issues;
  • B1.4 describe some major personal and societal benefits of being an engaged citizen;
  • B2.1 describe the structures and functions of the federal, provincial/territorial, and municipal/local levels of government in Canada (e.g., the federal Parliament, the Ontario Legislative Assembly, regional/municipal councils);
  • B2.2 explain the roles of various institutions, organizations, and figures within different levels of government in Canada (e.g., the Governor General, the Lieutenant Governor, the Prime Minister, the Premier, members of Parliament, members of Provincial Parliament, mayors, councillors);
  • B2.3 describe the main features of the Canadian electoral system (e.g., first-past-the-post, parties, ridings) and explain how it operates at the federal, provincial, and municipal levels;
  • B2.4 explain why political parties exist and how they shape Canadian political life;
  • B3.1 describe key rights and freedoms protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (e.g., fundamental freedoms, democratic rights, mobility rights, legal rights, equality rights, language rights, Indigenous rights);
  • B3.2 analyse responsibilities associated with some specific rights of Canadian citizens;
  • B3.3 describe rights and responsibilities associated with citizenship in the global context.
Anchor texts and documents: Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982); Constitution Act, 1867 and 1982; Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948); United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007); Truth and Reconciliation Commission Calls to Action (2015).

8. Strand C — Civic Engagement and Action

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Civic Contributions: analyse a variety of civic contributions, and ways in which people can contribute to the common good;
  • C2. Inclusion and Civic Action: assess ways in which people express their perspectives on issues of civic importance and how various perspectives, beliefs, and values are recognized and represented in communities in Canada;
  • C3. Personal Action on Civic Issues: analyse a civic issue of personal interest and develop a plan of action to address it.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 analyse contributions that people in various roles have made to the common good and to the resolution of significant civic issues (e.g., the Famous Five, Tommy Douglas, Viola Desmond, Tom Longboat, Cindy Blackstock, Murray Sinclair);
  • C1.2 describe a variety of ways in which people can take action to address local, national, and/or international issues of civic importance (e.g., voting, volunteering, petitioning, lobbying, protesting, joining an organization, social-media campaigning, running for office);
  • C1.3 explain why it is important for people to engage in civic action, and the personal and societal benefits of doing so;
  • C2.1 analyse responses, at both an individual and a systemic level, to discriminatory and inclusive actions in Canada;
  • C2.2 analyse, in terms of various perspectives, beliefs, and values, an issue of civic importance in Canada;
  • C2.3 describe ways in which various beliefs and values can be expressed by people involved in civic action;
  • C3.1 identify a civic issue of personal interest and formulate questions to guide an investigation into the issue;
  • C3.2 gather, interpret, and analyse information from a variety of sources to investigate the civic issue, using the concepts of political thinking;
  • C3.3 develop a plan of action to address the civic issue identified, taking into consideration various perspectives on the issue, and explain why their plan might be effective.
Civic Action Project: Each student plans (and where possible, carries out) a small civic-action initiative aligned to a real local or national issue — examples include a school recycling audit, a deputation to municipal council, an Indigenous land-acknowledgment policy proposal, a voter-registration drive for newly 18-year-olds, or a media campaign on climate, mental health, food security, or anti-racism.

11. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprising unit tests, civic-action project, source-evaluation tasks, deputation/speech, op-ed, in-class writing. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprising a Final Civic Inquiry / Civic Action Showcase (15%) and a Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

12. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes on Charter content, source-credibility checklists, civic-action self-rubrics).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., diagnostic on prior civics knowledge from elementary social studies, civic-issue brainstorm, inquiry-question draft).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, civic-action plan, final exam, formal speech or deputation).

13. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (Hansard transcripts, audio clips from CBC's The Current, captioned video, infographics from Elections Canada), multiple means of engagement (student-selected civic issue for the action project, choice of issue for the position paper), and multiple means of expression (essay, podcast, video deputation, op-ed, infographic) to meet the needs of diverse learners and to honour the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools. Where appropriate, the course incorporates Indigenous knowledge, perspectives, and ways of knowing — including treaty rights, the Royal Proclamation of 1763, the Indian Act, the residential school system, and contemporary Indigenous-Crown relations.

14. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CHV2O for sustaining the multi-week Civic Action Project and for collaborative work in Socratic seminars and town-hall simulations.
