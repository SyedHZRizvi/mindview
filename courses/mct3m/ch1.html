Chapter 1: Exponential Functions

Exponent laws, rational exponents, graphing \(y=ab^x\), solving exponential equations, growth and decay applications

📚 Strand A: Exponential Functions ⏱️ ~22h 📹 6 topic blocks
1.1 — Laws of Exponents with Integer and Rational Exponents
Apply the laws of exponents to simplify algebraic expressions; interpret negative and zero exponents; convert between radical and rational exponent form.
The exponent laws — product \( a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \), quotient \( a^m / a^n = a^{m-n} \), power \( (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \), zero \( a^0 = 1 \), negative \( a^{-n} = 1/a^n \), and rational \( a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} \) — let you simplify any expression with the same base. Example: \( 27^{2/3} = (\sqrt[3]{27})^2 = 3^2 = 9 \).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An exponent is a shorthand for repeated multiplication: \(a^n\) means \(a\) multiplied by itself \(n\) times, for any positive whole number \(n\). The base \(a\) is what's being multiplied; \(n\) is the exponent. The two-line rule that anchors everything else is the product law: when you multiply two powers of the same base, you add the exponents. \(a^3 \cdot a^4 = (aaa)(aaaa) = a^7 = a^{3+4}\). From this single observation, the rest of the exponent laws fall out by simple arithmetic.

The quotient law is the mirror: subtraction matches division. \(a^7 / a^3 = a^{7-3} = a^4\). The power-of-a-power law appears when you raise a power to another power: \( (a^3)^4 = a^3 \cdot a^3 \cdot a^3 \cdot a^3 = a^{12} \), so you multiply the exponents. The power-of-a-product rule says \( (ab)^n = a^n b^n \), and the power-of-a-quotient rule says \( (a/b)^n = a^n / b^n \) (with \(b \ne 0\)).

Two surprising rules extend exponents beyond positive integers. The zero exponent: from the quotient law, \(a^n/a^n = a^{n-n} = a^0\); but a number divided by itself is 1, so \(a^0 = 1\) (for \(a \ne 0\)). The negative exponent: \(a^{-n}\) must equal \(1/a^n\), so that \(a^n \cdot a^{-n} = a^0 = 1\) holds.

Finally, rational exponents connect powers to radicals. Define \(a^{1/n}\) as the \(n\)-th root of \(a\), \(\sqrt[n]{a}\). Then \(a^{m/n} = (a^{1/n})^m = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m\), or equivalently \(\sqrt[n]{a^m}\). Why does this make sense? Because raising \(a^{1/n}\) to the \(n\)-th power gives \(a^{n/n} = a^1 = a\) — which is exactly the defining property of the \(n\)-th root.

  • Base — the number being multiplied repeatedly (in \(2^5\), the base is 2).
  • Exponent — the number that counts the multiplications.
  • Power — the whole expression \(a^n\), or the value it equals.
  • Product law — \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\).
  • Quotient law — \(a^m / a^n = a^{m-n}\).
  • Zero exponent — \(a^0 = 1\) for \(a \ne 0\).
  • Negative exponent — \(a^{-n} = 1/a^n\).
  • Rational exponent — \(a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m}\).

Example 1 — Simplify \( \dfrac{(2x^3 y^2)^4 \cdot (3 x^{-2} y)^2}{6 x^5 y^7} \).

Apply power-of-a-product to each numerator term: \((2x^3 y^2)^4 = 16 x^{12} y^8\) and \((3 x^{-2} y)^2 = 9 x^{-4} y^2\).

Multiply numerator: \(16 \cdot 9 \cdot x^{12 - 4} \cdot y^{8+2} = 144 x^8 y^{10}\).

Divide by denominator: \(\dfrac{144 x^8 y^{10}}{6 x^5 y^7} = 24 x^{8-5} y^{10-7} = 24 x^3 y^3\).

Example 2 — Evaluate \(32^{3/5}\) without a calculator.

Rewrite as a root: \(32^{3/5} = (\sqrt[5]{32})^3\). Since \(2^5 = 32\), \(\sqrt[5]{32} = 2\).

So \(32^{3/5} = 2^3 = 8\).

Example 3 — Simplify \( \dfrac{x^{1/2} \cdot x^{-3/4}}{x^{1/4}} \).

Numerator: \(x^{1/2 + (-3/4)} = x^{2/4 - 3/4} = x^{-1/4}\).

Divide by \(x^{1/4}\): \(x^{-1/4 - 1/4} = x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\).

Watch out for:

  • \(-2^4\) versus \((-2)^4\). The first means \(-(2^4) = -16\); the second is \((-2)(-2)(-2)(-2) = 16\). Parentheses change the answer.
  • \(a^0 = 1\) only when \(a \ne 0\). The expression \(0^0\) is left undefined.
  • Forgetting that a negative exponent does not make the base negative — it makes the power a reciprocal. \(3^{-2} = \tfrac{1}{9}\), not \(-9\).
  • Adding exponents when multiplying different bases. \(2^3 \cdot 3^4 \ne 6^7\). The product law requires the same base.

1.2 — Rational Exponents and Radicals
Convert between rational exponent and radical form; evaluate rational exponent expressions; simplify radical expressions using rational exponents.
The bridge between exponents and radicals is \( a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} \). Read it both ways: \( 8^{2/3} \) is the cube root of \(8\) squared, which is \( 2^2 = 4 \); equivalently, the cube root of \(64\) is also \(4\). Rational exponents let you simplify radical expressions algebraically — turning awkward roots into clean multiplications of exponents.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A rational exponent is an exponent that's a fraction. The key idea is that \(a^{1/n}\) is the \(n\)-th root of \(a\). Why? Because the rules of exponents say \( (a^{1/n})^n = a^{n/n} = a^1 = a \); the only number that, when raised to the \(n\)-th power, returns \(a\), is the \(n\)-th root of \(a\). So we define \(a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}\). This single definition unlocks all of radical algebra.

Extending: \(a^{m/n}\) means "the \(n\)-th root of \(a\), then raised to the \(m\)-th power" — or, equivalently, "\(a\) raised to the \(m\)-th power, then the \(n\)-th root." Symbolically:

\[ a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m \]

Both orderings give the same answer (when both make sense). Often one ordering is computationally easier. To compute \(64^{5/3}\), take the cube root first (\(\sqrt[3]{64} = 4\)), then raise to the 5th power (\(4^5 = 1024\)) — small intermediate numbers. Going the other way (\(64^5\) first, then cube root) makes you work with huge numbers.

Technology contexts use rational exponents constantly. The half-life formula \(N(t) = N_0 (1/2)^{t/h}\) has a rational exponent whenever \(t\) is not a whole multiple of the half-life \(h\). The metric ratio for sound intensity per dB step uses \(10^{0.1}\), itself a rational exponent. Decibels, frequencies in music tuning, and pH all hinge on the same algebra.

  • Rational exponent — an exponent of the form \(m/n\) (where \(n \ne 0\)).
  • Principal root — for even \(n\), \(\sqrt[n]{a}\) returns the non-negative root (e.g. \(\sqrt{9} = 3\), not \(-3\)).
  • Radicand — the number under the radical symbol.
  • Index — the small number above the radical sign (the \(n\) in \(\sqrt[n]{\ }\)); when omitted, it's understood to be 2.

Example 1 — Rewrite \(\sqrt[3]{x^4}\) using a rational exponent and simplify.

\(\sqrt[3]{x^4} = x^{4/3}\).

If you wish, separate as \(x^{3/3 + 1/3} = x \cdot x^{1/3} = x\sqrt[3]{x}\). Both forms are equivalent.

Example 2 — Evaluate \( \left(\dfrac{16}{81}\right)^{3/4} \).

Apply rational exponent: take the 4th root, then cube.

\(\sqrt[4]{16/81} = \dfrac{\sqrt[4]{16}}{\sqrt[4]{81}} = \dfrac{2}{3}\).

Cube: \(\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \dfrac{8}{27}\).

Example 3 — Express \( (\sqrt{x})^5 \cdot \sqrt[3]{x^4} \) as a single power of \(x\).

\((\sqrt{x})^5 = x^{5/2}\); \(\sqrt[3]{x^4} = x^{4/3}\).

Multiply: \(x^{5/2} \cdot x^{4/3} = x^{5/2 + 4/3} = x^{15/6 + 8/6} = x^{23/6}\).

Watch out for:

  • \(\sqrt[n]{a + b} \ne \sqrt[n]{a} + \sqrt[n]{b}\) — roots do not distribute over addition.
  • Even roots of negatives are not real (e.g. \(\sqrt{-4}\) is not a real number); odd roots are fine (\(\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2\)).
  • \((\sqrt{a})^2 = a\) only when \(a \ge 0\). Watch out when \(a\) is an expression that could be negative.
  • Confusing \(a^{1/2}\) (means \(\sqrt{a}\)) with \(a^{-1/2}\) (means \(1/\sqrt{a}\)).

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.1 — "simplify algebraic expressions containing integer and rational exponents using the laws of exponents." Rational exponents are explicitly named in the MCT3M expectation. Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised).

1.3 — Graphing Exponential Functions \(y=ab^x\)
Sketch graphs of \( y = b^x \) and \( y = ab^x \); identify domain, range, intercepts, asymptote, and growth or decay behaviour.
For \( y = b^x \) with \( b > 0, b \ne 1 \): domain is all reals; range is \( (0, \infty) \); horizontal asymptote \( y = 0 \); y-intercept \(1\); no x-intercept. If \( b > 1 \) the function grows, if \( 0 < b < 1 \) it decays. The constant \(a\) in \(y = ab^x\) sets the y-intercept (since \(y(0) = a\)) and vertically stretches the graph.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An exponential function has the form \(f(x) = ab^x\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants, \(b > 0\), and \(b \ne 1\). The variable is in the exponent — that's what makes the function "exponential" (as opposed to polynomial, where the variable is in the base). This single placement difference produces dramatically different behaviour: exponential functions can outgrow any polynomial, or shrink toward zero faster than any polynomial.

Start with the simplest case: \(y = b^x\). Plot a few values. For \(b = 2\): \(y(0) = 1\), \(y(1) = 2\), \(y(2) = 4\), \(y(3) = 8\); but also \(y(-1) = 1/2\), \(y(-2) = 1/4\). The values double every unit step right and halve every unit step left. The graph rises steeply to the right and approaches but never touches the \(x\)-axis on the left — the \(x\)-axis is a horizontal asymptote.

The base \(b\) sets the shape. When \(b > 1\), the function is exponential growth: it rises to the right. When \(0 < b < 1\) (e.g. \(b = 1/2\)), the function is exponential decay: it falls to the right (and rises to the left). The case \(b = 1\) is degenerate — it gives \(y = 1\), a constant; and \(b \le 0\) is excluded because \(b^x\) wouldn't be defined for all real \(x\) (think \(b = -2\), \(x = 1/2\): \(\sqrt{-2}\) isn't real).

The coefficient \(a\) in \(y = ab^x\) sets the \(y\)-intercept: \(y(0) = a \cdot b^0 = a\). So \(a\) is the initial value (the value at \(x = 0\)). It also vertically stretches or compresses the graph. Negative \(a\) reflects the graph in the \(x\)-axis (giving negative values throughout). The horizontal asymptote is still \(y = 0\) (because \(b^x \to 0\) as \(x \to -\infty\) for growth, or \(x \to +\infty\) for decay).

  • Exponential function — \(f(x) = ab^x\) with \(a \ne 0\), \(b > 0\), \(b \ne 1\).
  • Base — the constant \(b\); determines growth (\(b>1\)) or decay (\(0
  • Initial value — the value \(a = f(0)\); always the \(y\)-intercept.
  • Horizontal asymptote — \(y = 0\) for the basic form \(y = ab^x\); the graph approaches but never crosses.
  • Growth factor — for \(b > 1\), each unit increase in \(x\) multiplies \(y\) by \(b\).
  • Decay factor — for \(0 < b < 1\), each unit increase in \(x\) multiplies \(y\) by \(b < 1\), reducing it.

Example 1 — Sketch \(f(x) = 2 \cdot 3^x\) and state its key features.

Initial value \(a = 2\). Base \(b = 3 > 1\), so growth.

Key points: \(f(-2) = 2/9 \approx 0.22\); \(f(-1) = 2/3 \approx 0.67\); \(f(0) = 2\); \(f(1) = 6\); \(f(2) = 18\).

Domain: \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). Range: \(y > 0\). \(y\)-intercept: \((0, 2)\). Horizontal asymptote: \(y = 0\) (as \(x \to -\infty\)). No \(x\)-intercept.

Example 2 — Sketch \(g(x) = 100(0.5)^x\); model a decay scenario.

Initial value \(a = 100\). Base \(b = 0.5 < 1\), so decay (halving every unit).

\(g(0) = 100\); \(g(1) = 50\); \(g(2) = 25\); \(g(3) = 12.5\); \(g(-1) = 200\).

Domain: \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). Range: \(y > 0\). \(y\)-intercept: \((0, 100)\). Asymptote: \(y = 0\) as \(x \to \infty\).

In technology context: 100 g of a radioactive isotope with a 1-day half-life. After 3 days, 12.5 g remain.

Example 3 — Compare \(y = 2^x\) and \(y = (1/2)^x\) algebraically.

\((1/2)^x = 2^{-x}\). So \(y = (1/2)^x\) is the reflection of \(y = 2^x\) in the \(y\)-axis. Where one grows, the other decays — same shape, mirror image.

Watch out for:

  • Confusing \(y = b^x\) (exponential) with \(y = x^b\) (power). The first has the variable in the exponent; the second has the variable in the base.
  • Saying \(2^{-x}\) is "negative." It is positive: \(2^{-x} = 1/2^x > 0\) for every real \(x\).
  • Drawing the asymptote as if the graph crosses it. The graph approaches \(y = 0\) but never reaches it.
  • Reading \(y = ab^x\) as \(y = (ab)^x\). The exponent applies only to \(b\); \(a\) is just a multiplier.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "determine, through investigation with technology, and describe the impact of changing the base and changing the sign of the exponent on the graph of an exponential function." Also A1.4 — pose and solve problems based on real-world applications modelled by exponential functions. Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

1.4 — Solving Exponential Equations Using Common Bases
Solve exponential equations by expressing both sides as powers of a common base; apply exponent laws to isolate the variable.
Strategy: rewrite both sides of \( b^{f(x)} = b^{g(x)} \) so they have the same base, then set the exponents equal: \( f(x) = g(x) \). Example: \( 4^{x+1} = 32 \) becomes \( (2^2)^{x+1} = 2^5 \), so \( 2^{2x+2} = 2^5 \), giving \( 2x+2 = 5 \), \( x = 3/2 \).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An exponential equation is an equation in which the unknown appears in an exponent. The cleanest technique to solve one — when it works — is the common-base method. The principle is simple: if \(b^A = b^B\) and \(b > 0, b \ne 1\), then \(A = B\). The exponential function is one-to-one (each input has a unique output), so two outputs that are equal must come from equal inputs.

To use the method, rewrite both sides of the equation as powers of the same base. Common useful bases: 2, 3, 5, and 10 (and their reciprocals). For example, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 are all powers of 2; 9, 27, 81 are powers of 3; 25, 125, 625 are powers of 5. Once both sides share a base, equate the exponents and solve the resulting linear (or sometimes quadratic) equation.

When does the method fail? When the two sides can't be written with the same base — for example, \(2^x = 7\). The bases 2 and 7 share no common power; in such cases you need logarithms (Topic 1.5 below). But many exam-style and applied questions are designed so the common-base method applies.

A subtle case: fractional or negative bases. \(\tfrac{1}{4} = 2^{-2}\), \(\sqrt{2} = 2^{1/2}\), \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{8}} = 2^{-3/2}\). Practise rewriting these in pure exponent form. Also, watch for terms like \(8^x \cdot 4^{x-1}\): apply exponent laws to gather into a single power before equating.

  • Common-base method — rewrite both sides of an exponential equation as powers of the same base, then equate exponents.
  • One-to-one property — for \(b > 0, b \ne 1\): \(b^A = b^B \implies A = B\). This is what justifies equating exponents.
  • Common useful bases — 2, 3, 5, 10 generate most powers seen in exam questions.

Example 1 — Solve \(4^{x+1} = 32\).

Rewrite both as powers of 2: \(4 = 2^2\), \(32 = 2^5\). Equation becomes \((2^2)^{x+1} = 2^5\), i.e. \(2^{2(x+1)} = 2^5\).

Equate exponents: \(2(x+1) = 5\). Solve: \(2x + 2 = 5\), \(2x = 3\), \(x = \tfrac{3}{2}\).

Check: \(4^{3/2 + 1} = 4^{5/2} = (4^{1/2})^5 = 2^5 = 32\). ✓

Example 2 — Solve \(9^{2x-1} = \dfrac{1}{27}\).

Rewrite as powers of 3: \(9 = 3^2\), \(\tfrac{1}{27} = 3^{-3}\). Equation becomes \((3^2)^{2x-1} = 3^{-3}\), i.e. \(3^{4x-2} = 3^{-3}\).

Equate exponents: \(4x - 2 = -3\). Solve: \(4x = -1\), \(x = -\tfrac{1}{4}\).

Example 3 — Solve \(2^{x+1} \cdot 4^{x-2} = 64\).

Rewrite all in base 2: \(2^{x+1} \cdot (2^2)^{x-2} = 2^6\).

Simplify: \(2^{x+1} \cdot 2^{2x-4} = 2^6 \Rightarrow 2^{3x-3} = 2^6\).

Equate exponents: \(3x - 3 = 6 \Rightarrow 3x = 9 \Rightarrow x = 3\).

Check: \(2^4 \cdot 4^1 = 16 \cdot 4 = 64\). ✓

Watch out for:

  • Equating exponents when the bases don't match. \(2^x = 4^x\) is not "\(x = x\)" — it's \(2^x = (2^2)^x = 2^{2x}\), giving \(x = 2x\) so \(x = 0\).
  • Forgetting to apply the distributive rule when expanding the inside of an exponent: \((2^2)^{x+1} = 2^{2(x+1)} = 2^{2x+2}\), not \(2^{2x+1}\).
  • Treating \(2^x + 2^y\) as \(2^{x+y}\). Sums of powers do not simplify — only products with the same base do.
  • Negative answers in growth/decay contexts. The algebraic solution may be valid, but a negative time often makes no physical sense in a word problem.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "solve exponential equations in one variable by determining a common base [e.g., solve \(4^{x+1} = 32\) by expressing each side as a power of 2]." Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

1.5 — Solving Exponential Equations Using Logarithms
Apply the common (base-10) logarithm to solve exponential equations when a common base is impractical; round answers to appropriate accuracy.
When the common-base method fails (e.g. \( 5 \cdot 1.08^x = 25 \)), take \( \log \) of both sides and use the power rule \( \log(b^x) = x \log b \). For \( 1.08^x = 5 \): \( x \log 1.08 = \log 5 \), giving \( x = \log 5 / \log 1.08 \approx 20.9 \).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The common-base method only works when both sides of an exponential equation can be written with the same base. In real-world problems — interest at 1.08, half-life at 0.5 but a base time of 1 year, dB calculations — the numbers rarely cooperate. We need a more general method, and that method is logarithms.

A logarithm is the inverse of an exponent. The statement \(\log_b x = y\) means "the power to which you must raise \(b\) to get \(x\) is \(y\)" — that is, \(b^y = x\). In MCT3M we use mainly the common (base-10) logarithm, written simply \(\log\), available on every scientific calculator.

The crucial property for solving exponential equations is the power rule of logarithms:

\[ \log(b^x) = x \log b \]

This says the logarithm of a power is the exponent times the logarithm of the base. The exponent "comes down" out of the function. To solve any exponential equation \(b^x = c\): take \(\log\) of both sides, apply the power rule to get \(x \log b = \log c\), then divide to isolate \(x\):

\[ x = \frac{\log c}{\log b} \]

You can then evaluate the numerator and denominator separately on a calculator and divide. The change-of-base formula \(\log_b c = \tfrac{\log c}{\log b}\) is exactly this same calculation.

  • Logarithm — \(\log_b x\) is the exponent \(y\) such that \(b^y = x\). In MCT3M, \(\log x\) means \(\log_{10} x\).
  • Power rule — \(\log(b^x) = x \log b\).
  • Inverse property — \(\log(10^x) = x\) and \(10^{\log x} = x\) (for \(x > 0\)).
  • Change of base — \(\log_b c = \dfrac{\log c}{\log b}\); the calculator's \(\log\) button gives base 10.

Example 1 — Solve \(1.08^x = 2\). (Time to double an investment at 8% annual compound interest.)

Take \(\log\) of both sides: \(\log(1.08^x) = \log 2\).

Power rule: \(x \log 1.08 = \log 2\).

Divide: \(x = \dfrac{\log 2}{\log 1.08} = \dfrac{0.30103}{0.033424} \approx 9.0065\).

So it takes about 9 years to double — consistent with the rule-of-72 estimate (\(72/8 = 9\)).

Example 2 — A capacitor discharges per \(V(t) = 12 \cdot 0.9^t\) (volts, seconds). When will the voltage first drop below 4 V?

Solve \(12 \cdot 0.9^t = 4\): \(0.9^t = \tfrac{1}{3}\).

Take \(\log\): \(t \log 0.9 = \log(1/3)\).

\(\log 0.9 \approx -0.04576\); \(\log(1/3) \approx -0.4771\).

\(t = \dfrac{-0.4771}{-0.04576} \approx 10.43\) s.

So after about 10.4 s, the voltage first reaches 4 V; after that it stays below.

Example 3 — Solve \(3^{2x+1} = 7\).

Take \(\log\): \((2x+1) \log 3 = \log 7\).

Divide: \(2x + 1 = \dfrac{\log 7}{\log 3} = \dfrac{0.8451}{0.4771} \approx 1.7712\).

Solve: \(2x = 0.7712\), \(x \approx 0.386\).

Check: \(3^{2(0.386)+1} = 3^{1.7712} \approx 7\). ✓

Watch out for:

  • \(\log(a+b) \ne \log a + \log b\) — logarithms do not distribute over addition.
  • \(\log(a/b) = \log a - \log b\) is correct; don't confuse with \((\log a)/(\log b)\), which is the change-of-base formula.
  • Forgetting that \(\log 0\) and \(\log\) of a negative number are undefined — only positive arguments work.
  • Rounding too early. Carry at least 4 decimal places for intermediate calculator values, then round to 1–3 sig figs at the end.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.3 — "solve simple exponential equations using logarithms in base 10 with a scientific calculator [e.g., solve \(1.05^x = 2\), giving \(x = \log 2 / \log 1.05\)]." Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

1.6 — Exponential Growth, Decay, and Half-Life Applications
Set up and solve exponential models in technology contexts: population growth, radioactive decay, compound interest, and capacitor/RC circuits.
Growth/decay form: \( A(t) = A_0 \, b^{t/T} \), where \(A_0\) is the initial amount, \(b\) is the multiplier per period \(T\). For doubling, \(b = 2\); for half-life, \(b = 1/2\). Compound interest: \(A = P(1 + i)^n\). Combining algebraic and graphical methods is essential.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Exponential models appear all over college-technology contexts. Voltage on a discharging capacitor falls exponentially. Bacteria populations double on a fixed schedule. Radioactive isotopes decay by half-life. Money in a compound-interest account grows by a fixed percentage each period. In every case, the same template applies:

\[ A(t) = A_0 \cdot b^{t/T} \]

Here \(A_0\) is the initial amount (at \(t = 0\)), \(T\) is the characteristic time (the time for one application of the growth or decay factor), and \(b\) is the growth or decay factor per period. For population that doubles every 4 hours, \(b = 2\) and \(T = 4\) h. For an isotope with half-life 30 years, \(b = 1/2\) and \(T = 30\) yr. For compound interest, the time-step form is \(A = P(1 + i)^n\), where \(i\) is the interest rate per compounding period and \(n\) is the number of periods.

The workflow is consistent: (1) identify \(A_0\), \(b\), \(T\) from the wording; (2) write the model; (3) for a specific time, substitute and compute; (4) for "when does \(A\) equal something," solve the exponential equation using common bases or logarithms (Topic 1.4 or 1.5).

A key pitfall in technology problems is unit consistency. If the half-life is given in days and the question asks about hours, convert before substituting. Similarly, in compound interest at "12% per year compounded monthly," the periodic rate is \(0.12/12 = 0.01\) per month, and \(n\) is the number of months — not years.

  • Exponential growth model — \(A(t) = A_0 b^{t/T}\) with \(b > 1\); rate increases with current value.
  • Exponential decay model — \(A(t) = A_0 b^{t/T}\) with \(0 < b < 1\); rate decreases with current value.
  • Half-life — the time \(T\) for an exponentially decaying quantity to drop to half its current value.
  • Doubling time — the time \(T\) for an exponentially growing quantity to double.
  • Compound interest — \(A = P(1 + i)^n\) where \(i\) is the periodic rate and \(n\) the number of compounding periods.

Example 1 — Bacteria population. A culture starts with 500 cells and doubles every 3 hours. Find the population after 12 hours. When will it reach 10 000?

Model: \(P(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t/3}\).

After 12 hours: \(P(12) = 500 \cdot 2^{12/3} = 500 \cdot 2^4 = 500 \cdot 16 = 8000\) cells.

Reach 10 000: \(500 \cdot 2^{t/3} = 10{,}000\), so \(2^{t/3} = 20\).

Take \(\log\): \(\tfrac{t}{3} \log 2 = \log 20\), so \(\tfrac{t}{3} = \tfrac{\log 20}{\log 2} = \tfrac{1.3010}{0.30103} \approx 4.322\), giving \(t \approx 12.97\) h.

Example 2 — Radioactive isotope. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample of 100 mg is left to decay; how much remains after 30 days?

Model: \(M(t) = 100 \cdot (0.5)^{t/8}\) (mg).

At \(t = 30\): \(M(30) = 100 \cdot 0.5^{30/8} = 100 \cdot 0.5^{3.75}\).

\(0.5^{3.75} = 1/2^{3.75} \approx 1/13.45 \approx 0.0743\).

So \(M(30) \approx 7.43\) mg remains.

Example 3 — Compound interest. \$2 500 is invested at 6% per year compounded quarterly. What is the value after 8 years?

Periodic rate: \(i = 0.06/4 = 0.015\). Periods: \(n = 4 \cdot 8 = 32\).

\(A = 2500 (1.015)^{32}\).

\((1.015)^{32}\): compute as \(10^{32 \log 1.015} = 10^{32 \cdot 0.006466} = 10^{0.20691} \approx 1.6103\).

\(A \approx 2500 \cdot 1.6103 \approx \$4{,}025.74\).

Watch out for:

  • Mixing the exponent and the time. The exponent in a half-life model is \(t/T\), not \(t \cdot T\). For a 4-hour half-life and \(t = 8\) h, the exponent is \(8/4 = 2\), so two halvings occur — not 8 × 4 = 32.
  • Forgetting the unit for the periodic rate in compound interest. "8% per year compounded monthly" means \(i = 0.08/12 \approx 0.00667\), not 0.08.
  • Confusing nominal annual rate with effective annual rate. At 8% nominal compounded monthly, the effective annual rate is \((1.00667)^{12} - 1 \approx 8.30\%\).
  • Negative answers in time. \(t \ge 0\) in physical decay models; reject negative times.
