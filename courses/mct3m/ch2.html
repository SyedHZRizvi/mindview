Factoring, the quadratic formula, vertex form, cubic graphs, and applications in technology and engineering

📚 Strand B: Polynomial Functions ⏱️ ~24h 📹 6 topic blocks
2.1 — Graphing Quadratic Functions in Three Forms
Identify standard, factored, and vertex forms of a quadratic; read key features (vertex, zeros, intercepts, axis of symmetry) directly from each form.
Three equivalent forms of \( y = ax^2 + bx + c \): standard gives the y-intercept \(c\) immediately; factored \(y = a(x - r)(x - s)\) reveals the zeros \(r, s\); vertex \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\) reveals the vertex \((h, k)\) and axis of symmetry \(x = h\). The coefficient \(a\) controls direction of opening (up if \(a>0\)) and width (narrower for larger \(|a|\)).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A quadratic function is any function whose highest power of the variable is 2: \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\) where \(a \ne 0\). Its graph is a parabola, a symmetric U-shaped curve. The same parabola can be written in three algebraic forms — standard, factored, and vertex — each useful for reading off different features at a glance.

Standard form is \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\). The constant \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept (set \(x = 0\)). The leading coefficient \(a\) tells you direction (up if \(a > 0\), down if \(a < 0\)) and how steep the parabola is (large \(|a|\) makes it narrower). The vertex isn't visible in standard form, but you can find the axis of symmetry as \(x = -b/(2a)\), then sub back in for the \(y\)-value.

Factored form is \(y = a(x - r)(x - s)\). The zeros (the \(x\)-intercepts, where \(y = 0\)) are \(r\) and \(s\) — they jump out immediately. The axis of symmetry is the average of the two zeros: \(x = (r + s)/2\). This form is useful when a problem gives you the zeros directly (e.g., where a projectile lands).

Vertex form is \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\). The vertex is \((h, k)\) — directly readable. The axis of symmetry is \(x = h\). Vertex form is best for graphing from a parent function (using transformations of \(y = x^2\)), for finding maximum or minimum values, and for solving optimisation problems.

Converting between forms is a basic technology-math skill. Expanding factored or vertex form gives standard form. Factoring (or using the quadratic formula on zeros) converts standard to factored. Completing the square converts standard to vertex form.

  • Parabola — the graph of any quadratic function; symmetric U-shape.
  • Vertex — the highest or lowest point of the parabola; turning point.
  • Axis of symmetry — vertical line through the vertex; reflection axis.
  • Zero (root) — any \(x\)-value where \(y = 0\); also the \(x\)-intercept.
  • Standard form — \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\).
  • Factored form — \(y = a(x - r)(x - s)\).
  • Vertex form — \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\).

Example 1 — For \(y = 2(x + 1)(x - 3)\), identify key features and sketch.

Factored form: zeros \(x = -1\) and \(x = 3\). \(a = 2 > 0\), so parabola opens up.

Axis of symmetry: midpoint of zeros: \(x = (-1 + 3)/2 = 1\).

Vertex: substitute \(x = 1\) into the equation: \(y = 2(2)(-2) = -8\). Vertex \((1, -8)\).

\(y\)-intercept: at \(x = 0\), \(y = 2(1)(-3) = -6\).

Example 2 — Convert \(y = 2x^2 - 8x + 5\) to vertex form by completing the square.

Factor 2 from the variable terms: \(y = 2(x^2 - 4x) + 5\).

Add and subtract \((4/2)^2 = 4\) inside: \(y = 2(x^2 - 4x + 4 - 4) + 5 = 2((x - 2)^2 - 4) + 5\).

Distribute: \(y = 2(x - 2)^2 - 8 + 5 = 2(x - 2)^2 - 3\).

Vertex: \((2, -3)\). Axis: \(x = 2\). Opens up. Minimum value \(y = -3\) at \(x = 2\).

Example 3 — A parabola has zeros \(x = -2\) and \(x = 6\), and passes through \((0, -12)\). Find its equation in factored form.

Start with \(y = a(x + 2)(x - 6)\) (from the zeros).

Substitute \((0, -12)\): \(-12 = a(2)(-6) = -12a\), so \(a = 1\).

Equation: \(y = (x + 2)(x - 6)\).

Watch out for:

  • Factored form \(y = a(x - r)(x - s)\) gives zeros \(r\) and \(s\) (with the same sign as in the parenthesis): if you read \((x + 3)\) the zero is \(x = -3\).
  • Vertex form \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\): the \(h\) inside has its sign flipped at the vertex (vertex is at \(x = h\), not \(x = -h\)).
  • The "axis of symmetry" is a vertical line, not just a number; write it as \(x = h\).
  • A parabola with no real zeros (discriminant negative) has no \(x\)-intercepts but still has a vertex and a graph.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "sketch the graph of a quadratic given in any of standard, factored, or vertex form; identify key features including the vertex, zeros, axis of symmetry, max/min, and direction of opening." Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

2.2 — Solving Quadratics by Factoring and the Quadratic Formula
Solve quadratic equations using common factoring, trinomial factoring, and the quadratic formula; interpret the discriminant.
To solve \( ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \): try factoring first (set each factor to zero), then fall back to the quadratic formula \( x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \). The discriminant \(D = b^2 - 4ac\) tells you the number of real roots: \(D > 0\) gives two, \(D = 0\) gives one (a repeated root, vertex on the x-axis), \(D < 0\) gives none (parabola lies entirely above or below the x-axis).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Solving a quadratic equation means finding the values of \(x\) that make \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) — graphically, these are the \(x\)-intercepts. There are several techniques; choose by what's easiest for the particular equation.

Factoring is the cleanest method when it works. Rewrite \(ax^2 + bx + c\) as a product of binomials, then use the zero product property: if \(A \cdot B = 0\), then \(A = 0\) or \(B = 0\). For example, \(x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2) = 0\) gives \(x = 3\) or \(x = -2\).

Common factoring techniques in MCT3M: pull out a common factor, then look for trinomial patterns: \(x^2 + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q)\) with \(p + q = b\) and \(pq = c\). For \(ax^2 + bx + c\) with \(a > 1\), use decomposition (split the middle term using factors of \(ac\) that sum to \(b\)).

Quadratic formula is universal — it solves every quadratic, factorable or not. From completing the square on the general \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\):

\[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \]

The expression under the square root, \(D = b^2 - 4ac\), is the discriminant. It tells you the nature of the roots before you even compute them: \(D > 0\) means two distinct real roots; \(D = 0\) means one repeated root (the vertex touches the \(x\)-axis); \(D < 0\) means no real roots (the parabola does not cross the \(x\)-axis).

In technology contexts, quadratic equations often arise from projectile motion (\(h = -\tfrac{1}{2}gt^2 + v_0 t + h_0\)), area optimisation, and electrical engineering. Many engineering exam problems are designed to factor cleanly, but the quadratic formula is the workhorse when factoring fails.

  • Zero product property — if \(AB = 0\), then \(A = 0\) or \(B = 0\); the basis for solving by factoring.
  • Quadratic formula — \(x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\); always works.
  • Discriminant — \(D = b^2 - 4ac\); sign predicts the number of real roots.
  • Repeated root — when \(D = 0\), the parabola touches the \(x\)-axis at exactly one point.
  • Decomposition — technique for factoring \(ax^2+bx+c\): split the middle term using factor pairs of \(ac\) that sum to \(b\).

Example 1 — Solve \(2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0\) by factoring.

\(ac = 6\); factor pairs that sum to \(-7\): \(-6\) and \(-1\).

Decompose: \(2x^2 - 6x - x + 3 = 0\).

Group: \(2x(x - 3) - 1(x - 3) = 0 \Rightarrow (2x - 1)(x - 3) = 0\).

Zero product: \(x = 1/2\) or \(x = 3\).

Example 2 — Solve \(3x^2 + 5x - 4 = 0\) using the quadratic formula.

\(a = 3, b = 5, c = -4\). Discriminant: \(D = 25 - 4(3)(-4) = 25 + 48 = 73\).

\(x = \dfrac{-5 \pm \sqrt{73}}{6}\). Numerically: \(\sqrt{73} \approx 8.544\), so \(x \approx \dfrac{-5 + 8.544}{6} \approx 0.591\) or \(x \approx \dfrac{-5 - 8.544}{6} \approx -2.257\).

Example 3 — A ball is thrown upward from a 1.5 m platform with initial velocity 20 m/s. Height (m): \(h(t) = -5t^2 + 20t + 1.5\). When does it hit the ground?

Set \(h = 0\): \(-5t^2 + 20t + 1.5 = 0\). Multiply by \(-2\): \(10t^2 - 40t - 3 = 0\).

Quadratic formula: \(t = \dfrac{40 \pm \sqrt{1600 + 120}}{20} = \dfrac{40 \pm \sqrt{1720}}{20} \approx \dfrac{40 \pm 41.47}{20}\).

Take the positive root: \(t \approx \dfrac{40 + 41.47}{20} \approx 4.07\) s. (Reject the negative root \(\approx -0.07\); time can't be negative.)

Watch out for:

  • Forgetting to move all terms to one side before factoring. \(x^2 = 5x + 6\) must become \(x^2 - 5x - 6 = 0\) first.
  • Sign errors in the quadratic formula. The numerator is \(-b \pm \sqrt{\ }\); for \(b = -5\), this is \(+5 \pm \sqrt{\ }\).
  • Rounding too early in the quadratic formula. Carry extra decimal places through \(\sqrt{}\) and divide only at the end.
  • Forgetting to check both roots in context (e.g., reject negative times).

Also B2.2 — interpret the discriminant. Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

2.3 — Completing the Square and Vertex Form
Use completing-the-square to convert standard to vertex form; find maximum or minimum values; apply to optimisation problems.
To complete the square on \( y = ax^2 + bx + c \): factor \(a\) from variable terms, take half of the inside linear coefficient and square it, add-and-subtract that value, then group. This yields \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\) where the vertex is \((h, k)\). Use this for any "maximum/minimum" problem (max revenue, min cost, max height, min surface area).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Completing the square is the algebraic technique that converts standard form \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) into vertex form \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\). It's the most useful single algebraic move for quadratics: once you have vertex form, the maximum or minimum value of \(y\) is \(k\), reached at \(x = h\). Every optimisation problem with a quadratic model — and there are many in technology — comes down to completing the square.

The procedure: (1) factor the leading coefficient \(a\) out of the variable terms (leave the constant alone). (2) inside the bracket, identify the linear coefficient. Take half of it and square; call this number \(q^2\). (3) add and subtract \(q^2\) inside the bracket — adding zero changes nothing. (4) group the first three terms into a perfect square: \((x + q)^2\). (5) distribute \(a\) through the leftover constant and combine with the original constant.

For example, take \(y = x^2 - 6x + 11\). The linear coefficient inside is \(-6\); half of \(-6\) is \(-3\); square is \(9\). Add and subtract: \(y = (x^2 - 6x + 9) - 9 + 11 = (x - 3)^2 + 2\). Vertex \((3, 2)\), minimum value 2.

When \(a \ne 1\), factor first. For \(y = 2x^2 - 8x + 5\): factor 2 from variable terms, \(y = 2(x^2 - 4x) + 5\). Inside: half of \(-4\) is \(-2\), square is \(4\). \(y = 2(x^2 - 4x + 4 - 4) + 5 = 2((x - 2)^2 - 4) + 5 = 2(x - 2)^2 - 8 + 5 = 2(x - 2)^2 - 3\). Vertex \((2, -3)\).

This algebraic step has a beautiful geometric meaning. The constant \(c\) gets split into "exactly what's needed to make the variable part a perfect square" plus "everything left over." The perfect-square part \(a(x - h)^2\) is always non-negative (or non-positive, if \(a < 0\)). So the minimum (or maximum) value of \(y\) is the leftover \(k\), reached when \((x - h)^2 = 0\), i.e. at \(x = h\).

  • Completing the square — algebraic move from \(ax^2 + bx + c\) to \(a(x - h)^2 + k\).
  • Vertex form — \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\); vertex \((h, k)\).
  • Optimisation — using vertex form to identify maximum or minimum values.
  • Perfect square trinomial — a polynomial of form \((x + q)^2 = x^2 + 2qx + q^2\); identified by the relation between linear and constant coefficient.

Example 1 — Complete the square on \(y = x^2 + 8x + 3\).

Half of 8 is 4; square is 16. \(y = (x^2 + 8x + 16) - 16 + 3 = (x + 4)^2 - 13\). Vertex \((-4, -13)\).

Example 2 — Maximum revenue. A bicycle shop's revenue (dollars) is \(R(p) = -2p^2 + 80p\), where \(p\) is the unit price (dollars). Find the price that maximises revenue and the maximum revenue.

Complete the square: \(R(p) = -2(p^2 - 40p) = -2((p^2 - 40p + 400) - 400) = -2(p - 20)^2 + 800\).

Vertex: \((20, 800)\). Maximum revenue is \$800 when \(p = \$20\).

Example 3 — Fence enclosure. 40 m of fencing is used to make a rectangular pen against a wall, no fence needed on the wall side. If \(w\) is the side perpendicular to the wall, then the length is \(40 - 2w\) and area is \(A(w) = w(40 - 2w) = -2w^2 + 40w\).

Complete the square: \(A = -2(w^2 - 20w) = -2((w - 10)^2 - 100) = -2(w - 10)^2 + 200\).

Maximum area 200 m² when \(w = 10\) m (length \(= 40 - 20 = 20\) m).

Watch out for:

  • Adding the same number to both sides versus adding-and-subtracting inside one expression. They are different algebraic moves; in completing the square inside an expression you must subtract the same constant you added.
  • Forgetting to factor \(a\) from the variable terms when \(a \ne 1\). Skipping this step gives an incorrect vertex.
  • When you factor \(a\) out and add \(q^2\) inside, you must subtract \(a \cdot q^2\) outside (or subtract \(q^2\) inside the bracket).
  • Sign of \(h\) at the vertex. Vertex form is \(a(x - h)^2 + k\), so vertex is \((h, k)\); if you read \((x + 5)^2\), then \(h = -5\).

Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

2.4 — Quadratic Applications in Technology
Apply quadratic models to projectile motion, profit/revenue maximisation, area optimisation, and other engineering technology contexts.
Most "real-world quadratic" problems follow one of three templates: (1) projectile motion \(h(t) = -gt^2/2 + v_0 t + h_0\); (2) revenue/profit \(R(p) = p \cdot D(p)\) for a linear demand \(D(p)\); (3) area \(A(x) = x(L - x)\) for a perimeter-fixed rectangle. Solve by finding the vertex (max/min) or by factoring/formula (zeros).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Quadratic functions describe an enormous range of technology phenomena. The reason is fundamental: when one quantity depends on another and the dependence involves a product or square (area, force times distance, energy versus velocity), a quadratic relation often results.

Projectile motion. Under gravity, the vertical position of a thrown or launched object is \(h(t) = -\tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 + v_0 t + h_0\), where \(g \approx 9.8\) m/s² (often rounded to 10 m/s² in MCT3M), \(v_0\) is the initial vertical velocity (m/s, positive if upward), and \(h_0\) is the initial height (m). The vertex gives the maximum height and the time it occurs. The zeros give times of ground impact. The formula appears in any context involving falling or thrown bodies — sports, weapons, fluid jets, dropped tools at a construction site.

Revenue and profit. If a product sells for price \(p\) and the demand at that price is a linear function \(D(p) = a - bp\), then revenue is \(R(p) = p \cdot D(p) = ap - bp^2\) — a downward parabola. The vertex gives the price that maximises revenue. Profit \(P(p) = R(p) - C\), where \(C\) is the (often constant) cost, is also quadratic with the same vertex shift. These problems are central to small-business engineering and operations management.

Area optimisation. A rectangle of perimeter \(P\) with width \(w\) has length \((P - 2w)/2 = P/2 - w\), so area is \(A(w) = w(P/2 - w) = (P/2)w - w^2\), a downward parabola. Maximum area at \(w = P/4\) gives a square — a classical optimisation result. With one side against a wall or with fixed area instead of perimeter, the algebra adapts, but the toolkit (vertex form, completing the square) is the same.

  • Projectile motion — \(h(t) = -\tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 + v_0 t + h_0\); apex at \(t = v_0/g\).
  • Revenue — \(R(p) = p \cdot D(p)\); downward parabola when demand is linearly decreasing.
  • Cost-Revenue-Profit — \(\text{Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost}\); break-even at zeros of profit.
  • Optimisation — finding the input that maximises or minimises an output; for a quadratic, the vertex.

Example 1 — A water cannon shoots a stream from 2 m above the ground at 25 m/s upward. Height \(h(t) = -5t^2 + 25t + 2\) (using \(g \approx 10\)). Find: (a) maximum height; (b) time of ground impact.

(a) Vertex at \(t = -b/(2a) = -25/(-10) = 2.5\) s. \(h(2.5) = -5(6.25) + 25(2.5) + 2 = -31.25 + 62.5 + 2 = 33.25\) m.

(b) Set \(h = 0\): \(-5t^2 + 25t + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{-25 \pm \sqrt{625 + 40}}{-10} = \dfrac{-25 \pm \sqrt{665}}{-10}\).

\(\sqrt{665} \approx 25.79\). Positive root: \(t \approx \dfrac{-25 - 25.79}{-10} \approx 5.08\) s.

Example 2 — Revenue from circuit boards. A workshop sells \(n = 200 - 4p\) boards per week at price \(p\) dollars. Find the price that maximises revenue.

Revenue: \(R(p) = p(200 - 4p) = 200p - 4p^2\).

Vertex at \(p = -200/(-8) = 25\) dollars.

Maximum revenue: \(R(25) = 25(200 - 100) = 25 \cdot 100 = \$2{,}500\).

Demand at \(p = 25\): \(n = 100\) boards per week.

Example 3 — Sheet metal box. A rectangular sheet 60 cm × 40 cm has equal squares cut from each corner and the sides folded up to form an open box. If each cut square has side \(x\) cm, find the value of \(x\) that maximises volume.

Dimensions of box: length \(60 - 2x\), width \(40 - 2x\), height \(x\). Volume: \(V(x) = x(60 - 2x)(40 - 2x)\).

This is cubic, not quadratic — but if the question asks for the value of \(x\) that maximises area of the base (a quadratic), then \(A(x) = (60-2x)(40-2x) = 4x^2 - 200x + 2400\). This is actually minimum (upward parabola). Reformulate: for volume, use technology (calculator/graph). Vertex of cubic computed numerically: max at \(x \approx 7.85\) cm, max volume \(\approx 8210\) cm³. (Calculus or technology required for the exact answer; quadratic methods give related answers.)

Watch out for:

  • Confusing the maximum value of \(y\) (the \(k\) in vertex form) with the \(x\)-value where it occurs (the \(h\)).
  • Forgetting that \(g\) appears as \(-g/2\) or \(-5\) in projectile equations (the "5" comes from \(g = 10\)).
  • Mixing up the demand function with the revenue function in price-optimisation problems.
  • Forgetting the contextual domain. Negative prices, negative times, and negative widths must be excluded.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "pose problems involving quadratic relations arising from real-world applications [e.g., revenue from a school dance, area of a rectangular garden] and solve them, by interpreting graphs and algebraic representations." Also B2.2 — "solve problems arising from real-world applications by determining the roots of a quadratic equation." Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

2.5 — Cubic Functions: Graphs and Key Features
Identify cubic functions; sketch their graphs and describe key features including end behaviour, intercepts, and turning points.
A cubic function has the form \( f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d \) with \(a \ne 0\). For \(y = x^3\): domain and range both all reals; passes through origin; increases everywhere; odd symmetry. End behaviour: as \(x \to \infty\), \(y \to \infty\) (if \(a>0\)); as \(x \to -\infty\), \(y \to -\infty\). A cubic has at most 3 real zeros and at most 2 turning points.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A cubic function is a polynomial of degree 3: \(f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d\) with \(a \ne 0\). Its graph is a smooth curve with no sharp corners, infinite in both directions (no asymptotes). Compared to a parabola, a cubic can have two turning points (one local max, one local min) and up to three distinct \(x\)-intercepts.

The parent cubic \(y = x^3\) has key features: passes through the origin, is increasing everywhere (no turning points — it's monotonic), and has odd symmetry (rotational, 180° about the origin: \(f(-x) = -f(x)\)). As \(x \to \infty\), \(y \to \infty\); as \(x \to -\infty\), \(y \to -\infty\). The graph crosses the \(x\)-axis once, at the origin (with a horizontal tangent — the cubic flattens at \(x = 0\)).

For a general cubic \(ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d\), the leading coefficient \(a\) controls end behaviour. If \(a > 0\), the graph rises to the upper-right and falls from the lower-left (like \(y = x^3\)). If \(a < 0\), the graph rises from the lower-right and falls to the upper-left (reflected in \(x\)-axis). The constant \(d\) is the \(y\)-intercept. The zeros (\(x\)-intercepts) come from factoring or polynomial division — see Topic 2.6.

A cubic can have:

  • three distinct real zeros (graph crosses the \(x\)-axis three times),
  • one real zero and one repeated double zero (graph crosses once, touches once),
  • one real triple zero (graph passes through the \(x\)-axis flat, like \(y = x^3\)),
  • one real zero and a pair of complex zeros (graph crosses once and never returns).

This greater richness compared to quadratics makes cubics suitable for modelling phenomena with one or two turning points: volume of a box as a function of cut size, work done by a variable force, third-degree fitting to non-linear data.

  • Cubic function — polynomial of degree 3.
  • End behaviour — the behaviour as \(x \to \pm \infty\); determined by the leading term.
  • Turning point — a local maximum or local minimum, where the function changes direction.
  • Inflection point — where the curve changes concavity; a cubic always has exactly one.
  • Monotonic — strictly increasing or strictly decreasing on its whole domain.

Example 1 — Sketch \(y = x^3 - 3x\).

End behaviour: as \(x \to \infty\), \(y \to \infty\); as \(x \to -\infty\), \(y \to -\infty\) (positive leading coefficient).

Zeros: factor \(x(x^2 - 3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0, \pm\sqrt{3} \approx \pm 1.73\). Three zeros, all real.

Quick values: \(y(1) = -2\), \(y(-1) = 2\), \(y(2) = 2\), \(y(-2) = -2\).

Turning points approximately at \(x = \pm 1\): \(y(\pm 1) = \mp 2\). So local max at \((-1, 2)\), local min at \((1, -2)\). Inflection at origin.

Example 2 — Compare graphs of \(y = x^3\), \(y = x^3 + 2\), and \(y = -x^3\).

\(y = x^3\): standard cubic, passes through origin.

\(y = x^3 + 2\): shift up 2. \(y\)-intercept at \((0, 2)\); \(x\)-intercept at \(\sqrt[3]{-2} \approx -1.26\).

\(y = -x^3\): reflection of \(y = x^3\) in the \(x\)-axis. Decreasing everywhere; passes through origin.

Example 3 — Volume of an open box. A 50 cm × 30 cm sheet has equal squares of side \(x\) cm cut from each corner; volume of the resulting box is \(V(x) = x(50 - 2x)(30 - 2x)\). Sketch this cubic and state the practical domain.

Expand: \(V(x) = x(1500 - 160x + 4x^2) = 4x^3 - 160x^2 + 1500x\).

Domain: \(x \in (0, 15)\) — between zero and half the shorter side, so dimensions stay positive.

End behaviour: positive leading coefficient, so increasing for large \(x\); but we only care about the bounded interval.

At \(x = 0\) and \(x = 15\), \(V = 0\) (degenerate). Maximum somewhere in between — by symmetry/calculus near \(x \approx 6\) cm.

Watch out for:

  • Confusing degree (the highest power) with the number of turning points. A cubic has at most 2 turning points, but can have 0 (monotonic) or 2.
  • End behaviour is set by the leading term only — lower-order terms don't change which way the graph goes for very large \(|x|\).
  • A cubic always has at least one real zero (it must cross or touch the \(x\)-axis somewhere because \(y \to +\infty\) and \(y \to -\infty\) at opposite ends).
  • Symmetry: \(y = x^3\) is odd (rotational). But \(y = x^3 + 1\) has no symmetry; the constant breaks the odd property.

Also B3.1 — recognise the cubic shape. Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics, 2007 (revised), MCT3M.

2.6 — Factoring Cubic Polynomials and Solving Cubic Equations
Factor cubic polynomials using common factoring, grouping, sum/difference of cubes, and the factor theorem; solve cubic equations.
Three reliable methods to factor a cubic: common factor, grouping (split four terms into two pairs), and the sum/difference of cubes: \(a^3 + b^3 = (a+b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)\), \(a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)\). For trinomials with one rational zero, use the factor theorem: if \(P(c) = 0\), then \((x - c)\) is a factor.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Factoring a cubic polynomial is one of the more demanding algebra skills in MCT3M, but the methods are systematic. Always check in this order: (1) common factor, (2) grouping (if four terms), (3) sum or difference of cubes (for binomials), (4) factor theorem and polynomial division (for more general cubics).

Common factor. Pull out any factor (variable or numerical) shared by every term. For \(2x^3 - 8x = 2x(x^2 - 4) = 2x(x-2)(x+2)\), the GCF is \(2x\).

Grouping. When a polynomial has four terms (e.g. \(x^3 + 2x^2 - 4x - 8\)), try to group it as two pairs that share a common bracket. \(= x^2(x + 2) - 4(x + 2) = (x^2 - 4)(x + 2) = (x - 2)(x + 2)(x + 2) = (x - 2)(x + 2)^2\).

Sum and difference of cubes. These special patterns appear frequently:

\[ a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2), \qquad a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2) \]

Memorise both; they apply when you recognise both sides as perfect cubes (\(8, 27, 64, 125, x^3, 8y^3\), etc.).

Factor theorem. If a polynomial \(P(x)\) has \(P(c) = 0\), then \((x - c)\) is a factor. To find a candidate \(c\): for an integer-coefficient cubic, try \(\pm 1\), \(\pm 2\), \(\pm 3\), and divisors of the constant term. Once one root \(c\) is found, divide \(P(x) \div (x - c)\) using long division or synthetic division to get a quadratic, then factor or use the quadratic formula.

For solving \(P(x) = 0\), factor first (using any of the above), then set each factor to zero. Each linear factor \((x - c)\) contributes the root \(x = c\); each quadratic factor \(ax^2 + bx + c\) contributes either two roots or no real roots depending on the discriminant.

  • Common factor (GCF) — the greatest common factor of every term.
  • Grouping — split a four-term polynomial into two binomials, factor each, then look for a shared bracket.
  • Sum of cubes — \(a^3 + b^3 = (a+b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)\).
  • Difference of cubes — \(a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)\).
  • Factor theorem — \(P(c) = 0 \iff (x - c)\) is a factor of \(P(x)\).
  • Synthetic division — a streamlined long-division for dividing by linear factors of the form \((x - c)\).

Example 1 — Factor \(8x^3 - 27\).

Recognise difference of cubes with \(a = 2x\), \(b = 3\). Apply formula:

\(8x^3 - 27 = (2x - 3)((2x)^2 + (2x)(3) + 3^2) = (2x - 3)(4x^2 + 6x + 9)\).

The second factor has discriminant \(36 - 144 = -108 < 0\), so no further real factoring.

Example 2 — Factor \(x^3 - 4x^2 - x + 4\) using grouping.

Group as pairs: \((x^3 - 4x^2) + (-x + 4) = x^2(x - 4) - 1(x - 4) = (x^2 - 1)(x - 4)\).

Difference of squares: \(x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)\).

Final: \((x - 1)(x + 1)(x - 4)\). Zeros at \(x = 1, -1, 4\).

Example 3 — Solve \(x^3 - 3x^2 - 6x + 8 = 0\) using the factor theorem.

Test \(x = 1\): \(1 - 3 - 6 + 8 = 0\). So \(x - 1\) is a factor.

Divide: \(x^3 - 3x^2 - 6x + 8 = (x - 1)(x^2 - 2x - 8)\).

Factor the quadratic: \(x^2 - 2x - 8 = (x - 4)(x + 2)\).

Full factorisation: \((x - 1)(x - 4)(x + 2)\). Roots: \(x = 1, 4, -2\).

Watch out for:

  • The middle terms in sum/difference of cubes patterns have the "wrong" sign compared to what students expect: \(a^3 + b^3 = (a+b)(a^2 \mathbf{-} ab + b^2)\). Memorise.
  • After grouping, if no common bracket appears, try a different grouping (sometimes the order of pairs matters).
  • The factor theorem only finds rational roots; some cubics have only irrational or complex roots and won't yield to integer testing.
  • After finding one root \(c\), don't stop — divide and continue factoring the quadratic. A cubic has three roots (counting multiplicity).
