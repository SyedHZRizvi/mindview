Chapter 1: Identifying Issues That Matter

The starting point of civic action is the disciplined identification of an issue: what makes it political, who is affected, whose rights are at stake, what evidence is credible, and how the issue connects to decisions made by governments and other power-holders.

🧭 1.1 The Political Inquiry Process & Concepts of Political Thinking
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Politics, as the Ontario curriculum frames it, is not a synonym for what happens during election campaigns. It is the ongoing process by which a community makes binding decisions about how to live together — who gets what, when, and how. The discipline of political science (and of CPC3O) gives you a method for examining those decisions: the political inquiry process. It is not a single template but a five-stage rhythm — formulate questions, gather and organise information, interpret and analyse, evaluate and draw conclusions, communicate. You will move through these stages many times in this course, sometimes in a single lesson, sometimes across a multi-week project.

Inquiry begins with a question that is actually answerable. "Is politics bad?" is not an inquiry question — it is a slogan. "How effective have municipal participatory-budgeting programs been at increasing youth civic participation in three Canadian cities since 2018?" is an inquiry question because it is open-ended (no yes/no), bounded (three cities, post-2018), contestable (informed people will disagree), and significant (real-world policy consequences). The first thing a Grade 11 politics student learns to do is to take a vague topic ("I care about climate change") and refine it into a question that an investigation can actually answer.

Alongside the inquiry process, you will work with four concepts of political thinking — the lenses that distinguish political analysis from other kinds. Political significance asks why this issue matters and for whom; an event can be hugely significant for one community and unnoticed by another. Objectives and results compares what political actors set out to do against what actually happened; intent and outcome often diverge. Stability and change asks what has remained constant and what has been transformed — change is rarely total and stability is rarely complete. Political perspective asks whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue — political actors hold positions because of where they sit, what they value, and whom they speak for. A strong political analysis names which concept is being used and what work it is doing in the argument.

Political inquiry is not value-neutral, but it is disciplined. Politics involves contested values — about freedom, equality, security, identity, the role of government, our obligations to one another and to the planet. The inquiry process does not ask you to drop your values; it asks you to be transparent about them, to engage seriously with values you do not share, and to ground your conclusions in evidence that someone with different values could also recognise as evidence. The test of a good political-science answer is not whether your grandmother agrees with it; it is whether someone who disagrees with your conclusion would still recognise that your reasoning is honest.

  • Political inquiry process — five-stage method: formulate questions → gather/organise → interpret/analyse → evaluate/conclude → communicate.
  • Inquiry question — open-ended, bounded, contestable, significant.
  • Political significance — why an issue matters and for whom.
  • Objectives and results — comparison of intent and actual outcome.
  • Stability and change — what has held constant and what has transformed.
  • Political perspective — whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue.

Worked Example — Turning a Vague Concern Into an Inquiry Question

A student arrives in class saying "I'm angry about housing." That is a starting point, not an inquiry question.

Step 1: bound the topic. Housing where? (One city, one province, Canada, one neighbourhood?) Housing for whom? (Renters, owners, students, newcomers, unhoused people?) Step 2: identify the political dimension. Who decides on housing supply, zoning, rent rules, social housing, homelessness response? At which level of government? Step 3: identify a question that an investigation can actually answer. "How effective have Ontario's 2023 zoning-reform provisions for fourplexes been at increasing housing supply in mid-sized cities (Kitchener, Hamilton, London) by 2026?" This is open-ended, bounded (Ontario, three cities, two-year window), contestable (developers, planners, and tenant groups disagree about effects), and significant (real policy stakes). The student now has a project — not just a feeling.

  • Treating "politics" as a synonym for election campaigns — political decisions are made constantly and at every level.
  • Skipping the question-formulation stage and jumping straight to "research" — without a focused question, research produces noise.
  • Naming a concept of political thinking ("significance") without doing analytical work with it.
  • Mistaking strong feelings for an argument — inquiry requires evidence that someone who disagrees with you would also recognise.
  • Believing political inquiry must be "objective" in a way that erases your values — the discipline is transparency about values, not their elimination.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in Canadian and/or international politics"; A1.6 — "use the concepts of political thinking when analysing current events and issues."

🧩 1.2 Identifying Issues: Political vs Personal, Local vs Global
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Not every problem is a political issue, and not every political issue requires the same level of response. One of the first analytical moves a CPC3O student learns is to distinguish a personal trouble from a political issue. The sociologist C. Wright Mills made the distinction famous: if one person loses their job, that is a personal trouble; when fifteen per cent of a city's workers lose their jobs at once, that is a public issue. The personal/political line is not always obvious — issues like mental health, intimate-partner violence, and unpaid care work were treated as private matters for generations before political movements made the case that they had structural, policy-shaped dimensions.

An issue is political when it (1) involves a contested public decision, (2) affects a community beyond the individual, and (3) is shaped by — or could be changed by — the action of a power-holder (a government, a corporation, an institution, or an organised movement). A student who is being bullied in a hallway has a personal trouble that the school's discipline system should address. The pattern of bullying in a school board, the policies that respond to it, the funding for mental-health supports, and the public statements about school safety are political issues — they involve contested decisions, broad effects, and identifiable power-holders.

Political issues exist on multiple scales. Local issues — the operating hours of a community centre, the routing of a bike lane, the closure of a school — are typically decided by municipal councils, school boards, local agencies, or provincial bodies acting in a specific community. National issues — federal climate policy, the design of the federal child benefit, the regulation of artificial intelligence — are decided in Ottawa or in provincial capitals. Global issues — climate change, refugee protection, pandemic preparedness, international trade — cross borders and involve international bodies (the UN, the WHO, regional organisations), bilateral treaties, and the foreign policy of national governments. Most issues that students care deeply about operate on more than one scale at once. Climate change is global in cause, national in policy, and local in lived impact.

When you identify an issue, you also start identifying its frame. The same set of facts can be framed as a public-safety issue, a public-health issue, a human-rights issue, an economic issue, or a cultural-identity issue, and the frame shapes which power-holders are seen as responsible and which solutions seem reasonable. Homelessness is a different political issue when framed as a housing-supply problem (build more housing), as a mental-health problem (expand treatment), as an income problem (raise social assistance), or as a public-order problem (enforce park rules). Skilled civic actors choose their framing carefully, knowing that the frame is itself part of the politics.

  • Personal trouble — a difficulty experienced by an individual within an existing social/policy structure.
  • Political issue — a contested public decision affecting a community, shaped by an identifiable power-holder.
  • Local / national / global — scales at which political issues operate; most issues operate on more than one.
  • Issue framing — the lens through which an issue is presented (safety, health, rights, economics, identity); framing influences who is seen as responsible.
  • Power-holder — a person, institution, or body whose decisions shape outcomes for others.
  • Public dimension — the threshold beyond which a private trouble is also a public concern.

Worked Example — Sorting Personal From Political

A student writes "I'm worried about being unable to afford university." Is this a personal trouble, a political issue, or both?

Step 1: identify the personal layer. The student's family budget, financial-aid options, and choice of university are private decisions. Step 2: identify the structural layer. Ontario university tuition was cut 10% in 2019 and then frozen, but student debt, OSAP eligibility rules, the cost of living in university towns, and post-graduation labour markets are shaped by public policy and contested decisions. Step 3: identify the public dimension. If many students share the worry — and Statistics Canada data show that they do — then affordability of post-secondary education is a political issue. Step 4: name the relevant power-holders. The Ontario Ministry of Colleges and Universities, the federal Canada Student Financial Assistance Program, the universities themselves, the federal Office of the Parliamentary Budget Officer. Conclusion: this is both personal and political. The student should be helped with personal financial planning and can engage politically (deputation to a Standing Committee on Finance, joining the Canadian Federation of Students, writing to their MP).

  • Treating every difficulty as either purely personal or purely political — most have both layers.
  • Naming an issue without identifying the relevant power-holder — vague calls for "the government" to act usually fail.
  • Conflating local, national, and global scales in a single argument — name which scale you mean.
  • Forgetting that the way an issue is framed shapes which solutions appear available.
  • Assuming "the people affected" and "the decision-maker" are the same — they often are not, and that gap is part of the politics.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "describe political issues of local, national, and/or international significance, and explain why they are of political concern"; B1.2 — "analyse how various individuals and/or groups have responded to selected political issues, with reference to political-thinking concepts."

👥 1.3 Stakeholders, Perspectives, and Whose Voice Counts
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every political issue has more than one stakeholder, and the analytical move that separates a strong essay from a weak one is taking the time to map them. A stakeholder is any person or group whose interests are affected by a decision — directly, indirectly, in the short term, or in the long term. A perspective is the position a stakeholder holds, which is shaped by their interests, their values, their lived experience, and the information they have access to. Stakeholders are not always organised, equally resourced, or equally heard; perspectives are not always articulated; both are nonetheless part of the political picture.

A stakeholder map starts by listing everyone who is affected — including people far from the decision-makers. For a debate over the route of a new hydro transmission line, the most visible stakeholders are landowners along the proposed route, the utility company, and the provincial regulator. But Indigenous communities with treaty rights to the land, downstream water users, future residents not yet living in the corridor, biodiversity advocates, and ratepayers across the province all have stakes. A strong stakeholder analysis names each, identifies what each gains or loses, identifies how each is currently represented in the decision-making process, and identifies which voices are not heard at all. The not-heard voices are often the most politically important to surface.

Perspectives are shaped by a mix of interests (what someone gains or loses materially), values (what someone believes is right), identity (community, culture, religion, place), and information (what someone has been told and by whom). Two stakeholders with the same interest can still disagree because their values differ; two stakeholders with the same value can disagree because their interests differ. The Ontario curriculum is clear that students should be able to articulate multiple perspectives accurately — what civics teachers call steel-manning: presenting the strongest version of a position you do not personally hold, in language a person holding that position would recognise as fair. Steel-manning is harder than dismissing, and it is the discipline that builds credibility.

The question of whose voice counts is itself a political question. Indigenous-rights theory (and the constitutional duty to consult, articulated in Haida Nation, 2004) makes clear that being affected is not enough; there are stakeholders who must be consulted by the Crown before decisions can lawfully proceed. Disability-rights advocacy has popularised the principle "Nothing About Us, Without Us" — that policies affecting disabled people must be co-designed with disabled people. Climate-justice advocacy has pushed the question of intergenerational stakeholders — what counts as representation for people who will be alive in 2050 but cannot vote today. Civics is, in part, the discipline of taking these questions seriously rather than waving them off.

  • Stakeholder — a person or group whose interests are affected by a decision.
  • Perspective — the position a stakeholder holds, shaped by interests, values, identity, and information.
  • Stakeholder map — a structured listing of all affected stakeholders and what each gains, loses, and represents.
  • Steel-manning — presenting the strongest version of a position you do not personally hold.
  • Duty to consult — the constitutional obligation on the Crown to consult Indigenous peoples on decisions that may affect their rights.
  • Intergenerational stakeholders — future people whose interests are affected by present decisions.

Worked Example — Stakeholder-Mapping a School-Bus Cancellation

A school board considers cancelling bus service to students living less than 1.5 km from their school.

Step 1: list the directly affected. Students in the affected zone; their parents and caregivers; teachers (start-of-day routines); bus operators and drivers. Step 2: list the indirectly affected. Local traffic and walking-route safety, the school's attendance rate, food-program access, students with mobility disabilities, racialised families with longer histories of unsafe street experience. Step 3: list the institutional actors. School-board trustees who will vote; provincial Ministry of Education (transportation funding rules); local police (school-zone safety enforcement); municipal traffic engineers. Step 4: identify the voices missing. Newcomer families without strong English/French; families working morning shifts who cannot attend evening consultations; students themselves. Step 5: surface the perspectives. Cost-cutting trustees value budget discipline; affected families value access and safety; advocates for equity value the disproportionate impact on lower-income families. The decision is not just a numbers exercise; it is a values exercise made visible by stakeholder analysis.

  • Listing only the loudest stakeholders — the loudest are not always the most affected.
  • Treating all stakeholders as equally resourced and equally heard — they are not, and the gap is part of the issue.
  • Ignoring intergenerational stakeholders, especially on climate, debt, and infrastructure decisions.
  • Confusing perspective with interest — they overlap but are not identical.
  • Skipping the steel-manning step — if you cannot state the opposing position fairly, you have not yet understood it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "explain why people have different perspectives on civic issues and how perspectives may change over time"; A1.4 — "interpret and analyse evidence and information relevant to their investigations, using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for political inquiry."

⚖️ 1.4 Rights Frameworks: The Charter, Human-Rights Codes, & UDHR
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A right is a claim a person or group can make against a power-holder — most often a government — that the law (or an international body) will recognise and, in many cases, enforce. Rights are not the only basis for political claims (interest, fairness, mercy, tradition, and prudence are all valid), but rights claims occupy a special place because they are framed as protections that should not be traded away in ordinary political bargaining. In a CPC3O issue analysis, identifying which rights are engaged is part of identifying the issue.

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982) sets out the constitutional rights all governments in Canada must respect. The Charter covers fundamental freedoms (s. 2 — conscience and religion, expression, peaceful assembly, association), democratic rights (s. 3–5 — voting, candidacy, the five-year limit on a legislature), mobility (s. 6 — entering, remaining in, and leaving Canada), legal rights (s. 7–14 — life, liberty, and security of the person; protection from unreasonable search; right to counsel; presumption of innocence), equality (s. 15 — equal protection without discrimination), and language rights (s. 16–22 — French and English in federal institutions). Charter rights are not absolute; section 1 allows "reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society," analysed through the Oakes test (pressing objective, rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionality). Section 33 — the notwithstanding clause — allows a legislature to override sections 2 and 7–15 for up to five renewable years, a uniquely Canadian feature whose use has expanded in recent years (Quebec's Bill 21, Ontario's threatened use in labour disputes).

The Charter binds governments only — federal, provincial, territorial, municipal, and the executive branch at every level. Private parties — employers, businesses, individuals — are not directly bound by the Charter. They are bound by provincial human-rights codes (in Ontario, the Human Rights Code, R.S.O. 1990, c. H.19) and, where federal jurisdiction applies, by the Canadian Human Rights Act. These statutes prohibit discrimination in employment, housing, services, and contracts on protected grounds (in Ontario: age, ancestry, citizenship, colour, creed, disability, ethnic origin, family status, gender expression, gender identity, marital status, place of origin, race, record of offences, sex, and sexual orientation). When a student is refused service because of religious dress, when an employee is harassed because of their gender identity, when a tenant is denied housing because they receive social assistance — these are human-rights-code matters, not Charter matters, even though they may feel similar.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948), adopted by the UN General Assembly with Canadian John Humphrey as one of its drafters, is the international foundation document. It is not directly enforceable in Canadian courts but it shapes Canadian foreign policy, treaty obligations, and how Canadian rights are interpreted. Subsequent International Covenants on Civil and Political Rights and Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (both 1966) operationalise the UDHR and are binding treaties Canada has ratified. More recent international frameworks include the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007 — Canada formally endorsed in 2010 and passed implementation legislation in 2021), and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006). Strong civics analysis names which framework applies, which level binds, and how the frameworks interact when they do.

  • Charter — Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982; binds Canadian governments; rights summarised in seven categories.
  • Section 1 (Oakes test) — reasonable limits on Charter rights, with the four-stage proportionality analysis.
  • Section 33 (notwithstanding clause) — five-year renewable legislative override of certain Charter sections.
  • Provincial human-rights code — anti-discrimination statute binding private parties (in Ontario: Human Rights Code).
  • UDHR — 1948 international declaration; foundational but not directly enforceable in Canada.
  • UNDRIP — 2007 UN declaration on Indigenous-peoples' rights; Canada implemented through 2021 legislation.
  • Charter vs. Code distinction — the Charter binds governments; codes bind private parties.

Worked Example — Charter, Code, or Both?

Scenario: a transit officer refuses to allow a passenger wearing a niqab to use a monthly pass that her face is partly covered when boarding.

Step 1: is the transit authority a government body? Yes — public transit is operated by a municipal commission, which is a creature of provincial statute. The Charter applies. Step 2: which Charter rights are engaged? Section 2(a) freedom of religion; section 15 equality (protected ground: religion). Step 3: would the human-rights code also apply? Yes — the Ontario Human Rights Code prohibits discrimination in services on the ground of creed, and transit is a service. Step 4: if the policy is government-issued, the Charter applies and the s. 1 Oakes test would govern justification. If a private taxi driver had done the same thing, only the human-rights code would apply. A real policy response would have to accommodate the wearer (verification of identity through alternative means — name + transit-pass photo, a brief verification by a female officer if needed) rather than refuse service. The accommodation test under both the Charter and the Code asks: is there a legitimate purpose? Has the duty to accommodate been met short of undue hardship?

  • Citing the Charter when the actor is a private party — the Charter binds government.
  • Citing the U.S. Bill of Rights or constitutional amendments in a Canadian context — they do not apply.
  • Treating Charter rights as absolute — section 1 saves many infringements that pass the Oakes test.
  • Forgetting section 33 — democratic majorities can, controversially, override certain rights for up to five years.
  • Confusing the UDHR with binding domestic law — it shapes interpretation but does not directly create enforceable Canadian rights.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "describe rights of Canadian citizens (e.g., the rights protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms; human rights protected by the Ontario Human Rights Code) and the related responsibilities"; B3.2 — "analyse, with reference to political-thinking concepts, ways in which rights and freedoms are protected in Canada and other parts of the world (e.g., the Universal Declaration of Human Rights; UNDRIP)."

🔍 1.5 Research, Source Evaluation, & Lateral Reading
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Political inquiry depends on credible evidence, and credible evidence depends on disciplined source evaluation. The internet's defining feature is not that information is free; it is that misinformation (false content shared without intent to deceive), disinformation (deliberately false content), and propaganda (content shaped to advance a political agenda) are abundant, well-designed, and indistinguishable at a glance from credible information. A Grade 11 politics student needs more than the slogan "check your sources." You need a method.

The traditional method most students were taught was vertical reading — staying on a website and looking at its design, claims, "About Us" page, and footnotes. Research on professional fact-checkers (Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew at Stanford, 2017) found that journalists and academics do something different: lateral reading. They leave the source within seconds, open new tabs, search for what other independent sources say about the original — the author, the organisation, the funding, the track record — and only then return to read the original carefully. The discipline is to ask "who is telling me this and what do other independent observers say about them?" before accepting any claim.

One useful framework for source evaluation is the CRAAP test — Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose. Currency: how recent is this, and is recency important for this question? Relevance: does this source actually address my question or a tangentially related one? Authority: who created this, what is their expertise, and what is their stake in the outcome? Accuracy: are the claims verifiable, are the citations real, do the numbers match cited primary sources? Purpose: what is the source trying to do — inform, persuade, sell, mobilise, alarm? CRAAP is not a checklist to memorise; it is a discipline to apply.

For political topics, you will encounter several genres of source, each with strengths and weaknesses. Government statistics and reports (Statistics Canada, the Parliamentary Budget Officer, the Auditor General, the Information and Privacy Commissioner) are primary sources of data — strong on numbers, weak on framing. Journalism (CBC News, the Globe and Mail, the Toronto Star, local newspapers) covers events and trends — strong on access and timeliness, weak when it relies on a single source or is shaped by editorial framing. Academic research (peer-reviewed journals, university research centres) gives careful analysis but is often slow and behind paywalls. Advocacy organisations (unions, think tanks, NGOs, industry associations) give you the strongest version of a particular perspective; you should read them as advocacy, not as neutral fact. Social media spreads claims faster than any other channel and has the highest concentration of misinformation. Indigenous knowledge holders and community-based research are essential primary sources on Indigenous issues, but they require relationship and consent, not extraction. A strong inquiry triangulates across multiple genres.

  • Misinformation — false information shared without intent to deceive.
  • Disinformation — deliberately false information designed to deceive.
  • Propaganda — information (true, false, or framed) produced to advance an agenda.
  • Lateral reading — leaving a source to check what independent sources say about it.
  • CRAAP test — Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
  • Primary source — original measurement, document, or first-hand account (government statistics; a transcript; a treaty text).
  • Secondary source — analysis or commentary about a primary source (news article; academic paper).
  • Triangulation — corroborating a claim across multiple independent sources or genres.

Worked Example — Lateral Reading on a Viral Statistic

A social-media post claims "Statistics Canada found that 1 in 4 Canadian families spent more than half their income on housing in 2025." A student wants to use this in an essay.

Step 1: pause. Do not copy the statistic. Open a new tab. Step 2: search the Statistics Canada website directly for housing-affordability data. Locate the source release: the term Statistics Canada uses is "core housing need" (under 30% of pre-tax income on shelter, adequate and suitable). Step 3: check the actual figure. The most recent Canadian Housing Survey reports approximately 10% of Canadian households in core housing need, with higher rates in renters, urban centres, and Indigenous households. The "1 in 4" figure may refer to a related but different measure (shelter-cost-to-income ratio above 30%), and the "more than half" claim is plausibly disinformation or a careless paraphrase. Step 4: decide. Use the verified Statistics Canada figure in the essay, cite the release, and explicitly note which measure you are using. Do not use the viral version. Step 5: extend the discipline. If you cannot find the primary source within five minutes, the claim is not yet usable.

  • Staying on a single website to evaluate it — switch tabs.
  • Trusting a number because it has a citation — read the citation.
  • Treating all "official" sources as equally reliable — the Auditor General and a government press release have very different functions.
  • Citing an advocacy organisation as neutral evidence — it is evidence of the perspective, not of neutral fact.
  • Forgetting to triangulate — a single source is rarely enough, even a strong one.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations"; A2.4 — "use appropriate forms of documentation to acknowledge different types of sources."

🗺️ 1.6 Mapping a Political Issue: From Concern to Project
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The capstone task for Chapter 1 — and the launching pad for the rest of CPC3O — is mapping one political issue you care about. The mapping converts a vague concern into a project a Grade 11 student can actually pursue. The map has six elements: (1) the issue stated as a question, (2) the affected stakeholders, (3) the responsible decision-maker(s), (4) the relevant rights and frameworks, (5) the evidence base, and (6) the candidate actions. Each of these draws on the previous five lessons; together they produce an issue-brief you can refine over the course of the semester.

The first element — the issue as a question — applies the discipline of section 1.1. Take your concern ("AI in schools") and translate it into something an inquiry can answer ("Should Ontario's Ministry of Education require all secondary schools to adopt an AI-acceptable-use policy by September 2027, and what should that policy include?"). The question's scope must match the time you have to work on it; if your question is too large, narrow it; if it is so narrow that it has only one obvious answer, broaden it.

The second element — affected stakeholders — applies section 1.3. List the people and groups gaining, losing, or implicated by the decision. Be specific: not "students" but "Grade 9–12 students in public secondary schools in Ontario, with attention to English Language Learners, students with IEPs, and Indigenous students." Distinguish those affected directly (students, teachers) from those affected indirectly (employers hiring graduates, universities receiving graduates). Note who has voice in the current decision process and who does not.

The third element — the decision-maker — names the specific person or body whose decision determines the outcome. "The government" is not a decision-maker; "the Ontario Minister of Education and Cabinet, advised by the Ministry's Office of Education Technology, with input from the College of Teachers and OPSBA" is. Identify whether the decision sits with one level of government (federal, provincial, municipal), a public agency (a school board, a regulator), or a private actor (a corporation, an institution). Different decision-makers respond to different forms of civic action — see Chapter 2.

The fourth element — rights and frameworks — applies section 1.4. Which Charter sections are engaged? Which provisions of the Ontario Human Rights Code or other statutes? Which international frameworks (UNDRIP for Indigenous issues, the Convention on the Rights of the Child for issues touching minors, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities for accessibility)? Naming the rights framework strengthens the political argument because it shifts the conversation from preference to legal and moral entitlement.

The fifth element — the evidence base — applies section 1.5. List the three to five sources you have already triangulated and the gaps you still need to fill. The sixth element — candidate actions — points forward to Chapters 2 and 3: petition, deputation, advocacy campaign, public-education effort, art / social-media campaign, formal submission to a consultation, voting, partnership with an existing organisation. By the end of Chapter 1 you have a sketch; by the end of the course you have a plan.

  • Issue-mapping — the six-element discipline that converts a concern into a project.
  • Bounded question — the issue stated as something an inquiry can answer in available time.
  • Direct vs. indirect stakeholders — those affected immediately vs. those affected downstream.
  • Identified decision-maker — the specific person or body whose decision determines the outcome.
  • Rights anchor — the Charter section, statutory provision, or international framework relevant to the issue.
  • Triangulated evidence — three to five sources, of differing genre, that cross-check each other.
  • Candidate actions — the menu of possible civic-engagement responses (developed in Chapter 2).

Worked Example — Mapping a Climate-Curriculum Issue

(1) Issue as question: "Should the Ontario Ministry of Education update the Grade 9 Geography curriculum to make climate-change adaptation a mandatory expectation rather than an optional one, and what content should be required?" (2) Stakeholders: Grade 9 students; secondary teachers; school boards; the Ontario Climate Action Network; environmental scientists; energy-sector employers; Indigenous communities (TEK-traditional ecological knowledge); parents. (3) Decision-maker: Ontario Minister of Education, advised by the Curriculum and Assessment Policy Branch. (4) Rights/frameworks: section 7 Charter (security of the person — youth climate-litigation cases); UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (article 28, right to education; article 3, best interests of the child); UNDRIP (educational provisions). (5) Evidence base: existing Ontario Geography curriculum; OECD curriculum analysis; the federal Climate Plan; case studies from Quebec's mandatory environmental-citizenship content; lateral-read of advocacy claims. (6) Candidate actions: youth deputation to the Standing Committee on Social Policy; coordinated petition through Fridays for Future Canada; op-ed in a regional paper; collaboration with the Ontario Teachers' Federation. The map fits on one page and gives a Grade 11 student a feasible plan for the semester.

  • Mapping a concern instead of an issue — a concern is a feeling; an issue is a contested public decision.
  • Naming "the government" instead of a specific decision-maker.
  • Listing rights without showing how they are engaged in the specific issue.
  • Skipping the evidence base — "I just feel strongly" is not a project.
  • Committing to a candidate action before mapping is complete — action without map often misses the actual decision-maker.
