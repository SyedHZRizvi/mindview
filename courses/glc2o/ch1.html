🔍 1.1 Why Self-Assessment Matters
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Self-assessment is the foundation of every later choice in this course. Before you pick a postsecondary destination, sketch a budget, or write a résumé, you have to know who you are bringing to that work. The Ministry of Education makes this point explicitly in Creating Pathways to Success: career and life planning begins with the question "Who am I?" and only then turns to "What are my opportunities?" Skipping the first question and starting with "What job pays well?" almost always produces a plan that wobbles the first time something unexpected happens — a course you hate, a job you dread on Sunday night, a program you transfer out of in second year.

Self-assessment is not a one-off personality test that hands you a career label. It is a habit. Researchers describe it as a cycle: gather evidence about yourself (inventories, feedback, journaling), interpret the evidence (what patterns repeat, what surprises you?), test it in a small real situation (try a club, a job shadow, a co-op), and revise. Each cycle sharpens your self-knowledge. The student who has done four cycles by graduation has a much better basis for choosing a program than the student who once did a single quiz in Grade 10.

Good self-assessment uses multiple sources. A single online quiz captures one slice of you. Triangulating — combining an interest inventory, a skills inventory, a learning-style survey, peer feedback, and your own reflective writing — produces a richer picture. The Ontario Skills Passport (OSP) categorises essential skills that employers value (reading, writing, numeracy, oral communication, digital, problem solving, teamwork, finding information, continuous learning) and is a useful common language for talking about skills.

One common student worry: "Won't a label limit me?" It depends on how you hold the label. A Holland code (RIASEC) or a personality result is best used as a hypothesis — "I might be drawn to investigative work, let me test that with a job shadow" — not a destination. The most damaging way to use self-assessment is to treat the result as fixed identity and ignore evidence that contradicts it. The most useful way is to treat the result as a starting point and re-test it as you grow. The Ministry emphasises that career-development planning is a continuous, lifelong process; the same point applies to self-knowledge.

  • Self-assessment — a structured process of examining your interests, skills, values, personality, and learning preferences.
  • Personal profile — the summary document that captures the results of self-assessment for use in planning.
  • Triangulation — combining multiple sources of evidence (inventories, feedback, reflection) to produce a richer picture.
  • Reflective writing — written self-examination that turns experience into self-knowledge.
  • Ontario Skills Passport (OSP) — Ministry database of essential employability skills and work habits.
  • Individual Pathways Plan (IPP) — the K-12 portfolio in which you record self-assessment results and goals.

Worked Example — Building a One-Page Personal Profile

Maya, a Grade 10 student, takes four short self-assessments early in the semester: a RIASEC interest quiz (top codes: Social, Investigative), a multiple-intelligences quiz (top three: interpersonal, linguistic, naturalistic), a values card-sort (top five: helping others, learning, balance, family, autonomy), and a VARK learning style (preference: read/write & kinesthetic). She also asks three trusted people — her science teacher, her drama coach, her older sister — to name one strength they see in her. Each says some version of "you explain things in a way that makes sense to people who are struggling." She synthesises into a one-page profile: "Interests: science, people, the outdoors. Values: helping, learning, balance. Skills (other-reported): explaining, listening. Learning preferences: read/write & hands-on. Working hypothesis: a career that blends science with people and explanation — possibly health professions, teaching, or environmental education." Notice that the profile is a hypothesis, not a verdict. Maya now has something concrete to test against opportunities in Unit 2.

  • Treating a single online quiz as a definitive answer.
  • Ignoring feedback from others — externally-observed strengths are valuable evidence.
  • Writing a profile that is so vague ("I like people") it can't guide decisions.
  • Refusing to revise the profile when contradictory evidence appears (e.g., you hated the volunteer placement you thought you'd love).
  • Confusing self-assessment with self-criticism — the goal is accurate description, not judgment.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "use a variety of self-assessment tools and processes to identify their personal characteristics (e.g., interests, abilities, learning styles, work values, personality traits)."

🎨 1.2 Interests — RIASEC and Holland's Theory
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Interests answer the question "what do I genuinely like doing?" The American psychologist John Holland argued that people and work environments can be sorted into six broad types — and that satisfaction tends to follow when there is a good match between the person and the work setting. The six types form the acronym RIASEC: Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional. Holland's theory is one of the most validated frameworks in career-development research and underlies the Canadian Occupational Information Network (NOC) and Job Bank tools that label careers by interest type.

Each type prefers a different way of engaging with the world. Realistic (Doers) like hands-on, mechanical, outdoor, or practical work — electricians, paramedics, farmers, athletes. Investigative (Thinkers) like analysing, researching, problem-solving — biologists, software developers, doctors, statisticians. Artistic (Creators) like self-expression, design, and aesthetic work — graphic designers, musicians, writers, architects. Social (Helpers) like teaching, counselling, helping, training — nurses, teachers, social workers, coaches. Enterprising (Persuaders) like leading, selling, and influencing — sales managers, entrepreneurs, lawyers, marketers. Conventional (Organisers) like structured, detail-oriented, data work — accountants, administrators, logistics analysts.

Most people are not one type — they are a combination of two or three. That is why RIASEC produces a three-letter code (e.g., SIA — Social/Investigative/Artistic). The top two letters matter most. Holland also proposed a hexagonal model in which adjacent types share more in common (Social and Artistic are neighbours; Realistic and Social are opposites). When your code combines opposite types, it is a hint that you might enjoy work that bridges them rather than picks one (e.g., an SR code might enjoy outdoor education).

Important caveat: an interest inventory measures preference, not aptitude or opportunity. You can love something you are not yet good at — and being interested makes it more likely you will put in the practice to become good. You can also be interested in fields that are over-supplied with applicants, or that pay poorly. Interest is necessary but not sufficient for a good career choice. The Ontario Ministry of Education positions self-assessment results — including RIASEC — as the start of inquiry, not the end. The next step is to research occupations that match your code (Unit 2) and to test the match in a low-stakes way (job shadow, club, summer job).

  • Interests — activities and topics a person finds genuinely engaging.
  • Holland's RIASEC — six interest types: Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional.
  • Holland code — the three-letter summary of a person's top three interest types.
  • National Occupational Classification (NOC) — Canadian system for sorting and describing every occupation.
  • Job Bank — federal labour-market information website.
  • Hexagonal model — Holland's diagram in which adjacent types share more characteristics.

Worked Example — Reading a Holland Code

Jordan completes a RIASEC inventory and gets the top three codes SAE (Social–Artistic–Enterprising). What does this say? S dominant: Jordan is drawn to people-helping work — counselling, teaching, health professions, community service. A second: Jordan also wants creative self-expression — design, performance, content creation. E third: Jordan is comfortable leading and persuading. SAE combinations cluster in roles that involve people and creativity and influence: drama teacher, marketing/communications in a non-profit, youth program director, school counsellor, theatre director. Jordan's next step (Unit 2) is to use the NOC and Job Bank to pull labour-market details on three SAE-coded occupations, and to identify a low-stakes test such as volunteering at a youth program or joining the school drama production.

  • Treating one RIASEC code as the answer to "what should I be?" — it's a starting hypothesis.
  • Confusing "I like watching crime shows" with "I'm interested in forensic work" — TV interest and career interest are not the same.
  • Picking only the top letter and ignoring the second and third (the combination matters).
  • Comparing your code to a friend's and assuming one is "better." There is no best code; only fit.
  • Forgetting that interest must be combined with skill, opportunity, and values for a sound choice.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "use a variety of self-assessment tools and processes to identify their personal characteristics (e.g., interests, abilities, learning styles, work values, personality traits)."

🛠️ 1.3 Skills, Aptitudes & Multiple Intelligences
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Skills are what you can do. They differ from interests (what you like) and from aptitudes (your underlying capacity to learn a skill quickly). A grounded self-assessment names all three. A student might be interested in music (interest), have an ear for pitch (aptitude), and read sheet music (skill); another might be interested in music, have less natural ear, but be a disciplined practiser whose skill grows over time. Both have careers in music open to them — they just travel different paths.

The Ontario Skills Passport (OSP) is the Ministry's framework for talking about employability skills. It names nine essential skills — reading, writing, numeracy, oral communication, digital, problem solving, working with others, finding and using information, and continuous learning — and three work habits — working safely, working independently, and using initiative. Every occupation in the NOC is mapped to the OSP categories, which means you can compare your skills profile against the demands of a real job. The OSP is also the common language Ontario co-op employers use to give feedback.

Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences framework offers a complementary lens. Gardner argued that intelligence is not one thing measured by an IQ test but at least eight related capacities: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalistic. The framework was originally a theory of cognition, but career counsellors have adopted it because it gives students who have been told they "aren't smart" a way to see their actual strengths. A student strong in bodily-kinesthetic and interpersonal intelligence might thrive as a paramedic; a student strong in naturalistic and spatial intelligence might thrive as a landscape architect or wildlife biologist.

The most useful exercise is to make a skills inventory — a short list of three to five skills you can demonstrate, each with one piece of evidence and one external corroboration. "I can plan and run an event" backed by "I organised the Grade 9 grad dance" and "my advisor wrote 'showed strong initiative' on my Learning Skills report" carries far more weight than "I'm a leader." On a résumé, in a cover letter, and in an interview, evidence-backed skill statements consistently outperform generic claims, because employers know that anyone can claim to be a hard worker, but few can name what they did last Tuesday to prove it.

  • Skill — a learned, demonstrable ability.
  • Aptitude — underlying capacity to learn a skill quickly.
  • Hard skill — a specific, teachable, often measurable ability (coding, blood-pressure measurement, welding).
  • Soft skill / employability skill — transferable ability that helps you succeed across roles (communication, teamwork, problem solving).
  • Ontario Skills Passport (OSP) — Ministry of Education framework of 9 essential skills and 3 work habits.
  • Multiple Intelligences (Gardner) — framework of eight kinds of intelligence: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic.
  • Skills inventory — personal list of skills, each with evidence and corroboration.

Worked Example — Turning a Vague Claim Into Evidence

Weak: "I'm a strong communicator."
Better: "I have led classroom discussions and presentations."
Strong (OSP-style): "Oral communication — I planned and delivered a 5-minute presentation on the school's mental-health resources to a Grade 9 advisory group; the teacher wrote 'clear, well-organised, made eye contact, answered questions confidently' on my Learning Skills report." Notice the strong version names the skill (using OSP language), gives a specific example, and corroborates it with external feedback. In a résumé bullet this becomes: "Designed and delivered presentation to 28 Grade 9 students on accessing school mental-health supports (positive evaluation from advisory teacher, March 2024)." Employers can immediately picture what you did and how well you did it.

  • Confusing interest with skill ("I like baking" doesn't mean you can run a bakery yet).
  • Claiming soft skills without evidence ("I'm a great team player" + no example).
  • Ignoring hard skills you actually have — basic spreadsheet, video editing, food handling certificate — that are highly valued by employers.
  • Treating Multiple Intelligences as a fixed identity rather than strengths that can be developed.
  • Forgetting work habits — punctuality, reliability, organisation — which employers often rate as more important than technical skill in entry-level roles.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.1 — "identify their strengths and areas for improvement in essential employability skills (e.g., communication, teamwork, problem solving, digital skills)."

💎 1.4 Work Values & What Motivates You
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Values are the qualities that matter most to you in how you live and work. They are deeper than interests (which can shift with mood) and more durable than skills (which can be learned). A job that matches your interests but violates your values produces what career counsellors call value conflict — a slow, draining mismatch that no amount of competence can fix. A famous example: a high-paying corporate lawyer who values balance and family time eventually burns out, not because she dislikes law but because the 70-hour week violates a deeper value. The reverse is also true: people in modest-paying jobs whose values are honoured (helping, autonomy, learning) often report higher career satisfaction than peers earning twice as much in roles that don't fit.

Work values are usefully grouped into three families. Intrinsic values are about the work itself — challenge, learning, creativity, helping, autonomy, meaning. Extrinsic values are about the rewards the work produces — salary, status, security, benefits, recognition. Lifestyle values are about how the work fits into the rest of your life — location, hours, travel, flexibility, family time. There is no "right" mix. A student who ranks security and benefits highly is not less idealistic than one who ranks meaning highest; they are differently calibrated, and both calibrations deserve respect.

A common Grade 10 mistake is to absorb values from family or social media without testing them. "I want to be rich" usually means "I want financial security" — and the practical career implications of those two statements are very different. Likewise, "I want to be famous" usually means "I want to feel my work is recognised and matters" — a need that can be met in many quieter ways. The exercise of distinguishing surface wants from underlying values sharpens the rest of the planning process.

A useful technique is the values card sort: write 20–30 values on cards (helping others, security, creativity, autonomy, prestige, family, learning, fun, leadership, etc.), spread them out, and force yourself to keep only your top five. Then sort the top five by importance. Doing this once tells you something. Doing it twice — once at the start of Grade 10 and again at the end — often surprises students by what has stayed stable and what has shifted. Stable values are foundations to build a career around. Shifting values warrant another look at evidence; sometimes growth changes priorities, sometimes a value was inherited rather than tested. Either is useful information.

  • Value — a quality or principle a person treats as importantly worth pursuing.
  • Work value — value applied specifically to choices about work and career.
  • Intrinsic value — value found in the nature of the work itself (challenge, helping, learning, meaning).
  • Extrinsic value — value found in rewards the work produces (salary, status, security).
  • Lifestyle value — value about how work fits into life (hours, location, flexibility).
  • Values card sort — technique for identifying top values through forced choice.
  • Value conflict — situation where a job's daily demands violate a person's deeper values.

Worked Example — Reading a Values Profile

Diego, after a values card sort, ends with top five: helping others, autonomy, balance (family time), learning, integrity. The pattern says: he wants work where (1) the daily impact on people is visible, (2) he has some control over how he does his work, (3) he protects evenings/weekends, (4) the work itself keeps him growing, (5) he can sleep at night about how it operates. Implications: medicine (helping, learning, integrity) is possible if he chooses a specialty with controllable hours — family medicine, dermatology, pathology — and is cautious about specialties that don't (surgery's on-call demands may violate the balance value). Implications: high-finance roles that pay well but require 70-hour weeks would create value conflict. A non-medical option fitting the same profile: teaching with a part-time tutoring side practice (helping, autonomy, balance, learning, integrity). Notice that values eliminate options that look attractive on paper but would not actually fit, and they keep options open that span fields.

  • Adopting your parents' values without testing them — they may or may not still fit you.
  • Treating "I want money" as a final answer; ask what the money is for (security? freedom? status?).
  • Ignoring lifestyle values — many career regrets are about how a job ate the rest of life.
  • Assuming intrinsic values are nobler than extrinsic ones — both are legitimate.
  • Failing to update your values list as you grow — the list at 15 may not fit at 25.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "analyse the results of their self-assessment to identify factors they need to consider in planning career pathways."

🧠 1.5 Personality & Learning Styles
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Personality describes how you tend to engage with the world — your habitual patterns of thought, emotion, and behaviour. Two people with identical interests and equal skill can do the same job very differently because of personality. One nurse is energised by busy emergency-room shifts and a constantly changing patient list; another nurse with the same training prefers the steady relationships of a community clinic. Both are excellent at their work; their personalities have steered them into the version of the work that suits them.

The Big Five (sometimes called OCEAN) is the personality model with the strongest research backing. It measures five dimensions: Openness (curiosity vs. preference for the familiar), Conscientiousness (planning, organisation, persistence), Extraversion (energy from social contact vs. from solitude), Agreeableness (compassion, cooperation vs. competition, directness), and Neuroticism/Emotional Stability (tendency toward negative emotion vs. calm). Each is a spectrum, not a category. Research links Conscientiousness to job performance across nearly every field — students who score high on this dimension tend to do well in school, on co-op placements, and in their first jobs.

Other personality frameworks you may meet — the Myers-Briggs (MBTI), DISC, Enneagram — are popular in workplaces but have weaker research support than the Big Five. They can still be useful conversation-starters about preferences, but they should not be treated as scientific. Whatever framework you use, the same principle applies: results are hypotheses to test, not labels that fix you. Personality is also more stable than mood — a quiet day doesn't make you "introverted overall." Across many situations and over months and years, the pattern emerges.

Learning styles are related but different. The VARK framework (Visual, Auditory, Read/write, Kinesthetic) describes how you most easily take in new information. Recent research suggests that matching teaching to a single learning style does not reliably boost achievement — but knowing your preference still helps you study smarter. If you are kinesthetic, you might draw concept maps and re-explain ideas aloud while pacing; if you are read/write, you might re-summarise notes in your own words. The most reliable finding across decades of educational research is that active studying (self-testing, spaced practice, elaboration) outperforms passive studying (re-reading, highlighting) regardless of preferred style. The Ministry curriculum emphasises understanding your preferences so you can play to your strengths in school, work, and the community.

  • Personality — habitual patterns of thought, emotion, and behaviour across situations.
  • Big Five (OCEAN) — Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.
  • Trait spectrum — personality dimensions are continuous, not either/or.
  • Conscientiousness — the personality dimension most strongly linked to job performance.
  • Introversion / extraversion — where you draw energy from (solitude vs. social contact).
  • VARK — model of learning preferences: Visual, Auditory, Read/write, Kinesthetic.
  • Active studying — self-testing, spaced practice, elaborative rehearsal (outperforms passive re-reading).

Worked Example — Personality, Learning Style, and Day-to-Day Choices

Priya scores high on Conscientiousness and Openness, moderate on Extraversion, high on Agreeableness, low on Neuroticism. She also prefers visual and read/write learning. What does this profile suggest day-to-day? For school: she should write out her own summary notes (read/write) with concept maps (visual), set a weekly planner (conscientiousness in action), and ask for explanations when something is unclear (openness). For group work: her agreeableness will make her tempted to take on too much; she should practise saying "I can do these two pieces, who's on the third?" For career hypotheses: high Openness and Conscientiousness together is a profile common in research and design roles; moderate Extraversion means she is fine with people but doesn't need the constant social input of, say, retail or front-line sales; high Agreeableness fits helping professions, education, healthcare. She is using personality results to refine, not to label.

  • Treating a personality result as fixed identity rather than tendency.
  • Using MBTI as if it had the research backing of the Big Five (it doesn't).
  • Confusing shy/quiet with introverted — introverts can be socially skilled; shyness is anxiety.
  • Believing that you must be taught in your preferred learning style — research says active studying matters more.
  • Forgetting that conscientiousness — boring as it sounds — is the single trait that most reliably predicts work success.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "demonstrate an understanding of how their personal characteristics may affect their success in school, work, and the community."

🌱 1.6 Character, Growth Mindset & Habits of Success
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Character is the bundle of habits — moral, social, and intellectual — that you practise often enough that they become who you are. The Ministry curriculum treats character as malleable, not fixed: students are expected to identify character traits associated with success and to demonstrate the ones they want to develop. Common traits named on Ontario's Learning Skills and Work Habits report — responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation — are the same traits employers prize and the same traits universities cite when explaining why first-year students succeed or struggle.

The psychologist Carol Dweck distinguishes a fixed mindset — the belief that ability is innate and cannot be much changed — from a growth mindset — the belief that ability is developed through effort, strategy, and feedback. Decades of research find that students with a growth mindset persist longer through difficulty, recover better from setbacks, and end up higher-achieving over time. Crucially, mindset is itself learnable. The shift is from internal talk like "I'm not a math person" to "I haven't mastered this kind of problem yet" — the small word yet reframes ability from verdict to in-progress.

Angela Duckworth's research on grit — sustained passion and perseverance for long-term goals — extends Dweck's work. Grit is less about working harder in the moment and more about staying engaged with a chosen direction across years. Grittiness is not stubbornness; it includes the willingness to revise tactics while keeping the larger goal. Grit research is best read alongside studies that warn against celebrating endurance for its own sake — sometimes the right call is to change direction, and persistence in a poor-fit pathway becomes its own problem. The growth-mindset move is to ask, "what evidence would change my mind about this direction?" — and then watch for it.

Day-to-day, character shows up in small habits: arriving on time, doing what you said you would do, listening before speaking, asking for help when stuck, telling the truth in difficult situations, and being kind to the new person. None of these are heroic; all of them compound. Employers who supervise co-op students consistently report that the difference between a strong and a weak placement is rarely talent — it is whether the student showed up reliably, took initiative on small problems, and asked thoughtful questions. The Ministry's Learning Skills are essentially a school-based scaffold for these professional habits, which is why the Career Studies course returns to them so often.

  • Character — the bundle of habits that defines how a person consistently acts.
  • Fixed mindset — belief that ability is innate and largely unchangeable.
  • Growth mindset — belief that ability is developed through effort, strategy, and feedback.
  • Grit — sustained passion and perseverance toward long-term goals (Duckworth).
  • Learning Skills (Ontario) — six habits reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation.
  • Resilience — capacity to recover from setbacks and adapt under stress.
  • Integrity — consistency between values, words, and actions, especially when no one is watching.

Worked Example — Fixed vs. Growth Mindset Self-Talk

Tanya bombs her first Grade 10 math test, dropping from her Grade 9 average. Two responses:

Fixed-mindset self-talk: "I'm just not a math person. Grade 10 math is too hard. I should pick courses without math next semester." Consequence: the bad mark becomes a verdict; effort drops; the next test confirms the verdict.

Growth-mindset self-talk: "I haven't mastered factoring yet. What specifically did I get wrong on this test? Was it the concept (didn't understand factoring) or the conditions (got tired, ran out of time, did the wrong question)? What study strategy will I try differently before the next test?" Consequence: the bad mark becomes diagnostic information; effort is redirected, not withdrawn; the next test gives a meaningful comparison. Notice that growth mindset is not "everything will be fine if I think positively" — it is a specific, evidence-collecting response to a setback.

  • Confusing growth mindset with toxic positivity ("everything will work out!") — it is a method, not a slogan.
  • Praising students for "being smart" rather than for effort and strategy — this entrenches fixed mindset.
  • Using "grit" to justify staying in a poor-fit path long after evidence says to revise.
  • Treating Learning Skills as separate from career skills — they are the same skills under different names.
  • Ignoring the role of small, consistent habits — character is built one small choice at a time.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "describe character traits and habits of mind that are associated with success (e.g., responsibility, integrity, perseverance, growth mindset)."
