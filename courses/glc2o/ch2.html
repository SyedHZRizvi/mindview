🌐 2.1 Researching Careers — Sources & Method
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Career research is structured information-gathering, not random Googling. A Grade 10 student typing "best jobs 2026" into a search engine gets clickbait. A Grade 10 student following a research process produces an evidence-based summary they can actually use. The Ministry curriculum makes this explicit: students must demonstrate a research process — identify a focus, plan, gather from multiple sources, evaluate, synthesise, and cite — and apply it to careers that interest them.

Step one is focus. "Tell me everything about careers" is too broad to research. "What does an early childhood educator (ECE) do in Ontario, what training is required, what does it pay, and is demand growing?" is researchable. A focused question forces specific evidence. Step two is source selection. The most reliable Canadian sources for career research are public, free, and updated regularly: the federal Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca) with the Canadian National Occupational Classification (NOC) descriptions, Ontario's Ontario Skills Passport (skills.edu.gov.on.ca), Statistics Canada for labour-market data, professional-association websites for entry requirements, and direct interviews with people in the field. Step three is triangulation — using at least three independent sources before drawing conclusions. Step four is citation, so you (or your teacher) can re-check anything you wrote.

The National Occupational Classification (NOC) is the federal system that gives every Canadian occupation a code and a standardised description. Each NOC entry tells you the typical duties, the educational/training pathway, the average wage, and the projected demand. Ontario uses NOC codes inside Job Bank's labour-market tools, so learning to navigate NOC means you can move quickly between "the job" and "the data about the job." Most students who struggle with career research never learn this one-step lookup; learning it cuts an hour off every later research task.

An informational interview — a 15-minute conversation with someone actually doing the work — is the most under-used Grade 10 research tool. Job descriptions describe duties on paper; informational interviews tell you what the work feels like on Tuesday morning. A good interview asks three things: (1) walk me through a typical day; (2) what surprised you about this work when you started; (3) what would you tell a Grade 10 student considering this field? Five interviews across a semester is enough to spot patterns. Most working adults are flattered to be asked and remember being your age; the asking is a skill in itself.

  • Career research — structured information-gathering about an occupation or field.
  • Research focus — a specific researchable question (not "tell me about careers").
  • Triangulation — confirming a finding across three or more independent sources.
  • National Occupational Classification (NOC) — Canadian federal system of occupation codes and descriptions.
  • Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca) — federal labour-market website with wage, outlook, and posting data.
  • Ontario Skills Passport (OSP) — Ministry framework of essential skills tied to NOC occupations.
  • Informational interview — short, fact-finding conversation with someone in a field of interest.

Worked Example — One Career, Three Sources

Question: "Is becoming a paramedic in Ontario a strong choice for someone with SR Holland code, conscientiousness strength, and a value for helping?"

Source 1 (Job Bank / NOC 32102 Paramedical Occupations): typical duties — assess patients, administer emergency care, transport; median wage in Ontario approximately $43–$50/hour for Primary Care Paramedic (varies by region and year); outlook rated "good" or "moderate" in most ON regions. Source 2 (Ontario Paramedic Association & college program pages, e.g., Centennial College PCP): entry pathway — two-year Primary Care Paramedic college program → Advanced Emergency Medical Care Assistant certification → provincial registration; secondary-school prerequisites include Grade 12 English (ENG4C/U), Biology, and a vocational-physical assessment. Source 3 (informational interview with a working PCP): "Best part — every shift is different and we help people on their worst day. Hardest part — shift work, vicarious trauma, regular CPR/recert training. Tell a Grade 10 student to volunteer with St John Ambulance now to test the fit." Synthesis: strong fit for the profile, but the lifestyle (shift work, emotional load) needs honest weighing — recommend a job-shadow in Grade 11.

  • Relying on a single source (especially a single ranked-list article).
  • Confusing wage averages with starting salaries — entry pay is usually much lower.
  • Treating American data as Canadian — pathways and licensing differ.
  • Skipping informational interviews because they feel awkward; they are the single most useful step.
  • Not citing sources, so the research can't be re-checked or updated later.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "demonstrate an understanding of how to use a research process for career exploration (e.g., identifying a focus, planning the research, gathering information from multiple sources, evaluating and synthesising findings)."

📈 2.2 Labour Market Trends in Ontario & Canada
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The labour market is the set of arrangements through which workers and employers find each other. It is shaped by technology, demographics, immigration, public policy, global trade, and large shocks (recessions, pandemics, climate transition). For Grade 10 planning, you don't need to forecast the next decade with precision; you need to read current trends well enough to avoid betting your time and money on a pathway that the evidence already suggests is shrinking.

Canadian labour-market data is published by Statistics Canada (the Labour Force Survey) and synthesised by Employment and Social Development Canada through the Canadian Occupational Projection System (COPS), which forecasts each NOC occupation as either "labour shortage," "balanced," or "surplus" over the next decade. Ontario's Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development publishes regional labour-market outlooks each year. The common pattern across recent reports: chronic shortages in skilled trades (electricians, plumbers, welders, industrial mechanics), health care (nursing, personal-support workers, paramedics, physicians outside large urban centres), early childhood education, and selected STEM fields (cybersecurity, data analytics, cleantech engineering); growing demand in green economy roles tied to climate transition; and surplus or stagnation in some long-popular university fields (general business, communications, undifferentiated arts) without specialised skills.

A handful of meta-trends shape the next decade. Demographic ageing means a wave of retirements, especially in trades, public service, and health care — opportunities for younger workers who train into those fields. Automation and AI will reshape clerical, retail, and some white-collar work; jobs that involve judgment, care, persuasion, dexterity, or applied creativity are harder to automate. Climate transition is creating new occupations (energy auditors, EV-charging electricians, climate-adaptation planners, sustainable-construction tradespeople). Hybrid and remote work have settled into a permanent pattern in some sectors (knowledge work) and not in others (trades, healthcare, hospitality). The Canadian and Ontario governments project most net job growth this decade in healthcare, the skilled trades, STEM, and services — not in fields students often default to.

A grounded reading of trends keeps two things in tension. On one hand, do not bet on a shrinking pathway (e.g., "I'll be a typist" was a real career in 1985; it isn't a planning target today). On the other hand, do not chase whatever appears hot this month — by the time a "hot field" is in headlines, supply is already chasing it. The Ministry curriculum expectation is to identify trends and explain "their potential implications" — which means using trends to think probabilistically (this pathway looks more durable, this one is risky), not deterministically (this pathway is guaranteed). Combining a clear-eyed read of trends with strong self-knowledge produces choices that are both realistic and authentic.

  • Labour market — the arrangement through which workers and employers exchange labour for wages.
  • Statistics Canada — federal statistical agency; publisher of the Labour Force Survey.
  • Canadian Occupational Projection System (COPS) — federal 10-year forecast of each NOC occupation.
  • Labour shortage / balanced / surplus — COPS classification of how supply matches demand.
  • Demographic ageing — the proportion of older workers retiring; creates openings for replacements.
  • Automation — replacement of human tasks by machines and software.
  • Green economy — sectors driven by climate-transition needs (renewables, efficiency, adaptation).
  • Hybrid / remote work — work arrangements partly or wholly outside a central office.

Worked Example — Reading a COPS Outlook

A Grade 10 student is comparing two pathways: electrician (NOC 72200) versus journalist (NOC 51113). What does the COPS data suggest, roughly?

Electrician: COPS has flagged construction electricians as in chronic shortage across most provinces. Drivers: demographic ageing (large retirement wave), EV charging build-out, residential construction. Implication: training paths into the trade (OYAP, apprenticeship) lead to strong demand and median wages typically $35–$50/hour in Ontario for journeypersons. Journalist: COPS has flagged journalists as in surplus in many regions due to advertising shifting to social platforms, layoffs at major outlets, and rising freelance share. Implication: journalism remains a real career path, but it requires specialised skills (data journalism, video, niche expertise) and tolerance for income variability. A student who values stability and physical work and is interest-coded R would do well with the trade. A student who values writing/communication and is willing to specialise and freelance can still pursue journalism, but should plan more deliberately. Either choice is legitimate; the data informs the planning, it does not dictate it.

  • Reading one news headline as the entire labour-market picture.
  • Ignoring regional variation — Toronto, Sudbury, and Windsor have very different markets.
  • Treating "hot job lists" as forecasts — they are usually rear-view summaries.
  • Believing automation will eliminate "all" of any sector; usually it changes the tasks, not the field.
  • Forgetting that personal fit (Unit 1) matters more than chasing the highest-growth occupation.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "identify current trends in the local and global labour market and explain their potential implications for career planning."

🎓 2.3 Postsecondary Destinations — Apprenticeship, College, University, Workplace
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Ontario's secondary system was redesigned in 2003–06 around four postsecondary destinations: apprenticeship, college, community living, university, and the workplace. (The official list often groups these as five — apprenticeship, college, university, workplace, community living.) The point is that university is one option among several, not a default. A grounded Grade 10 student understands what each pathway leads to, what each costs in time and money, and which secondary courses keep each pathway open.

Apprenticeship is a paid combination of on-the-job training (about 80–90%) and in-class instruction (about 10–20%), leading to a Certificate of Qualification in a recognised trade. Apprenticeships in Ontario run two to five years depending on trade, are administered through the Skilled Trades Ontario agency, and start with finding a sponsor employer. Trades fall into four sectors: construction (electrician, carpenter, plumber), industrial (millwright, tool-and-die maker), motive power (automotive technician, truck/coach), and service (cook, hairstylist, child development practitioner). Apprenticeships are paid from day one and produce strong wages; the Canadian Federation of Independent Business estimates Ontario will see large skilled-trade shortages through the 2030s.

College programs in Ontario are typically one to three years, applied/career-focused, and offered at one of 24 public colleges (e.g., Seneca, Centennial, George Brown, Mohawk, Conestoga, Algonquin, Fanshawe). Credentials include certificates (1 year), diplomas (2 years), advanced diplomas (3 years), graduate certificates (post-bachelor's), and, increasingly, applied bachelor's degrees. Tuition is generally $4,000–$8,000/year for domestic students. College programs include nursing, paramedic, ECE, business, IT, journalism, culinary, dental hygiene, architectural technology, and many more. Many programs have co-op or paid placement.

University programs in Ontario are typically three to four years for an undergraduate degree at one of 23 publicly funded universities (e.g., Toronto, Waterloo, McMaster, Queen's, Western, Ottawa, York, Carleton, Guelph, McGill is in Quebec, etc.). Tuition is generally $7,000–$14,000/year for domestic Ontario students depending on program (engineering, business, and some health programs cost more). Most university degrees focus on academic disciplines and produce graduates equipped for further study, professional school (medicine, law), or knowledge-economy roles. Workplace is direct entry to employment after secondary school. Plenty of Ontario adults built strong careers this way, often combined with employer-paid training or a later credential. Community living recognises that for some students with significant intellectual disabilities, the most appropriate destination is supported community participation, often with the help of agencies like Community Living Ontario.

  • Pathway / postsecondary destination — the route a student plans to follow after Grade 12.
  • Apprenticeship — paid on-the-job training (80–90%) + in-class instruction (10–20%) → Certificate of Qualification.
  • Skilled Trades Ontario — the provincial regulator for trades and apprenticeships.
  • College credential — certificate (1y), diploma (2y), advanced diploma (3y), graduate certificate, applied degree.
  • Undergraduate degree — university credential, typically 3–4 years (some longer).
  • OUAC — Ontario Universities Application Centre.
  • OCAS — Ontario College Application Service.

Worked Example — Same Career, Four Possible Routes

Aisha wants to work in healthcare. What routes does Ontario offer? Apprenticeship route: Personal Support Worker (PSW) certificate (~25–35 weeks at a community college) → community-care or long-term-care roles. College route: two-year Practical Nursing diploma → Registered Practical Nurse (RPN); or four-year collaborative BScN with a university → Registered Nurse (RN). University route: four-year BScN → RN; or a science degree → medical school (4 years + 2–7 years residency) → MD/physician. Workplace route: entry-level positions in long-term care while completing PSW or other certifications in parallel. Each pathway is real, each leads to nursing/healthcare work, each has very different time and cost. Notice that wage and time-to-credential trade off against each other; there is no single "best" path, only the path that fits one student's profile.

  • Assuming "university is best" — the best pathway is the one that fits the student.
  • Treating apprenticeship as "for students who can't do school" — most trades require strong literacy, numeracy, and digital skills.
  • Ignoring the time-and-cost differences between credentials.
  • Forgetting that mid-career switches between destinations are common — many adults blend college, work, and university across a lifetime.
  • Not checking secondary-course prerequisites — Grade 12 U vs. C designations matter for entry to university vs. college.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "describe a variety of postsecondary learning destinations (apprenticeship, college, community living, university, workplace) and the requirements and entry points for each."

🔧 2.4 OYAP, Skilled Trades & Specialist High Skills Major
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program (OYAP) lets you start an apprenticeship while you are still in high school, earning secondary-school credits at the same time. You attend school as usual for part of the week and work for a registered employer-sponsor for the other part. Hours worked count toward your apprenticeship hours, so you finish your trade certification sooner — sometimes by years. OYAP placements are typically Grade 11 or Grade 12 and require a co-op placement plus an OYAP registration. It is one of the most under-used opportunities in Ontario secondary school.

The skilled trades in Ontario are organised into four sectors. Construction includes electrician, plumber, carpenter, refrigeration/AC mechanic, sheet-metal worker, ironworker, sprinkler/fire-protection installer, mason. Industrial includes millwright, industrial mechanic, machinist, tool-and-die maker, welder, instrumentation technician. Motive power includes automotive service technician, truck-and-coach technician, motorcycle technician, aircraft maintenance, heavy-equipment operator. Service includes cook, baker, hairstylist, child-development practitioner, horticultural technician, arborist. Trades require strong literacy and numeracy (math is unavoidable; trig and basic algebra are everyday tools), strong digital skills (estimating software, CAD, diagnostic equipment), and consistent work habits. The pay is competitive — journeyperson electricians in Ontario typically earn over $80,000–$100,000/year — and many trades offer entrepreneurship pathways (starting your own shop after gaining experience).

Specialist High Skills Major (SHSM) is a Ministry-approved bundle that lets Grade 11 and 12 students focus a portion of their secondary studies on a sector while earning their OSSD. Sectors include construction, hospitality and tourism, manufacturing, health and wellness, transportation, energy, and many more — roughly 19 sectors province-wide. An SHSM student takes a specified set of credits, completes sector-recognised certifications (CPR, WHMIS, customer service, etc.), and graduates with a red seal on their OSSD signalling sector readiness. SHSM students remain eligible for any postsecondary destination but enter it with a head-start of sector-specific evidence on a résumé.

A frequent Grade 10 mistake is to think the trades are an alternative for students who can't do school. That story is two decades out of date. Most modern trades require Grade 12 math at the C or U level, the ability to read codes and regulations, the ability to use complex diagnostic software, and ongoing certification updates. Ontario faces a structural shortage of skilled trades and a wave of trade retirements; this is the right decade to consider OYAP, SHSM-Construction, SHSM-Manufacturing, or a co-op placement that points toward a trade. Begin by talking to your school's OYAP coordinator, guidance counsellor, or co-op teacher.

  • OYAP — Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program; starts apprenticeship hours while still in secondary school.
  • Skilled trade — a regulated occupation requiring formal training and certification.
  • Apprentice / journeyperson — apprentice is in training; journeyperson holds the Certificate of Qualification.
  • Compulsory trade — trade legally requiring certification (e.g., electrician, plumber, hairstylist).
  • Voluntary trade — trade where certification is not legally required but is professionally valuable.
  • Red Seal — interprovincial standard certifying trade competence; Red Seal trades let you work across Canada.
  • Specialist High Skills Major (SHSM) — Ministry-approved sector-focused bundle of credits and certifications.

Worked Example — Building an OYAP Pathway

Lucas is in Grade 10, interest-coded R (Realistic), strong in math, enjoys hands-on work, lives near a town with several construction firms. His Grade 10–12 plan: Grade 10: Career Studies, basic-skills certifications through SHSM-Construction prep (WHMIS, fall-protection orientation), math at the academic level. Grade 11: Co-op with a local electrical contractor (4 credits); register as an OYAP apprentice in the electrician trade; SHSM courses including Manufacturing/Construction Tech. Grade 12: Continue OYAP placement, complete SHSM certifications, finish Grade 12 college-level Math (MAP4C or workplace-equivalent), and write the entrance exam for the apprenticeship in-school portion. Outcome: Lucas leaves Grade 12 with ~2,000 apprenticeship hours already on the books, an OSSD with the SHSM red seal, and a sponsoring employer — saving 1–2 years toward his Certificate of Qualification compared to a peer who starts from scratch after Grade 12. The same template works for plumbing, carpentry, automotive, machinist, child-development practitioner, and many more trades.

  • Believing trades are "easier" than university — they require different skills, not lesser ones.
  • Waiting until Grade 12 to ask about OYAP — earlier conversations open more options.
  • Not checking which trades are compulsory vs. voluntary in Ontario.
  • Forgetting that math at Grade 11 and 12 (especially trig) is needed for most construction trades.
  • Underestimating the entrepreneurship route — many tradespeople own their own shops within ten years.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "describe a variety of postsecondary learning destinations (apprenticeship, college, community living, university, workplace) and the requirements and entry points for each."

🚀 2.5 Entrepreneurship & Self-Employment
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Entrepreneurship is the process of creating value by identifying a need, designing something to meet it, and getting paid to deliver it. About 16% of Canadian workers are self-employed (Statistics Canada Labour Force Survey, recent years), and many more do part-time freelance or side-business work. Entrepreneurship is not a single occupation; it is a mode of working that can apply to almost any field — a self-employed electrician, a freelance graphic designer, a tutoring business, a food-truck operator, an online retailer, a software-as-a-service founder. Career Studies treats entrepreneurship as one legitimate pathway that students should at least consider.

A productive way to think about the question is to separate two things: the activity (you provide a product or service to customers) and the structure (you are the legal owner of the business, not an employee). The activity can be tested very young — a tutoring business, a lawn-care service, an Etsy storefront — without much formal structure. As businesses grow, structure becomes important: choosing a sole proprietorship, partnership, or incorporated company; registering with the province; collecting and remitting HST; understanding basic bookkeeping; carrying liability insurance.

The "Lean Startup" idea (Eric Ries) and the related Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder) are common frameworks for thinking through a small business. The Canvas asks nine questions on one page: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, cost structure. Filling it in for a real idea — even a hypothetical Grade 10 idea — exposes the gaps that informal thinking hides. Most start-ups fail because of weak market need or weak unit economics; the Canvas surfaces both before money is spent.

Entrepreneurship has real advantages — autonomy, upside, the chance to build something — and real costs: uneven income, long hours in the early years, the requirement to be both maker and manager, no automatic benefits or pension. The personality profile that suits self-employment usually combines comfort with uncertainty, strong self-regulation, and the willingness to do the boring administrative work of running a business as well as the exciting craft work. Ontario supports young entrepreneurs through the Summer Company program (provincial grants and mentorship for students aged 15–29 starting a summer business), Futurpreneur Canada (start-up loans and mentorship), and many municipal small-business enterprise centres. Even a small tested experience — a tutoring business or a weekend-market booth — produces evidence that informs the choice between employment and self-employment later.

  • Entrepreneur — a person who creates and runs their own business venture.
  • Sole proprietorship — simplest business form; owner and business are legally the same.
  • Incorporation — legally separating the business from the owner; reduces personal liability.
  • Business model — the way a business creates, delivers, and captures value.
  • Lean Startup / Build-Measure-Learn — Ries's approach to validating ideas with small experiments before scaling.
  • Business Model Canvas — one-page tool for sketching a business across nine building blocks.
  • HST — Harmonised Sales Tax; once sales exceed $30,000/year, registration and remittance are required.
  • Summer Company / Futurpreneur Canada — youth-entrepreneurship support programs.

Worked Example — A Grade 10 Business Model Canvas

Idea: weekend tutoring for Grade 7–8 students in math, run by a Grade 10 student. Customer segment: Grade 7–8 families in the local neighbourhood, weak in math, willing to pay $20–$30/hour. Value proposition: a slightly older student who remembers the curriculum, offers flexible weekend sessions in coffee shops/online, costs much less than a private tutoring centre. Channels: printed flyers in two community centres + Facebook neighbourhood group + word-of-mouth. Customer relationships: one-on-one, weekly recurring. Revenue streams: hourly rate $25; bundle of 4 sessions for $90. Key resources: curriculum knowledge, a quiet meeting space, basic supplies. Key activities: session prep, marketing, scheduling. Key partners: family members (transportation), school teacher (testimonial). Cost structure: minimal — printing, transit, materials. Sanity check: if I book 4 sessions/week × 30 weeks × $25 = $3,000/year (net of ~$300 costs). That is a realistic Grade-10 side business, and it tests teaching, marketing, and customer-service skills that transfer to many later careers.

  • Treating entrepreneurship as exclusively for "founders" with big tech ideas — most Canadian entrepreneurs run small local businesses.
  • Underestimating the administrative side of running a business — invoicing, taxes, scheduling.
  • Not testing a small version of an idea before committing serious time or money.
  • Confusing income (revenue) with profit (revenue minus costs).
  • Not registering once required — collecting HST without remitting it is a serious problem.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "describe the demands and rewards of selected occupations they have researched."

🤝 2.6 Community Involvement & the 40-Hour Requirement
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every Ontario student must complete 40 hours of community involvement as part of the Ontario Secondary School Diploma (OSSD). The Ministry's stated goal is to encourage students to develop awareness and understanding of civic responsibility and of the role they can play in their communities. In practice, the 40 hours often functions as a structured first contact with the world of work — the placement where many students discover an interest, a skill, or a values match they hadn't anticipated.

Hours can begin in the summer after Grade 8 and must be complete before graduation; they are tracked in the school's online portal and documented on a Ministry form signed by the supervising organisation. Approved activities include volunteering at a not-for-profit, helping in a community organisation (food bank, library, community centre, hospital), assisting at a school event (running concessions at sports games, organising a graduation), tutoring, helping coach a younger team, environmental clean-ups, and many more. Not approved: anything that displaces a paid worker (working a cash register at a for-profit business), business activities that earn wages or commission, court-ordered service, or anything during instructional school hours.

Done strategically, the 40 hours can launch a career exploration. Choose placements that connect to your Holland code, the OSP skills you want to develop, and an industry you want to test. A student exploring healthcare might volunteer at a long-term-care home; one exploring education might tutor; one exploring environment might join a watershed-cleanup group; one exploring trades might help on a Habitat for Humanity build. Track what you did in enough detail that you can later put it on a résumé and discuss it in a postsecondary application. A strong résumé bullet from a placement reads: "Volunteer reading tutor, Toronto Public Library (Sep 2024 – Jun 2025, 60 hours): supported four Grade 2 students in weekly one-on-one reading sessions; coordinated with branch librarian to track progress." That bullet conveys an OSP skill (oral communication, working with others), evidence (60 hours, four students), and an outcome (progress tracking).

Volunteering also produces references. Postsecondary applications, scholarship applications, and first jobs all ask for references; a placement supervisor who has seen you work consistently for six months is often a stronger reference than a teacher who has had you in class. Ask early, give your reference time to write, and follow up to thank them. The whole habit — choosing meaningful placements, doing them well, building references — is part of the larger career-development competence the curriculum aims to develop.

  • Community involvement — unpaid, voluntary activity that benefits a community organisation or cause.
  • 40-hour requirement — Ontario OSSD minimum; can be completed any time between summer after Grade 8 and graduation.
  • Volunteering — giving time without expectation of payment.
  • Civic engagement — participating in the life of a community as a citizen.
  • Reference — a person who can speak to your character and work in a recommendation.
  • Approved / non-approved activity — Ministry-defined eligibility for the 40 hours.

Worked Example — Designing a 40-Hour Plan

Sasha is interest-coded SAI (Social–Artistic–Investigative) and is exploring health professions and education. Her Grade 9–11 plan: Summer after Grade 9 (10 hours): Junior camp leader at the local community centre — practises working with younger children, public-facing role. Grade 10 (12 hours): Volunteer at the long-term-care home recreation programs — tests interest in health, builds gentleness/communication skill. Grade 11 summer (12 hours): Library reading tutor — tests teaching role. Grade 11 fall (10 hours): Helps run school open-house tours — public-speaking practice. Total: 44 hours (4 over the minimum), across four placements, with one supervisor in each who can serve as a reference. Each placement deliberately tested a different OSP skill, and each fed into the Unit-3 portfolio (résumé bullets, story bank for interviews). The whole plan was costless and added to Sasha's clarity about which postsecondary destination fits.

  • Leaving the 40 hours to the last semester before graduation — late panic, fewer choices.
  • Picking placements purely by convenience instead of strategically by career fit.
  • Not getting the Ministry form signed in real time — recreating hours from memory is unreliable.
  • Doing activities that don't count (paid retail, court-ordered, school instructional time) — confirm with guidance counsellor first.
  • Failing to maintain the relationship with placement supervisors — they make excellent references.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "describe a variety of options for community involvement and the requirements of the Ontario Student Diploma 40-hour community-involvement requirement."
