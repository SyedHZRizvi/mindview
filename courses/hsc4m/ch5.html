❓ 5.1 The Social-Science Inquiry Process — From Topic to Question
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Social-science inquiry is a disciplined, ethical, evidence-based way of asking questions about human behaviour and cultural patterns. The Ontario curriculum frames it as a recursive process with several phases: exploring topics, formulating research questions, gathering evidence, interpreting and analyzing, evaluating and drawing conclusions, and communicating findings with proper citation. The process is recursive because at any point you can — and should — loop back. A surprising finding may force you to refine the research question; a gap in sources may force you to redefine the scope. Strong researchers do not march in a straight line from topic to conclusion; they zigzag, then communicate the result as if it were always linear.

The biggest single skill in inquiry is formulating a good research question. A topic ("Indigenous languages in Canada") is not a research question; it is an entire field. A research question is narrower, specific, and answerable with evidence within your time and resource budget. Good questions are specific (about what, where, when), focused (one main thing, not five), open (not yes-or-no), and researchable (you can actually find evidence). "Why is Indigenous-language revitalization important?" is too broad. "How has the Tsi Tyónnheht Onkwawén:na adult-immersion program in Tyendinaga contributed to Mohawk-language revitalization between 2005 and 2025, and what conditions have shaped its outcomes?" — that is a question you could actually research with available sources.

Several question-types map to different research methods. Descriptive questions (What is the structure of...? How many...?) call for surveys, content analysis, descriptive ethnography. Comparative questions (How does X differ from Y?) call for systematic comparison across two or more cases. Causal questions (What produces...? Why does X happen?) call for experimental or quasi-experimental design (rare in cultural research) or strong process-tracing across cases. Interpretive questions (What does X mean to participants?) call for ethnographic and interview methods. Normative questions (Should we...? Is it just to...?) are philosophical rather than empirical and call for argumentative analysis. Knowing what kind of question you are asking lets you pick the right method and the right evidence.

Choosing a topic deserves attention too. The best HSC4M Independent Study Project (ISP) topics are personally engaging (you will spend many hours with this; pick something you want to know more about), tractable (sources exist, scope is manageable), significant (the question matters for understanding culture), and ethical (you can do the work without harming participants, especially if doing fieldwork). When the topic touches Indigenous communities, the principle of OCAP — Ownership, Control, Access, Possession — set by the First Nations Information Governance Centre, requires that research be done with communities, not on them. Strong ISP proposals will identify the topic, formulate a focused research question, justify why it matters, identify methods, and commit to ethical guidelines — usually in a single 1–2 page proposal that the teacher will give feedback on before the major work begins.

  • Research question — focused, specific, answerable question that drives inquiry.
  • Hypothesis — proposed answer to a research question, to be tested by evidence.
  • Variable — measurable factor that can vary (age, language proficiency, attitude).
  • Operationalization — defining a concept so it can be measured (turning "wellbeing" into specific indicators).
  • Inductive reasoning — building generalisations from specific observations.
  • Deductive reasoning — testing specific predictions derived from general theory.
  • OCAP principles — First Nations Information Governance Centre's Ownership, Control, Access, Possession framework for research with Indigenous communities.

Worked Example — Refining a Topic into a Research Question

Start with a topic that interests you: "K-pop fandom." That is interesting but unresearchable as a Grade 12 ISP. Narrow it. First refinement: "K-pop fandom in Canada" — still too broad. Second: "Tamil-Canadian K-pop fandom" — getting somewhere, but unclear what question you are asking. Third: "How does participation in K-pop fan communities shape the cultural identity of second-generation Tamil-Canadian adolescents in the GTA?" — this is a focused, interpretive research question. To answer it you would need: a clear concept of cultural identity (operationalise: ethnic self-identification, language use at home, friendship networks); a sample of Tamil-Canadian K-pop fans (perhaps 8–12 for in-depth interviews); semi-structured interviews; and a comparison sample (non-fans?) or a longitudinal sense (before/during/after fan involvement). The question is specific, focused, answerable with feasible methods, and culturally and theoretically significant — it speaks to debates about diasporic identity, mediated identity, and the role of transnational popular culture. A strong ISP proposal would also address ethics: informed consent, anonymity, and the question of whether the researcher is herself part of the fan community (insider/outsider position). That is the level of disciplined question-formulation HSC4M's ISP demands. Iterating from "topic I find interesting" to "focused research question I can answer" is the single most important inquiry skill the course teaches.

  • Confusing topic with research question (a topic is a field; a question is what you ask about it).
  • Asking yes-or-no questions that close inquiry rather than open it.
  • Picking a question so broad that no available sources will address it satisfactorily.
  • Picking a question too narrow to sustain a 1500–2000-word research paper.
  • Skipping the proposal stage and discovering the question is unanswerable after weeks of work.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "formulate effective questions to guide their research on world cultures."

📋 5.2 Methods — Ethnography, Interview, Survey, Content Analysis
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Method choice should flow from the research question. The five most commonly-used social-science methods for cultural inquiry are ethnographic fieldwork, interviews, surveys, content analysis, and secondary-source / archival research. Each has strengths and limits. Strong HSC4M projects often combine two — for instance, interviews with content analysis — to triangulate findings.

Ethnographic fieldwork is the signature method of cultural anthropology. The researcher spends extended time (weeks to years) in a community, participating in daily life while observing, learning the language, building relationships, taking detailed field notes. Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) is the classic example. The strength is depth and contextual richness — you understand practices from the inside, not just as external behaviour but as meaningful action. The limit is time and scope: you cannot ethnograph at scale; one community is not the whole world. For Grade 12 ISP work, "ethnographic" usually means a small-scale, ethically-bounded mini-ethnography — perhaps 5 hours of observation at a community event, with consent and proper protocols.

Interviews are conversations with research participants, designed to elicit perspectives the researcher could not obtain from observation alone. Structured interviews ask the same questions in the same order to every participant (good for comparison). Semi-structured interviews have a guide but allow flexibility (most common in cultural inquiry). Unstructured interviews are open conversations. Good interviewing requires preparation (a question guide), active listening (do not just talk), patience with silence, and follow-up probes ("Can you tell me more about that?"). Recording (with consent), transcribing, and coding for themes (Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis, 2006, is a standard reference) are the analytical work that follows the conversation.

Surveys use standardised questionnaires across many respondents to capture distributions and correlations. Strength: scale and comparability. Limit: superficial — you get what people will report on a checkbox, not what they actually do, mean, or feel deeply. Content analysis systematically examines media texts (news articles, social-media posts, ads, films, song lyrics) for patterns. Quantitative content analysis counts (how often does a stereotype appear?); qualitative content analysis interprets (how does the framing position the reader?). Both are valuable. Secondary-source research — reading and synthesising what others have already studied — is part of any project (literature review) and may be the main method when fieldwork is not feasible. For Grade 12 ISP, secondary research using peer-reviewed journals, government reports, ethnographic books, and reputable journalism is often the primary mode, supplemented by one or two original primary-source moves (an interview, a content analysis of selected media, a small-scale survey).

  • Ethnography — long-term participant-observation in a community, producing thick description.
  • Participant observation — the core ethnographic technique: take part while observing.
  • Field notes — detailed observational and reflective notes from fieldwork.
  • Interview guide — prepared question list for an interview, allowing flexibility.
  • Coding (thematic) — labelling interview/observation data by themes for analysis.
  • Content analysis — systematic examination of media texts for patterns (quantitative or qualitative).
  • Triangulation — using multiple methods or sources to converge on a finding.

Worked Example — Choosing Methods for an ISP Question

Return to the example research question: "How does participation in K-pop fan communities shape the cultural identity of second-generation Tamil-Canadian adolescents in the GTA?" What methods would address this well? Semi-structured interviews with 6–8 participants are the natural primary method — the question is interpretive (what does this experience mean to participants?), and interviews can capture inner perspectives surveys cannot. Each interview should run 45–60 minutes, be recorded with consent, transcribed, and thematically coded. Content analysis of the participants' social media profiles (with consent) — what proportion of posts are Tamil-related, K-pop-related, Canadian-related — gives a complementary external observation. Secondary-source literature review on Tamil-Canadian diaspora identity (Sundar 2008, Cheran 2007), on K-pop and transnational fandom (Jin 2016, Lie 2014), and on hybrid identity (Hall, Bhabha, Rai) provides the analytical framework. Ethics: informed consent, anonymity (use pseudonyms in the final paper), participants' right to withdraw, and consideration of whether the researcher is herself part of the fan community (insider positionality is acceptable but should be acknowledged). Together, these methods triangulate the finding — interview data on meaning, content-analysis data on observable practice, and secondary literature for the theoretical frame. That is a Grade 12 ISP-level methods plan. The actual ISP would propose this in the 1-2 page proposal, get teacher feedback, and then execute with regular check-ins.

  • Choosing method without thinking about whether it actually answers the research question.
  • Doing surveys with no theoretical grounding — just collecting opinions.
  • Interviewing without a guide and without follow-up probes.
  • Treating Wikipedia as a source — it is a useful starting point but cite the original sources it draws on.
  • Ignoring ethics, especially with vulnerable populations or sacred topics.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "locate and select information relevant to their investigations from a variety of primary and secondary sources."

📚 5.3 Sources — Primary vs Secondary; Evaluating Quality
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The single most important moment in social-science inquiry is the moment when a student decides whether a given source is good enough to use. The distinction between primary and secondary sources is foundational. A primary source is one created by participants in or witnesses to the events or culture being studied — original ethnographies, interviews, government documents, photographs, letters, treaties, songs, artefacts. A secondary source is one created by analysts who studied primary sources — textbooks, journal articles summarising earlier work, encyclopedia entries. Both are valuable. Strong research uses primary sources for the actual evidence and secondary sources for the analytical framework and contextual orientation. Many Grade 12 papers over-rely on secondary sources (it is easier); the move that distinguishes a strong paper is bringing in primary material.

For cultural studies, primary sources include: published ethnographies (Margaret Mead's Coming of Age in Samoa; Lila Abu-Lughod's Veiled Sentiments), oral history interviews, government documents (the Royal Proclamation of 1763, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's report and Calls to Action, Statistics Canada census reports), Indigenous oral tradition as recorded by community-authorised sources, films and documentaries (with awareness that all editing is interpretation), photographs and visual material (with attention to who took them and why), and original survey data. Secondary sources include peer-reviewed journal articles in American Anthropologist, American Journal of Sociology, Cultural Anthropology, Canadian Journal of Sociology; academic books from university presses; reputable journalism in outlets with editorial standards (the Globe and Mail, the Toronto Star, the CBC, the Guardian, the New York Times) when used appropriately.

The CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose), developed at the Meriam Library at CSU Chico, gives a quick rubric for evaluating any source. Currency: when was it published? For fast-changing topics (social media, current events), recent matters. For historical analysis, older is fine if classical. Relevance: does it actually address your specific question, or just the broader topic? Authority: who is the author and what is their credibility? Look for institutional affiliations, peer review, prior publications. Accuracy: are the claims supported by evidence the source itself shows or cites? Are statements verifiable elsewhere? Purpose: why was the source created — to inform, persuade, sell, propagandise? Different purposes call for different reading strategies, but unacknowledged purpose is a red flag.

The 21st century has produced a new layer of source challenges. Wikipedia is a useful orientation tool but should not be cited; rather, follow its footnotes to the actual scholarly sources. News media require attention to specific editorial standards and political position (the Globe and Mail and Toronto Star have different editorial policies; National Post has another). Social media can be primary data (Tweets, TikToks as evidence of cultural practice) but require ethical attention to consent and privacy when they were not posted for research purposes. AI-generated content (this is a 2026 reality students are negotiating) is not a source — it is a synthesis tool that may hallucinate facts; never cite ChatGPT as a source, and verify any factual claim it makes against actual primary or secondary sources. The discipline of source evaluation, applied carefully, is what separates strong cultural-studies work from social-media-driven opinion. HSC4M's Strand A explicitly asks students to develop this skill — and it is the skill that will most clearly transfer into university and into life as an informed citizen.

  • Primary source — original material from the period or culture being studied.
  • Secondary source — later analyst's interpretation or synthesis of primary material.
  • Peer review — process by which scholarly journals validate articles before publication.
  • CRAAP test — Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
  • Triangulation — confirming a finding from multiple independent sources.
  • Bias — systematic slant in a source's selection, framing, or interpretation.
  • Source provenance — the chain of where a source came from and how it has been handled.

Worked Example — Source Evaluation in Practice

You are researching residential schools in Canada. You find five potential sources. Evaluate each. Source 1: the TRC Final Report (six volumes plus the executive summary, 2015). Verdict: excellent primary source. Authored by the official commission, includes survivor testimony, official record review, and the 94 Calls to Action. Cite. Source 2: a Wikipedia article on residential schools. Verdict: use as orientation only; follow its references to actual scholarly sources and cite those. Source 3: a peer-reviewed article in the Canadian Journal of Native Studies on cultural genocide. Verdict: excellent secondary source; peer-reviewed, written by an Indigenous-studies scholar. Cite. Source 4: a YouTube video by a non-Indigenous commentator claiming the TRC overstated the harms. Verdict: a primary source of contemporary discourse, but not an authoritative analytical source — the commentator's credentials, evidence, and ideological framing need careful scrutiny, and the source's claims should be checked against the actual TRC record. Use selectively and with critical framing. Source 5: the Indigenous and Northern Affairs Canada (now Crown-Indigenous Relations) historical statement of apology by Prime Minister Stephen Harper, June 11, 2008. Verdict: primary source — an official government statement. Cite. The skill is to evaluate each on the CRAAP criteria, balance primary and secondary sources, and triangulate findings. A Grade 12 ISP that uses 8–12 well-evaluated sources, with at least 4 primary, is doing serious scholarship.

  • Treating all online sources as equivalent — quality varies enormously.
  • Over-relying on secondary sources because they are easier to read.
  • Citing Wikipedia directly instead of following its footnotes.
  • Ignoring the date of publication on fast-changing topics.
  • Forgetting to evaluate sources for purpose and political position (every source has a position; the question is whether it is acknowledged).

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — "assess various aspects of information gathered from primary and secondary sources (e.g., source's bias, accuracy, relevance, currency)."

⚖️ 5.4 Research Ethics — Consent, Confidentiality, OCAP
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Research ethics is not an optional add-on to social-science work; it is the foundation. The history of social-science research includes substantial harm done in the name of inquiry. The Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972) deceived hundreds of Black men in Alabama, denying them treatment so researchers could observe disease progression. The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971), the Milgram Obedience studies (1961), and many early ethnographies of Indigenous peoples involved practices that contemporary ethics would not permit. The current ethical framework for Canadian academic research is the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS 2, currently 2018 with updates), enforced through institutional Research Ethics Boards (REBs). For Grade 12 work in HSC4M, students are not bound by TCPS 2 formally but should learn its principles as preparation for university work and as practice in respectful research.

The three core ethical principles are respect for persons, concern for welfare, and justice. Respect for persons translates to informed consent: participants must understand what they are being asked to do, must agree freely, and must be able to withdraw at any point without penalty. Concern for welfare translates to minimising harm — physical, psychological, social, economic. Justice translates to fair selection of participants and equitable distribution of research benefits and burdens (no using vulnerable populations for research that benefits only privileged communities). For interview research, this means: explain the project clearly; obtain explicit (ideally written) consent; anonymise findings unless participants explicitly want to be named; offer participants the chance to review their quotations before publication; respect their right to withdraw at any time including after the interview.

For research involving Indigenous peoples in Canada, the additional framework is OCAP — Ownership, Control, Access, Possession — articulated by the First Nations Information Governance Centre. Ownership: First Nations communities collectively own information about themselves. Control: First Nations control how their information is collected, used, and disclosed. Access: First Nations must have access to information about themselves regardless of where it is held. Possession: First Nations need physical possession of data and information about themselves. TCPS 2 Chapter 9 explicitly requires that research involving Indigenous peoples be conducted in partnership with the relevant communities, with their consent, and in ways that benefit them. Grade 12 students undertaking ISP work with Indigenous topics should consult their teacher and ideally connect with Indigenous community organisations (FNTI, OFIFC, urban Indigenous centres) for guidance.

Two more ethical dimensions deserve mention. Researcher reflexivity — the practice of explicitly acknowledging one's own position (gender, race, class, age, religion, relationship to the topic) and how it shapes the inquiry — is now standard in cultural research. A non-Indigenous researcher writing about Indigenous issues is in a different position from an Indigenous researcher; acknowledging this is not relativism but disciplined honesty. Plagiarism and academic integrity: cite every source, use quotation marks for direct quotation, paraphrase carefully (substantively different words and structure, with citation), and never use AI-generated text without acknowledgement. The University of Toronto Scarborough's Centre for Teaching and Learning, the Scribbr citation guides, and the OWL at Purdue all give clear standards. The Grade 12 student who masters research ethics now will save themselves from major problems in their university career and will produce more respectful, more accurate, more useful research from the start.

  • Informed consent — participants understand what they will do and agree freely.
  • Confidentiality / anonymity — protecting participants' identifying information.
  • OCAP principles — Ownership, Control, Access, Possession for Indigenous research.
  • TCPS 2 — Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement, the federal research ethics framework.
  • Research Ethics Board (REB) — institutional body that reviews research protocols.
  • Researcher reflexivity — acknowledging one's own social position in the research.
  • Academic integrity / plagiarism — proper citation; never claim others' work as one's own.

Worked Example — Ethics Plan for an HSC4M ISP

Suppose your ISP examines second-generation Indo-Canadian young women's experience of arranged-marriage discussions in their families. You will conduct interviews with 5–7 participants. Informed consent: use a one-page consent form that explains the project, the interview process, the topics you may cover, how the data will be stored and used, the right to withdraw, and your contact information and your teacher's. Have each participant sign before the interview begins; offer a copy for them to keep. Confidentiality: assign pseudonyms; remove identifying details (name of college, specific village in India of origin, distinctive personal experiences); store recordings on encrypted devices; destroy after the project. Reflexivity: if you are yourself Indo-Canadian, acknowledge this in your methods section — the insider position has both access advantages and assumed-knowledge risks. If you are not Indo-Canadian, acknowledge the outsider position, the limits it produces, and the ways you will compensate (deeper background reading, follow-up validation with participants). Sensitivity: the topic involves family relationships, gender, and immigration — emotionally weighty material. Begin with less sensitive questions, allow silence, do not press if participants seem reluctant, and offer to skip any question. End with a debrief and information about mental-health resources (the school counsellor, kids help phone) in case the conversation has been hard. Anonymity caveat: in a small community, even pseudonyms may not fully protect identity; be especially careful with quoted specifics. Reporting: share findings with participants if they wish, before final submission. That kind of carefully-thought-out ethics plan is exactly what makes Grade 12 ISP work serious — and it is exactly the work the curriculum is asking students to learn how to do.

  • Conducting "research" with friends and family without explicit consent processes.
  • Treating ethics as paperwork rather than as ongoing relational practice.
  • Quoting people without anonymisation in a small community where they could be identified.
  • Researching Indigenous communities without consulting community protocols and OCAP.
  • Using ChatGPT-generated text without acknowledgement — this is academic misconduct.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.5 — "analyse and interpret research information, applying concepts of social sciences thinking."

📝 5.5 Communicating Findings — APA Citation and Effective Writing
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The final phase of social-science inquiry is communication. A finding nobody reads is, for academic purposes, no finding at all. Communication has two parts: structure (the architecture of your paper) and style (the texture of your sentences). For HSC4M and for university work in anthropology, sociology, and psychology, the citation standard is APA (American Psychological Association) 7th edition. APA differs from MLA (used in English) and Chicago (used in history) in its detail and convention; learning it now is direct preparation for university social-science work.

The standard structure for a research paper or ISP is: Title page (project title, your name, course code, date); Abstract (150-word summary of question, methods, findings); Introduction (research question, why it matters, brief preview of the paper); Literature review (what does prior scholarship say? what gap are you addressing?); Methods (how did you do the research?); Findings / Discussion (what did you find, and what does it mean?); Conclusion (so-what, limitations, future directions); References (alphabetical list of all cited sources in APA format). For a 1500–2000-word ISP, the proportions might be: Intro 200, Literature 400, Methods 250, Findings/Discussion 800–1000, Conclusion 150, plus References. The structure is not a straitjacket; sometimes the literature review folds into the discussion, sometimes the methods are brief. But for first-time researchers, the standard structure is a useful scaffold.

APA in-text citations follow the author-date format: (Boroditsky, 2011). For direct quotations, include the page: (Boroditsky, 2011, p. 65). Multiple authors: (Markus & Kitayama, 1991); for three or more, (Hofstede et al., 2010). The reference list at the end gives the full bibliographic detail. Example formats: Journal article: Boroditsky, L. (2011). How language shapes thought. Scientific American, 304(2), 62–65. Book: Geertz, C. (1973). The interpretation of cultures. Basic Books. Edited book chapter: Crenshaw, K. (1991). Mapping the margins. In M. Albertson Fineman & R. Mykitiuk (Eds.), The public nature of private violence (pp. 93–118). Routledge. Government document: Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada. (2015). Honouring the truth, reconciling for the future: Summary of the final report. Web source: include author/organisation, date, title, URL. The Purdue OWL APA guide and the Scribbr APA generator are excellent free resources for getting the details right.

Style matters too. Social-science writing aims for clarity (the reader should follow on a first read), precision (specific terms used with specific meanings), evidence-driven (claims backed by citation or data), and academically appropriate register (formal but not stiff; first person is increasingly acceptable in social-science writing). Avoid jargon that does not earn its keep; avoid the passive voice where active works better; avoid filler ("It is important to note that..."); cite rather than assert ("The TRC found that..." rather than "Everyone knows that..."). For an HSC4M ISP, plan to draft, leave it alone for a few days, then revise hard — most strong writing happens in revision, not first draft. Read it aloud to catch awkward sentences. Have a peer or tutor read it for clarity. Use the Hemingway App or Grammarly for surface-level edits but do not rely on them for substance. The final polished paper, properly cited and clearly argued, is what represents your inquiry to the world. Treat that step with the same care you put into the rest of the research.

  • APA 7th edition — the citation standard for social-science work (anthropology, sociology, psychology, education).
  • In-text citation — author and date in parentheses at the point of reference.
  • Reference list — alphabetical list of cited sources at the end of the paper.
  • Literature review — synthesis of prior scholarship on the topic.
  • Abstract — 150-word summary of question, methods, findings.
  • Plagiarism — using others' work without proper attribution.
  • Academic register — formal, evidence-driven, precise writing style.

Worked Example — Two Versions of the Same Sentence in APA Style

Take a finding from your interview research: a participant said that K-pop fandom helped her feel proud of her South-Asian heritage by giving her models of confident non-white celebrity. Weak version: "Some people say that K-pop helps Asian kids feel better about themselves." Issues: who said? where? on what evidence? "Better about themselves" is vague. No citation. Strong version (with citation): "Lila (pseudonym, age 16) framed K-pop fandom as a route to ethnic pride: 'Seeing all these beautiful Korean and Asian stars on a global stage made me proud of being brown, in a way I didn't expect.' Her account resonates with Jin's (2016) framing of K-pop as a vehicle for Asian global visibility that has produced complex re-readings of Asian and Asian-diaspora identity in North America." What changed: a specific source (a quoted participant), grammatical embedding of the quotation, attribution to an academic secondary source, and an explicit interpretive move ('resonates with'). This is what a Grade 12 ISP finding paragraph should look like — primary evidence interpreted through a secondary-source theoretical lens, with both properly cited. Each paragraph of the findings section should aim for this quality of integration. Achieving it is what graduates a first-year university paper from acceptable to excellent — and HSC4M's ISP is your training ground for that move.

  • Submitting without proofreading — typos and citation errors signal sloppy work.
  • Citing only in the reference list, not in the text where evidence appears.
  • Direct quoting without page numbers (APA requires page for direct quotes).
  • Using AI tools to generate text — this is academic misconduct and the universities and the schools detect it.
  • Writing in passive voice throughout — it obscures who did what.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.7 — "communicate the results of their inquiries using appropriate formats and citation conventions (APA, 7th edition)."
