🌐 1.1 What is Culture? — Definitions and Dimensions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

"Culture" is one of the most over-used and under-defined words in everyday English. People talk about "company culture," "cancel culture," "popular culture," "high culture," and "having culture" — usually without specifying what the word actually means. The first job of HSC4M is to displace that everyday looseness with a disciplined social-science definition, because the difference between casual use and analytical use is the difference between gossip and inquiry. When social scientists use the word "culture," they are not asking "is this person sophisticated?" They are asking, "what shared, learned system of meanings does this group use to make sense of the world?"

The classic anthropological definition was given by Edward Burnett Tylor in 1871 in Primitive Culture: "Culture or Civilization, taken in its wide ethnographic sense, is that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society." Modern anthropologists have refined this — Clifford Geertz called culture a "web of significance" that humans themselves spin and then inhabit — but every working definition keeps four features in view. Culture is learned (transmitted, not biological); shared (held by a group, not just an individual); symbolic (operates through signs, language, gesture); and integrated (its parts hang together, so changing one element changes others).

It is also useful to split culture into material and non-material elements. Material culture is the physical stuff a society produces and uses — tools, buildings, clothing, food, art objects, technology. Non-material culture is the intangible: beliefs, values, norms, language, ideologies, knowledge systems. The two are not separable in practice. A smartphone is material culture, but the etiquette of when to answer, the expectation of constant connection, and the algorithms that shape what we see are non-material. Studying a culture means studying both, and tracing how they interlock.

One distinction that always trips up students is culture versus society. A society is a group of people who live in a defined territory and share institutions; a culture is the shared meaning-system that gives that group its way of life. A single society can host many cultures (Canada, India, the United States); a single culture can stretch across many societies (Christianity, Islam, hip-hop, climate-justice movements). Holding the two terms apart sharpens almost every analytical paragraph you will write in this course.

  • Culture — the learned, shared, symbolic, transmitted system of meanings of a group.
  • Society — a group of people sharing territory, institutions, and structured relationships.
  • Material culture — physical objects produced and used by a society (artefacts, technology, art).
  • Non-material culture — intangible beliefs, values, norms, language, knowledge.
  • Norms — shared rules of behaviour (folkways, mores, taboos, laws).
  • Values — abstract standards of what is good, right, beautiful in a culture.
  • Symbol — anything that carries socially-agreed meaning (a flag, a wedding ring, the colour red).
  • Worldview — the underlying cognitive and ethical framework a culture provides.

Worked Example — Analysing a Cup of Tea

Take a single object: a cup of tea. The cup, the kettle, the leaves, the milk — all material culture. But the meaning of tea is non-material and varies enormously by culture. In Japan, the chanoyu tea ceremony is a meditative ritual with formal movements derived from Zen Buddhism; preparing tea is preparing a moment of presence. In Britain, "having a cuppa" is everyday hospitality and emotional first aid — offered to a friend who has had bad news. In Morocco, mint tea is poured from height into small glasses three times — bitter as life, strong as love, sweet as death, the saying goes — and refusing the third is rude. In Tibetan and Mongolian cultures, butter tea (po cha) is salted and fatty, suited to high-altitude life. In each case the same material object (tea) is embedded in a different non-material system (ritual, etiquette, hospitality, nutrition). A social scientist analysing tea cross-culturally is studying how shared symbols carry shared meaning — and how those meanings are inseparable from environment, history, and religion. This is the integrated, symbolic character of culture in miniature.

  • Treating "culture" as a synonym for "ethnicity" or "country" (a single country can hold many cultures).
  • Confusing culture (a meaning-system) with society (a structured group).
  • Listing only material culture and forgetting the non-material values that animate it.
  • Using "culture" to mean "fancy taste" (the everyday-English sense) inside a social-science paragraph.
  • Assuming culture is fixed — every working definition emphasizes that culture is dynamic and contested.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "explain the meaning of the term culture, with reference to its various dimensions (material and non-material, cognitive, behavioural, symbolic)."

🔍 1.2 Characteristics of Culture — Learned, Shared, Symbolic, Integrated, Dynamic
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Once you have a working definition of culture, the next move is to nail down its key characteristics. Most anthropology textbooks converge on five: culture is learned, shared, symbolic, integrated, and dynamic. Each adjective is doing analytical work — together they explain why culture is real (it has effects), why it varies (because it is learned), and why it changes (because it is integrated and dynamic, not fixed).

Culture is learned. No human is born with culture. Newborns from any society can be raised in any other and will become culturally competent there — they will speak its language without an accent, eat its food, hold its values, find its humour funny. The technical term for this process is enculturation: the lifelong transmission of culture through family, peers, school, media, and institutions. The implication is profound. Cultural differences cannot be explained by biology or "nature"; they are the result of patterned learning. This is one reason racism is not just morally wrong but factually mistaken: there is no genetic basis for cultural difference.

Culture is shared. A purely private meaning-system is not culture; it is idiosyncrasy. Culture is what a group holds in common — enough in common to coordinate behaviour, to interpret a gesture, to mourn a death the same way. But "shared" does not mean uniform. Subcultures and countercultures share enough with the dominant culture to be intelligible, while differing in important ways (hip-hop is a subculture that emerged from Black American culture and now has global reach). Culture is symbolic. Most of what we do as humans is conducted through symbols — language being the master example, but also gestures, dress, food, art, money, flags, religion. A green light means "go" only because everyone in the relevant culture agrees it does. The arbitrariness of the sign is what makes culture both possible and infinitely various.

Culture is integrated. The parts of a culture hang together; a change in one ripples through others. The shift to industrial wage labour did not just change how people earned money; it transformed family structure (the nuclear family), gender roles (the male breadwinner ideal), schooling (compulsory mass education), even time perception (clock time replacing seasonal time). This is why naive culture-change projects so often fail: a Western NGO introducing a new agricultural technology may discover that the technology is rejected because it undermines local kinship obligations, religious calendars, or gender divisions of labour. Culture is dynamic. All cultures change — through innovation, contact, conflict, and reinterpretation. Anthropologists used to write about cultures as if they were timeless museum-pieces; that "ethnographic present" is now recognised as a fiction. There is no culture that has not been re-shaped by encounter and time. Holding all five characteristics together gives you a robust working concept that will keep you out of essentialism (treating cultures as fixed, pure objects) and into analysis.

  • Enculturation — the lifelong learning of one's own culture.
  • Acculturation — change resulting from sustained contact with another culture.
  • Subculture — a group within a larger society that shares enough but maintains distinct practices.
  • Counterculture — a subculture that consciously opposes dominant values.
  • Dominant culture — the most powerful, mainstream cultural system in a society.
  • Cultural universals — features found in every known society (language, family, religion, food taboos).
  • Cultural particulars — features specific to a single culture.

Worked Example — Identifying the Five Characteristics in Wedding Customs

Consider weddings. Learned: a child raised in Sicily will know the village rituals; a child of Sicilian immigrants raised in Toronto will know a hybrid version. The form is not inherited genetically. Shared: a wedding only works if guests, officiants, and the couple share the script. Symbolic: the white dress, the rings, the breaking of glass (Jewish), the henna (South Asian), the lasso (Mexican) are not functionally useful — they signify. Integrated: weddings tie into religion (which officiates), economy (who pays the dowry/bride-price), family structure (whose name changes), and law (who is legally entitled to marry). Changing one element — for example, same-sex marriage becoming legal — ripples through all of these. Dynamic: Canadian weddings in 2026 look very different from 1956. The white dress is now optional; non-religious ceremonies are common; the legally-recognised gender configuration has expanded. The same five characteristics will let you analyse any cultural practice — funerals, food, work, schooling — at a similar level of disciplined detail.

  • Treating "culture is learned" as if it removes responsibility — learned behaviours can be unlearned.
  • Assuming "shared" means uniform; almost every culture contains internal disagreement.
  • Ignoring the integration of a culture and analysing one element (like food) as if it stood alone.
  • Romanticising "traditional" cultures as static and "modern" cultures as dynamic — both change.
  • Confusing cultural universals (every society has religion) with cultural uniformity (every society has the same religion).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "describe the key characteristics of culture (e.g., learned, shared, symbolic, integrated, dynamic)."

🌍 1.3 Cultural Universals and Cultural Particulars
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Anthropology stands or falls on a paradox: cultures vary enormously, yet every known human society has language, family structure, religion or cosmology, food rules, music, kinship terms, art, and a story about death. These features that appear in every known culture are called cultural universals, and the anthropologist George Peter Murdock famously listed dozens of them in his 1945 essay "The Common Denominator of Cultures." Universals do not mean uniform content — every culture has language, but the languages differ; every culture has food taboos, but the taboos differ — they mean that there are common categories humans use to organise life.

The opposite of a universal is a cultural particular — a practice or meaning specific to one culture (or a small set of cultures). The British practice of queuing in a single-file line is a particular; the Japanese practice of bowing as greeting is a particular; the Inuit practice of building a tupiq (skin tent) for summer travel is a particular. Cultural particulars are not "less real" than universals; they are the concrete forms that universal categories take in specific times and places.

Anthropologists make a methodological distinction between emic and etic perspectives, owed to linguist Kenneth Pike (1954). The emic view is the insider's view — how the people in a culture themselves describe and understand their practices, using their own concepts. The etic view is the outsider's view — how an analyst describes the same practices using external, comparative concepts. Both are needed. Pure etic analysis flattens difference; pure emic immersion makes comparison impossible. Strong social science holds both: it learns the emic perspective from the inside, then steps back to an etic frame to compare across cultures. Most ethical inquiry is heavy on the emic — letting insiders speak in their own categories first — before any etic generalisation is offered.

The political stakes are real. Treating Western categories (the nuclear family, capitalist economy, secular state) as universal "normal" and treating non-Western particulars as "exotic" or "primitive" is a colonial habit that distorted social science for a century. The modern discipline tries to flip the move: everything is particular, and the analyst's job is to compare particulars without ranking them. Margaret Mead's Coming of Age in Samoa (1928), whatever its later controversies, did this provocatively — by showing that adolescence in Samoa looked nothing like the American stereotype of adolescent storm-and-stress, she suggested that the "universal" of teenage rebellion was actually a particular of a specific Western moment. Used carefully, the universal/particular framework gives you a powerful analytical tool that can compare cultures without ranking them.

  • Cultural universal — a feature found in every known human culture (language, kinship, religion).
  • Cultural particular — the specific form a universal takes in a given culture.
  • Emic perspective — the insider's, participant's view, using local categories.
  • Etic perspective — the outsider's, analyst's view, using comparative categories.
  • Cultural relativism — analysing practices in their own context, not by external standards.
  • Ethnocentrism — judging another culture by the standards of one's own.
  • Holism — the anthropological principle of studying all parts of a culture as interconnected.

Worked Example — Marriage as Universal, Particular, Emic, Etic

The institution of marriage is a cultural universal — every known society has some socially-recognised form of long-term partnership. But the particulars vary wildly. In the Nayar of southwest India (studied by Kathleen Gough in the 1950s), women historically had multiple "visiting husbands" who lived with their own matrilineal families; the husband-wife unit did not co-reside as the European model assumes. Among the Nuer of South Sudan (studied by E. E. Evans-Pritchard), "ghost marriage" allowed a woman to marry the spirit of a deceased man, with a living relative fathering children in his name to continue the lineage. From an emic view, both arrangements make perfect sense — they preserve property, kinship, and ritual obligations exactly as the local system requires. From an etic view, anthropologists treat them as variations on the universal category "marriage," expanding the definition rather than rejecting these arrangements as not-really-marriage. A Grade 12 essay that uses this framework can hold complexity that simpler approaches lose.

  • Treating cultural universals as the "real" categories and particulars as deviations.
  • Conflating "universal" (found in every culture) with "natural" (biologically fixed).
  • Mistaking the etic outsider's view for the only legitimate analysis — ignoring how insiders frame their own lives.
  • Using emic descriptions without context, producing texts that feel like uncritical reportage.
  • Listing exotic-sounding examples without explaining their internal logic.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "explain key concepts associated with the study of culture (e.g., subculture, counterculture, dominant culture, cultural universals, ethnocentrism, cultural relativism)."

⚖️ 1.4 Ethnocentrism and Cultural Relativism
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Ethnocentrism is the habit of judging other cultures by the standards of one's own — treating one's familiar way as "normal" and other ways as "weird," "primitive," or "wrong." William Graham Sumner coined the term in 1906, but the phenomenon long predates him. Every culture has some version of ethnocentrism; what makes it analytically dangerous is that it operates below conscious notice. The Toronto teenager who calls a custom "gross" or "weird" is doing ethnocentric work; so is the British colonial administrator who labelled local kinship arrangements "uncivilised." Both are using their own framework as the implicit ruler against which everything else is measured.

The traditional anthropological response is cultural relativism, articulated by Franz Boas at Columbia University in the early 20th century and developed by his students Ruth Benedict and Margaret Mead. The principle: every cultural practice must be analysed in the context of its own meaning-system, not against external moral standards. Practices that look bizarre or repugnant from outside frequently make sense once you understand the surrounding web of kinship, religion, environment, and history. Cultural relativism is a methodological discipline — it forces the analyst to suspend judgment long enough to actually understand — before any moral conclusion is reached.

Cultural relativism does not, however, require moral relativism (the claim that any practice is acceptable as long as someone's culture endorses it). That extreme view collapses when applied to obviously harmful practices: female genital cutting, honour killing, residential schools, chattel slavery, child marriage. Most contemporary anthropologists distinguish methodological cultural relativism (a research stance: understand before judging) from ethical cultural relativism (a moral position: never judge), and accept only the first. The hard, productive question is how to combine methodological humility (taking insiders seriously) with ethical seriousness (recognising universal human rights). The UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and UNDRIP (2007) attempt this balance institutionally.

In practice, Canadian classrooms encounter this tension in concrete ways. Are arranged marriages "wrong"? It depends — the form ranges from forced marriage (a violation of consent) to genuine matchmaking with full consent of both partners (a different but defensible practice). Was European colonialism "wrong" by 1500s European standards? Yes, by the contemporary Spanish Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas, who in A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1542) condemned the cruelty in terms his own culture should have heard. Cultural relativism does not require moral abdication; it requires careful, contextual, historically-informed judgment. Mastering this distinction is one of the most important pieces of intellectual work you will do this year.

  • Ethnocentrism — judging other cultures by the standards of one's own.
  • Cultural relativism — analysing practices in their own context, suspending external judgment.
  • Methodological cultural relativism — a research stance: understand first.
  • Moral relativism — the (controversial) claim that all practices are equally valid.
  • Universal human rights — claims of rights belonging to all humans, regardless of culture (UDHR 1948).
  • Cultural imperialism — the imposition of one culture's standards on another, often by colonial or economic power.

Worked Example — The Two Faces of Cultural Relativism (Right and Wrong)

Consider two cases. Case A: A new immigrant family in Toronto cooks fermented fish (Filipino bagoong, Korean jeotgal, or Swedish surströmming). A neighbour calls the smell "disgusting." The neighbour's reaction is ethnocentric — the practice is harmless and beloved in its original context; the disgust says more about the neighbour's enculturation than about the food. Cultural relativism corrects the move: in context, the practice is normal and valued. Case B: A 14-year-old girl is to be forcibly married to a 40-year-old man because "it's our culture." Cultural relativism does not require us to accept this. The principle of methodological relativism asks us to understand how the practice came to exist (historical patriarchy, dowry pressures, economic precarity). The principle of universal rights — particularly the rights of the child, freedom from coercion, and the right to consent — overrides cultural appeals where they are used to justify harm. The grown-up move is to do both at once: take cultural context seriously (Case A) and recognise that some practices violate universal protections (Case B). Each case requires close, particular reasoning; there is no shortcut.

  • Equating "tolerance" with the abandonment of all moral judgment.
  • Using "it's their culture" as a blanket defence of any practice — most cultures contain internal critics.
  • Assuming ethnocentrism is only a flaw of "other people" — every reader has ethnocentric habits.
  • Treating cultural relativism as relativism about facts (it is about judgment, not about whether things happened).
  • Forgetting that cultures themselves contain disagreement; "their culture" is often a single faction speaking for many.

🔬 1.5 Three Social-Science Lenses — Anthropology, Sociology, Psychology
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

HSC4M is built around the idea that you can look at any cultural phenomenon through three social-science lenses, each with its own questions, methods, and theoretical traditions. The three are anthropology, sociology, and psychology. Strong essays in this course can name which lens they are using and what each lens contributes — and which limits each one has. The lenses are not in competition; they are complementary tools.

Anthropology is the comparative, holistic study of humans across time and place. The discipline includes four traditional sub-fields: cultural anthropology (ethnography of contemporary societies), archaeology (material remains of past societies), linguistic anthropology (language as cultural practice), and biological anthropology (human evolution and variation). Its signature method is ethnographic fieldwork — long-term participant observation, learning the local language, building relationships, and producing a "thick description" (Clifford Geertz's term) of how life is lived from the inside. Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922), based on two years in the Trobriand Islands, is the founding example. Anthropology's strength is depth and contextual richness; its limit is generalisability (one community is not the whole world).

Sociology studies the structure and functioning of modern societies, especially industrial and post-industrial ones, with attention to institutions, inequality, power, and social change. Founders include Émile Durkheim (social facts, anomie, solidarity), Max Weber (bureaucracy, rationalisation, charisma), and Karl Marx (class, capitalism, ideology). Sociology's methods are diverse: surveys, statistical analysis, comparative case studies, content analysis, and increasingly, computational and digital methods. Where anthropology asks "how does this small group make sense of life?", sociology asks "how do institutions structure life across an entire society — and who wins and loses?" Sociology's strength is large-scale pattern; its limit is missing the texture of lived experience that ethnography captures.

Psychology studies individual mental processes and behaviour, with cultural psychology being the sub-field most relevant to HSC4M. Cross-cultural psychologists (Geert Hofstede's dimensions of culture, Hazel Markus and Shinobu Kitayama's work on independent vs interdependent self-construals) compare how mental processes — perception, memory, emotion, motivation — differ across cultural contexts. Psychology's signature method is the controlled experiment, supplemented by surveys, neuroimaging, and longitudinal studies. Its strength is causal claims about individual minds; its limit is that experiments often rely on undergraduate WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic) samples and may not generalise. Combining all three lenses gives you a stereoscopic view: anthropology shows the meaning-system, sociology shows the institutional scaffolding, psychology shows the individual mind operating within both.

  • Anthropology — comparative, holistic study of humans across time and place.
  • Ethnography — long-term participant-observation fieldwork producing thick description.
  • Sociology — study of institutions, structure, inequality, and change in modern societies.
  • Psychology — study of individual mental processes and behaviour.
  • Cultural psychology — sub-field examining how culture shapes mind.
  • Thick description — interpretive ethnography that conveys context and meaning (Geertz).
  • WEIRD samples — Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic — the biased default in much psychology research.
  • Holism — the principle of studying parts as integrated wholes.

Worked Example — Three Lenses on the Same Practice (Tipping in Restaurants)

Why do North American diners tip waitstaff 15–20% while Japanese diners tip nothing? Each lens illuminates a different facet. An anthropological analysis would describe tipping ethnographically: who tips, when, how the bill is calculated, what tipping signals about the relationship between server and guest in each context. The Japanese resistance to tipping makes sense within a value system where good service is the baseline expectation, not a market transaction; offering money can read as suggesting the server's work was inadequate. A sociological analysis would notice that North American tipping is tied to a labour-market structure where waitstaff receive sub-minimum-wage (the U.S. federal tipped wage is $2.13/hour) and depend on tips for income, while Japanese restaurants pay full wages. The institution drives the behaviour. A psychological analysis would ask: how does the act of tipping affect customers' sense of generosity, control, and reciprocity? Experiments show that tippers feel higher status and a sense of social obligation that can produce guilt when tip is insufficient. All three together: tipping is a culturally-meaningful symbol (anthropology), structurally produced by labour markets (sociology), and psychologically experienced as moral exchange (psychology). A Level 4 HSC4M paragraph can name all three.

  • Treating the three lenses as competing rather than complementary.
  • Calling all social-science work "sociology" because it is the most familiar term.
  • Forgetting that anthropology's holism means it integrates psychological and sociological observations within its frame.
  • Generalising from WEIRD psychology samples to "human nature" without acknowledging the limitation.
  • Ignoring archaeology and linguistic anthropology when "anthropology" is mentioned — both are core sub-fields.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "compare and contrast the approaches of anthropology, sociology, and psychology to the study of culture."
