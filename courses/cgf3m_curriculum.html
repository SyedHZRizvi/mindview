1. Course Description

This course investigates Earth's physical systems and the dynamic interactions among them. Students explore the processes that shape landforms, climates, soils, and ecosystems; investigate how natural systems support life and how human activities alter those systems; analyse contemporary geographic issues such as climate change, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion; and apply the geographic inquiry process to evaluate sustainability proposals. Throughout, students develop spatial-thinking and systems-thinking skills, working with maps, satellite imagery, GIS data, and primary sources to make evidence-based arguments about the physical Earth and humanity's place within it.

ConceptApplication in CGF3M
Spatial SignificanceWhy is a particular place important? Why are earthquakes concentrated along plate boundaries? Why is the boreal forest where it is?
Patterns & TrendsLatitude–temperature patterns; ocean-current circulation; biome distribution by climate; trends in atmospheric CO₂.
InterrelationshipsLithosphere–atmosphere–hydrosphere–biosphere couplings; agriculture as a forcing on soil, water, and climate.
Geographic PerspectiveEnvironmental, economic, political, social, and Indigenous-knowledge lenses on the same physical issue.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Geographic Inquiry & Skills: Geographers ask spatially-grounded questions and answer them through a disciplined inquiry process using maps, GIS, fieldwork, and primary sources.
  • Strand B — Physical Processes: Earth's surface is the product of interacting physical systems — plate tectonics, weather and climate, the hydrologic cycle, and soil-forming processes — that operate on radically different time scales.
  • Strand C — Human–Physical Interactions: Human activity is now a major geomorphic agent. Land use, resource extraction, agriculture, urbanization, and hazard exposure all reshape, and are reshaped by, the physical environment.
  • Strand D — Sustainability: Sustaining the human enterprise within Earth's planetary boundaries requires understanding renewability, carrying capacity, and the long-term consequences of resource decisions — including Indigenous stewardship traditions.
  • Strand E — Geographic Issues: Contemporary issues such as climate change, water scarcity, biodiversity loss, and environmental justice can be evaluated using geographic methods to weigh evidence, perspectives, and policy options.

5. Strand A — Geographic Inquiry & Skill Development (embedded across all units)

A1. Geographic Inquiry

  • A1.1 formulate questions to guide investigations into geographic issues, patterns, and processes
  • A1.2 select and organize relevant evidence and information from a variety of primary and secondary sources
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence relevant to their investigations using a variety of tools and approaches (maps, GIS, statistics)
  • A1.5 use the geographic concepts of spatial significance, patterns and trends, interrelationships, and geographic perspective
  • A1.6 evaluate evidence and draw conclusions about the issues, processes, or patterns they are investigating
  • A1.7 communicate the results of geographic investigations in a variety of forms (reports, presentations, infographics, GIS maps)
  • A1.8 use accepted forms of documentation to acknowledge sources

A2. Developing Transferable Skills

  • A2.1–A2.4 apply geographic skills and knowledge to contemporary problems; identify career and post-secondary pathways in geography, environmental science, planning, surveying, GIS analysis, and resource management.

Career Connections

Cartographer, GIS analyst, hydrologist, soil scientist, climatologist, urban & regional planner, environmental consultant, conservation officer, parks ranger, remote-sensing specialist, environmental lawyer, sustainability officer, geographer (academic and applied).

6. Strand B — Physical Processes (Unit 1)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. analyse the characteristics of, and the relationships between, Earth's physical systems (e.g., the lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere);
  • B2. demonstrate an understanding of the processes that produce Earth's major physical features (plate tectonics, weathering, erosion, deposition);
  • B3. analyse the patterns of weather and climate and the factors that affect them at global and local scales.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 explain the four spheres (lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere) and the feedback couplings among them;
  • B2.1 describe the structure of Earth's interior (crust, mantle, outer/inner core);
  • B2.2 explain plate-tectonic theory (convergent, divergent, transform boundaries; hotspots) using examples such as the San Andreas fault, the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the Pacific Ring of Fire;
  • B2.3 describe weathering, erosion, and deposition by water, wind, and ice, and their landform outcomes;
  • B3.1 explain factors that influence climate (latitude, elevation, ocean currents, prevailing winds, proximity to water, continentality);
  • B3.2 describe the global atmospheric circulation (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar cells; ITCZ; trade winds; jet streams) and the resulting climate zones;
  • B3.3 explain the hydrologic cycle and storage reservoirs (oceans 97%, ice caps, groundwater, surface water, atmosphere).
STSE focus: earthquake-resistant construction; reading weather radar and satellite imagery; climate-attribution science.

7. Strand C — Biomes, Ecosystems, & Human–Physical Interactions (Units 2 & 3)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. analyse the characteristics of major terrestrial biomes and the climatic factors that produce them;
  • C2. demonstrate an understanding of ecosystem dynamics (energy flow, nutrient cycles, succession, biodiversity);
  • C3. analyse interactions between human activities and natural systems, including agriculture, resource extraction, urbanization, and exposure to natural hazards.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 describe the eight major terrestrial biomes (tropical rainforest, temperate deciduous forest, boreal forest/taiga, tundra, grassland, desert, savanna, Mediterranean) using climate, soils, flora, and fauna;
  • C1.2 describe the 15 Canadian terrestrial ecozones and their dominant ecosystems;
  • C2.1 explain energy flow (food chains, food webs, trophic pyramids, 10% rule);
  • C2.2 explain biogeochemical cycles (water, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur);
  • C2.3 explain primary and secondary ecological succession (e.g., glacial retreat in Glacier Bay; forest fire recovery);
  • C3.1 analyse agricultural systems (subsistence, commercial, industrial; the Green Revolution) and their environmental impacts;
  • C3.2 analyse urbanization and land-use change (sprawl, urban heat islands, brownfields, smart growth);
  • C3.3 explain natural hazards (earthquakes, volcanoes, hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, wildfires) and risk-mitigation strategies.
STSE focus: the Walkerton water tragedy (May 2000); Fort McMurray wildfire (May 2016); urban densification debates in the Greater Toronto Area.

8. Strand D — Sustainability & Stewardship (Unit 4)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. analyse the concept of sustainability and the relationship between resource use and resource renewability;
  • D2. demonstrate an understanding of strategies for sustainable management of resources (forests, fisheries, fresh water, soils, minerals, energy);
  • D3. analyse Indigenous knowledge systems and stewardship traditions as approaches to sustainable resource management.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 distinguish renewable, non-renewable, and flow resources; explain the concept of carrying capacity and ecological footprint;
  • D1.2 calculate ecological footprints using global hectares per capita; compare Canada (~7.7 gha) with global average (~2.7 gha) and Earth's biocapacity (~1.6 gha);
  • D2.1 evaluate sustainable forestry (FSC certification, selective harvesting); the collapse and partial recovery of the Atlantic cod fishery;
  • D2.2 evaluate energy transitions (fossil fuels → renewables; the role of nuclear; intermittency and storage);
  • D3.1 explain Indigenous approaches to land stewardship (Seven Generations principle; Two-Eyed Seeing; Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas/IPCAs);
  • D3.2 evaluate land-use planning that integrates Indigenous knowledge (e.g., Edéhzhíe IPCA, NWT, 2018).
STSE focus: Truth and Reconciliation Commission Calls to Action; the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) in Canadian resource law; Indigenous-led conservation.

9. Strand E — Geographic Issues & Inquiry (Unit 5)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. analyse selected contemporary geographic issues (climate change, water scarcity, biodiversity loss, environmental justice) using the geographic inquiry process;
  • E2. demonstrate an understanding of the tools of geographic analysis (maps, GIS, remote sensing, fieldwork);
  • E3. analyse the impact of human activities on the natural environment from local to global scales.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 analyse the scientific evidence for anthropogenic climate change (Keeling curve, IPCC AR6, paleoclimate proxies) and evaluate mitigation/adaptation strategies;
  • E1.2 analyse global water scarcity (physical vs economic scarcity; the Aral Sea; the Colorado River Compact);
  • E1.3 analyse deforestation (Amazon, Congo, Borneo) and the connection to soy, beef, palm-oil, and timber supply chains;
  • E2.1 use GIS software (QGIS, ArcGIS Online) to produce a thematic map;
  • E2.2 read and interpret remote-sensing imagery (Landsat, MODIS) for land-cover change analysis;
  • E3.1 calculate and compare personal/household ecological footprints; propose evidence-based reductions.
STSE focus: the COP21 Paris Agreement (2015) and Canada's nationally-determined contribution; environmental racism (Africville, NS; Grassy Narrows mercury crisis); citizen-science platforms (iNaturalist, eBird, Globe at Night).

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, inquiry projects, GIS labs, fieldwork reports, and assignments. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a culminating geographic inquiry project and/or a final examination administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, learning logs, peer feedback).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs next steps.
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, inquiry projects, final examination).

14. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation.
