GLE4O — Advanced Learning Strategies: Skills for Success After Secondary School, Grade 12 Open

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 to 12: Guidance and Career Education (2006, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: GLE4O  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Type: Open  |  Prerequisite: None (recommended: GLE3O or successful completion of GLC2O)

1. Course Description

This course improves students' learning and personal-management skills, preparing them to make the transition from secondary school to post-secondary destinations — university, college, apprenticeship, the workplace, or community living. Students will develop and apply skills required for success in post-secondary settings: independent learning, self-advocacy, critical reading, executive function, professional communication, financial literacy, and adult civic engagement. They will assess their readiness for the transition, set short- and long-term goals, and design action plans for the year after OSSD graduation. This is the capstone of the Career-Education sequence (GLC2O Grade 10 → GWL3O / GPP3O / GLE3O Grade 11 → GLE4O Grade 12). The audience is graduating seniors aged seventeen or eighteen who will, within months of finishing the course, encounter rent, OSAP, taxes, first jobs, voting, and adult self-direction.

3. Overall Goals of the Course

By the end of the course, students will:

  • demonstrate self-awareness of their values, strengths, and growth areas as emerging adults, and use that self-knowledge to set realistic post-secondary goals;
  • apply effective learning strategies for university-, college-, or workplace-level expectations, including critical reading at speed, independent executive function, and academic-integrity practices;
  • communicate clearly and professionally in adult workplace and post-secondary contexts, including email, meetings, presentations, feedback exchanges, and conflict resolution;
  • demonstrate financial literacy and independent-living skills appropriate for an Ontario resident aged eighteen, including budgeting, credit management, lease comprehension, banking, taxes, OHIP, and transportation choice;
  • plan for active civic engagement and lifelong learning, including voting, volunteering, mentorship, and adaptation to a changing labour market.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Self-Awareness, Identity, and Adult Goal-Setting: Successful transitions to adulthood are grounded in calibrated self-knowledge — values, strengths, mental-health needs, and locus of control.
  • Strand B — Strategies for Post-Secondary Academic and Career Success: Post-secondary expectations differ meaningfully from secondary in pace, independence, and the form of evidence required; specific transferable strategies (critical reading, citation management, support-service use) close that gap.
  • Strand C — Communication, Collaboration, and Conflict Resolution: Adult communication is structured by professional registers, by feedback as a shared learning tool, and by interest-based negotiation rather than positional argument.
  • Strand D — Independent Living and Civic Engagement: Financial literacy, tenancy rights, taxation, healthcare, civic participation, and lifelong learning are interconnected adult-life competencies that should be exercised — not merely studied — before graduation.

5. Strand A — Self-Awareness, Identity, and Adult Goal-Setting

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Adult Identity Formation: demonstrate an understanding of identity-development theories (e.g., Erikson) and apply them to their own transition from late adolescence into emerging adulthood;
  • A2. Values, Strengths, and Self-Assessment: assess their own values, strengths, and growth areas as they conclude secondary school, and use that assessment to inform post-secondary planning;
  • A3. Goal-Setting and Personal Direction: develop short- and long-term goals (1-year, 5-year, 10-year horizons) using established frameworks, and design plans of action;
  • A4. Resilience and Mental-Health Self-Advocacy: demonstrate knowledge of mental-health literacy and self-advocacy skills appropriate for adult contexts.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 describe Erikson's psychosocial stages relevant to emerging adulthood (identity vs role-confusion, intimacy vs isolation);
  • A1.2 distinguish "emerging adulthood" (Arnett) from adolescence and from established adulthood;
  • A2.1 use values-clarification activities to articulate their top five terminal values and three instrumental values;
  • A2.2 produce a strengths inventory drawing on coursework, extracurricular involvement, and lived experience;
  • A3.1 set SMART goals across 1-, 5-, and 10-year horizons with explicit milestones;
  • A3.2 distinguish internal from external locus of control and apply attributional analysis to their own outcomes;
  • A4.1 describe major mental-health frameworks (the WHO 2014 definition, the dual continua model);
  • A4.2 identify Ontario-specific supports (Good2Talk for post-secondary students, ConnexOntario, the Kids Help Phone 0–20 line, campus counselling services).
Anchor frameworks: Erik Erikson, Childhood and Society (1950); Jeffrey Arnett, "Emerging Adulthood" (American Psychologist, 2000); Julian Rotter's locus of control (1966); Carol Dweck's mindset research; Ontario's Creating Pathways to Success (2013); the World Health Organization's 2014 mental-health definition.

6. Strand B — Strategies for Post-Secondary Academic and Career Success

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Post-Secondary Pathway Knowledge: demonstrate an understanding of the differences between secondary school and university, college, apprenticeship, and the workplace in pace, independence, and expectations;
  • B2. Academic Learning Strategies: apply learning strategies appropriate for post-secondary contexts, including critical reading at speed, lecture engagement, seminar participation, and research-based learning;
  • B3. Academic Integrity and Digital Tools: apply academic-integrity principles and use digital productivity tools (citation managers, reference systems);
  • B4. Executive Function and Support Services: manage their own time, attention, and well-being without imposed structure, and access available academic-support services.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 compare contact hours, assessment frequency, and feedback cadence between OSSD courses and a typical first-year university course;
  • B1.2 describe the apprenticeship learning loop (Red Seal trade structure) and contrast it with classroom learning;
  • B2.1 apply the SQ3R, Cornell, or zettelkasten note-taking methods to a sample first-year reading;
  • B2.2 distinguish skimming, scanning, and deep reading and apply each appropriately;
  • B3.1 cite sources using APA or Chicago format and use a citation manager (Zotero, Mendeley, or library equivalent);
  • B3.2 describe how plagiarism and AI-misuse are treated in Ontario universities and colleges;
  • B4.1 apply implementation-intention and timeboxing techniques to a real assignment;
  • B4.2 identify the academic-support services available at three Ontario post-secondary institutions of personal interest (writing centres, accessibility services, peer tutoring).
Pathway anchors: Ontario Universities' Application Centre (OUAC); Ontario College Application Service (OCAS); Ministry of Labour, Training and Skills Development apprenticeship pathways; Creating Pathways to Success 2013; institutional academic-integrity policies (e.g., U of T Code of Behaviour on Academic Matters).

7. Strand C — Communication, Collaboration, and Conflict Resolution

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Professional Communication: communicate clearly in adult professional registers — email, meetings, presentations, written reports;
  • C2. Feedback and Emotional Intelligence: give and receive feedback as an adult, applying frameworks of emotional intelligence and psychological safety;
  • C3. Cross-Cultural and Intergenerational Communication: adapt communication for diverse cultural and generational contexts, and recognise common patterns of miscommunication;
  • C4. Conflict Resolution and Leadership: apply interest-based negotiation techniques (Harvard model) and lead without formal authority.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 draft a clear professional email following subject-line, greeting, ask, and sign-off conventions;
  • C1.2 prepare and deliver a 5-minute professional presentation with appropriate visual aids;
  • C2.1 apply Goleman's five domains of emotional intelligence in a workplace simulation;
  • C2.2 use the Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI) feedback model and the radical-candor framework;
  • C3.1 describe Hofstede's cultural dimensions and apply them to a cross-cultural workplace scenario;
  • C3.2 identify common patterns of intergenerational miscommunication (Baby Boomer / Gen X / Millennial / Gen Z workplace norms);
  • C4.1 apply the Fisher-Ury "Getting to Yes" framework — separate people from problem, focus on interests not positions, generate options for mutual gain, use objective criteria;
  • C4.2 describe leadership without authority (Heifetz's adaptive-leadership framework; Brown's daring-leadership framework).
Anchor frameworks: Daniel Goleman, Emotional Intelligence (1995); Fisher, Ury, and Patton, Getting to Yes (1981, revised 2011); Geert Hofstede, Cultures and Organizations; Amy Edmondson on psychological safety (Harvard Business School); Brené Brown, Dare to Lead; Kim Scott, Radical Candor.

8. Strand D — Independent Living and Civic Engagement

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Financial Literacy: demonstrate basic financial literacy appropriate for an Ontario resident aged eighteen — budgeting, credit, debt, saving, investing;
  • D2. Independent Living: demonstrate understanding of rental tenancy, banking, healthcare access, transportation, insurance, and taxation in Ontario;
  • D3. Civic Engagement: participate as an informed adult citizen — voting, volunteering, contributing to community;
  • D4. Lifelong Learning and Labour-Market Adaptation: plan for continuous learning beyond formal schooling, including responses to labour-market change (automation, AI).

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 build a 50/30/20 personal budget for a hypothetical or real income;
  • D1.2 describe how Canadian credit scores are calculated (Equifax / TransUnion) and the consequences of credit-card debt;
  • D1.3 describe Ontario Student Assistance Program (OSAP) loan structure, repayment grace period, and Repayment Assistance Plan;
  • D1.4 distinguish TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, and RESP at a working level;
  • D2.1 read and interpret a standard Ontario residential lease (the Standard Form of Lease) and identify tenant rights under the Residential Tenancies Act;
  • D2.2 file a basic T1 General income-tax return using free software (e.g., Wealthsimple Tax);
  • D2.3 describe how OHIP and the Ontario Health Insurance Plan apply to an 18-year-old leaving home;
  • D3.1 register to vote with Elections Canada and Elections Ontario and identify the federal, provincial, and municipal voting cycles;
  • D3.2 plan a volunteer commitment of 20+ hours appropriate to personal interests and time;
  • D4.1 describe automation and AI's projected impact on three labour-market sectors of personal interest;
  • D4.2 articulate a personal lifelong-learning plan including certification, professional development, and informal learning.
Anchor documents and tools: Ontario Residential Tenancies Act (2006); Standard Form of Lease 2229E; Ontario Student Assistance Program (OSAP); Canada Revenue Agency T1 General; Elections Ontario; Elections Canada; Statistics Canada labour-market data; the Future Skills Centre (Toronto Metropolitan); the World Economic Forum Future of Jobs reports.

11. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprising unit tests, the Adult Transition Portfolio (a multi-chapter artefact built across the course), action plans, budget projects, presentations, and assessment-OF tasks. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprising the Adult Transition Portfolio Defence and Final Examination administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

12. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes on Erikson, budgeting math, lease comprehension, conflict-resolution frameworks; reflective journals; goal-setting trackers).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., diagnostic on incoming financial literacy, post-secondary pathway awareness, communication confidence).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, the Adult Transition Portfolio, the Final Exam, the Portfolio Defence).

13. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (Ministry pathway documents, Statistics Canada labour-market dashboards, OSAP and CRA resources, podcast and video interviews with recent post-secondary alumni), multiple means of engagement (student-selected pathway, choice of volunteer issue, choice of lifelong-learning focus), and multiple means of expression (written portfolio, video defence, oral presentation, slide deck, infographic) to meet the needs of diverse learners. Where appropriate, the course incorporates Indigenous knowledge, perspectives, and ways of knowing — including First Nations post-secondary supports (Indspire), Indigenous-specific financial-literacy resources (AFOA Canada), and treaty-rights education within civic engagement. The course honours the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools.

14. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in GLE4O because the course models adult independence: the student is the agent of their own transition plan, and the learning-skills evidence is itself a major signal to admissions officers, employers, and apprenticeship masters.
