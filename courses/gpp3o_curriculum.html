GPP3O — Leadership and Peer Support, Grade 11 Open

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Guidance and Career Education (2006, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: GPP3O  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Type: Open  |  Prerequisite: None

1. Course Description

This course prepares students to act in leadership and peer-support roles. They will design and implement a plan for contributing to their school and/or community; develop skills in communication, interpersonal relations, teamwork, and conflict management; and apply those skills in leadership and/or peer-support roles — for example, as a peer mentor, peer mediator, peer tutor, student-council member, or club organiser. Students will examine group dynamics and decision making, ways in which people learn, and the use of inclusive language. They will also examine theories of leadership, the contribution of leaders in Canadian society, and pathways from leadership experience into postsecondary study and the workplace. GPP3O is most often taken alongside or following GLC2O Career Studies and is a useful preparation for senior co-op, social-science, and human-services pathways.

3. Overall Goals of the Course

By the end of the course, students will:

  • demonstrate an understanding of personal strengths, character, values, and emotional intelligence as foundations for leadership and helping;
  • apply interpersonal and communication skills — active listening, assertive expression, non-verbal awareness, constructive feedback, and conflict resolution — in real and simulated leadership situations;
  • analyse a range of leadership theories (trait, behavioural, situational, transformational, transactional, servant, ethical) and evaluate their fit for diverse Canadian contexts;
  • describe and apply peer-support, peer-mediation, and mentoring frameworks, including confidentiality, scope of role, and Ontario reporting obligations;
  • design, implement, and reflect on a small leadership or peer-support initiative in their school or community, including planning, teamwork, risk management, and evaluation of impact.

5. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Personal Knowledge and Management (A): Leadership begins with self-awareness — strengths, growth areas, emotional intelligence, and the habits that let a person show up reliably for others.
  • Interpersonal Knowledge and Skills (B): Communication and conflict-resolution skills are learnable, practicable, and the everyday medium through which leadership happens.
  • Exploration of Opportunities (C): Leadership and peer-support roles take many forms; theories give us a vocabulary for naming choices, and Canadian contexts shape how leadership looks in practice.
  • Preparation for Transitions and Change (D): Peer-support and mentoring frameworks let students help others while honouring confidentiality, scope of role, and Ontario reporting duties; reflection turns experience into transferable capacity.

6. Strand A — Personal Knowledge and Management

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Personal Awareness: demonstrate an understanding of their own personal characteristics — strengths, values, beliefs, learning styles, and emotional patterns — and how these relate to leadership and helping roles;
  • A2. Personal Management: apply strategies to manage their well-being, time, and stress, and to set and pursue personal and academic goals;
  • A3. Self-Reflection: use self-assessment and reflective tools to monitor their own growth as a learner, leader, and helper.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 use a variety of self-assessment tools (e.g., personality inventories, learning-style surveys, values card-sorts) to identify their characteristics relevant to leadership;
  • A1.2 describe their personal strengths and areas for growth as a leader and as a helper;
  • A1.3 identify their emotional patterns — including triggers, stress responses, and recovery strategies — and explain how these patterns affect their interactions with others;
  • A1.4 explain the components of emotional intelligence (self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills) and assess their own profile;
  • A2.1 apply time-management strategies (e.g., prioritisation, scheduling, deadline back-planning) to school and leadership commitments;
  • A2.2 demonstrate the use of healthy stress-management strategies (physical activity, sleep hygiene, mindfulness, social support, professional help when needed);
  • A2.3 set short- and long-term SMART goals (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound) connected to leadership growth;
  • A2.4 demonstrate strategies for building resilience in response to setbacks, failure, and feedback;
  • A3.1 use a reflective tool (journal, video log, learning portfolio, conferencing) to record growth as a leader/helper across the semester;
  • A3.2 invite, receive, and act on feedback from peers, teachers, and mentors.
Anchor frameworks: Daniel Goleman's Emotional Intelligence (1995, 2006); Carol Dweck's growth-mindset research; Ontario's Creating Pathways to Success (2013) Individual Pathways Plan (IPP); the Ministry of Education's Foundations for a Healthy School framework on student well-being.

7. Strand B — Interpersonal Knowledge and Skills

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Communication Skills: demonstrate effective use of verbal and non-verbal communication, including active listening, in a variety of interpersonal and group situations;
  • B2. Group Dynamics: describe and analyse processes that affect the functioning of groups, including stages of group development, roles, and decision-making strategies;
  • B3. Conflict Management: demonstrate strategies for preventing, managing, and resolving interpersonal and group conflict.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 use active-listening techniques (paraphrasing, summarising, open questions, attending behaviours) in helping conversations;
  • B1.2 distinguish among passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, and assertive communication and apply assertive language in role-plays;
  • B1.3 interpret non-verbal cues (proximity, eye contact, posture, tone, facial expression) and explain how culture, gender, neurodivergence, and power shape their meaning;
  • B1.4 give and receive constructive feedback using a structured model (e.g., behaviour–impact–suggestion);
  • B1.5 use inclusive language and challenge exclusionary or discriminatory language in groups;
  • B2.1 describe stages of group development (e.g., Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning) and identify the leader's role at each stage;
  • B2.2 identify functional and dysfunctional roles in groups and apply strategies for facilitation, agenda-setting, and meeting management;
  • B2.3 describe and apply decision-making strategies (consensus, majority, consultative, autocratic) and judge their fit for specific situations;
  • B3.1 describe sources of conflict in groups and identify five conflict styles (Thomas–Kilmann: competing, avoiding, accommodating, compromising, collaborating);
  • B3.2 demonstrate steps in a peer-mediation framework and apply restorative-practice questions to a school conflict;
  • B3.3 identify boundaries: when to refer a peer to a teacher, counsellor, or other professional rather than handle a situation alone.
Anchor frameworks: Bruce Tuckman's stages of small-group development (1965); Thomas–Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument; restorative-practices questions developed by IIRP (International Institute for Restorative Practices); Ontario's Equity and Inclusive Education Strategy.

8. Strand C — Exploration of Opportunities (Leadership Theory)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Theories of Leadership: describe and analyse a range of leadership theories and evaluate their fit for different situations and groups;
  • C2. Leadership in Canadian Society: investigate the contribution of leaders — including women, Indigenous, racialised, and disability-rights leaders — in Canadian public life;
  • C3. Ethical and Cultural Dimensions of Leadership: explain ethical responsibilities of leaders, including avoidance of coercion, conflict of interest, and abuse of position.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 describe trait theory and explain its limits as a complete account of leadership;
  • C1.2 describe behavioural theories (Ohio State and Michigan studies; task-oriented vs. relationship-oriented behaviour) and connect them to a school example;
  • C1.3 explain situational leadership (Hersey & Blanchard) — directing, coaching, supporting, delegating — and apply it to a group with members at different developmental levels;
  • C1.4 distinguish transactional and transformational leadership (Bass, Burns) and identify Canadian examples of each;
  • C1.5 describe servant leadership (Greenleaf) and ethical leadership and evaluate their applicability to peer-helper roles;
  • C2.1 analyse the contributions of at least three Canadian leaders, including at least one Indigenous leader and one female leader;
  • C2.2 describe ways in which cultural context — including Indigenous concepts of leadership (e.g., the Seven Grandfather Teachings) — shapes the practice of leadership;
  • C2.3 identify pathways into leadership roles in volunteer, school, work, and community settings;
  • C3.1 identify ethical responsibilities of leaders and apply an ethical-decision framework to a case;
  • C3.2 describe ways that gender, race, dis/ability, and class affect access to leadership and shape how a leader is perceived.
Anchor frameworks: Bass & Avolio's Full Range Leadership Model; Hersey & Blanchard's Situational Leadership II; Robert K. Greenleaf, The Servant as Leader (1970); the Seven Grandfather Teachings as articulated in Anishinaabe community sources; the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action (2015) for education.

9. Strand D — Preparation for Transitions and Change (Peer Support / Application)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Peer-Support and Mentoring Frameworks: describe and apply peer-helper, peer-mediation, and mentoring frameworks;
  • D2. Boundaries, Confidentiality, and Safety: demonstrate understanding of the limits of the peer-helper role, confidentiality, and Ontario reporting obligations;
  • D3. Leadership Initiative and Reflection: plan, implement, and reflect on a school or community leadership / peer-support initiative.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 distinguish among peer tutoring, peer mentoring, peer mediation, and peer support;
  • D1.2 apply a five-step helping conversation (engage – listen – clarify – options – next-steps) in a role-play;
  • D1.3 identify community and school resources to which a peer can be referred (e.g., guidance counsellor, social worker, Kids Help Phone 1-800-668-6868, Black Youth Helpline, Indigenous Hope for Wellness Line);
  • D2.1 explain the limits of confidentiality and identify situations that must be reported (risk of harm to self or others; abuse of a child under 16);
  • D2.2 describe Ontario's mandatory reporting duty under the Child, Youth and Family Services Act (2017) — every person has a duty to report a reasonable suspicion of child abuse to a children's aid society;
  • D2.3 describe boundary issues including dual relationships, gifts, social-media contact, and burnout;
  • D3.1 plan a leadership/peer-support initiative including charter, milestones, resources, risk assessment, and evaluation plan;
  • D3.2 form a team, assign roles, and apply meeting-management strategies through implementation;
  • D3.3 use a reflective cycle (e.g., Gibbs: description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan) to learn from the initiative;
  • D3.4 connect leadership-portfolio evidence to postsecondary and career pathways (university, college, apprenticeship, workplace, gap-year service).
Anchor frameworks: Gibbs Reflective Cycle (1988); Carol Tilley's peer-helper model adapted to Canadian school settings; Ontario Child, Youth and Family Services Act (2017); the Public Health Agency of Canada's Promoting Mental Health Means Promoting the Best of Ourselves.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark: unit tests, reflective journals, peer-helper conversation videos, case-study analyses, in-class role-plays, leadership-project plan and implementation, observation records of helping skills.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark: Leadership Project Showcase (15%) — students present their initiative, evidence of impact, and reflection — combined with a Final Examination (15%) covering all four strands.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (emotional-intelligence inventories, active-listening rubrics, project self-evaluations, learning journals).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic descriptive feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., baseline communication checklist, conflict-style inventory, prior-leadership-experience inventory).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, mediation role-play, project plan, project implementation, portfolio, final exam).

14. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (video case studies, audio podcasts, infographic frameworks, written texts), multiple means of engagement (student-chosen leadership project, choice of peer-helper role, choice of leader for biographical study), and multiple means of expression (essay, role-play, video, podcast, portfolio, presentation) to meet the needs of diverse learners and to honour the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools. The course incorporates Indigenous perspectives on leadership — including the Seven Grandfather Teachings, circle process, and consensus decision-making — alongside Western theoretical frameworks.

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. All six are central to GPP3O — the course is in many ways an explicit cultivation of these skills, especially for students moving into postsecondary co-op, residence life, employment, and community leadership.
