❓ 3.1 Formulating Inquiry Questions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every serious civic inquiry begins with a question — but not just any question. The Ontario curriculum distinguishes between fact-recall questions, concept-application questions, and investigative inquiry questions, and political inquiry depends mostly on the third type. A fact-recall question ("How many seats are in the House of Commons?") has a single correct answer that can be looked up. A concept-application question ("Why does Canada use FPTP and not PR?") asks students to apply learned ideas. An investigative inquiry question ("Should Ontario adopt mixed-member proportional representation, and what would the trade-offs be?") asks for an evidence-based judgement that the inquirer must reason through.

Strong inquiry questions share certain features. They are open-ended — they cannot be answered yes/no or in a single sentence. They are bounded — they specify a place, a time, and a scope so the inquiry stays focused. They are contestable — informed people can reasonably disagree about the answer. They are significant — answering them matters to someone beyond the inquirer. And they invite multiple perspectives — they cannot be answered by hearing only one side. Compare two versions of the same topic. Weak: "Is climate change bad?" (yes/no; not contestable among informed people; vague). Strong: "How should the City of Toronto balance climate-resilience investments (sea walls, storm-water tunnels) against transit and affordable-housing needs in the 2026–2030 capital budget?" (open-ended, bounded, contestable, significant, multi-perspective).

The political-inquiry process the Ontario curriculum recommends — sometimes drawn as a circle to emphasise iteration — has five stages. Formulate questions; gather and organise information and evidence; interpret and analyse; evaluate and draw conclusions; communicate findings. Each stage can return to an earlier stage if new evidence requires reformulating the question. Skipping a stage produces predictable failures: jumping from question to conclusion without evidence; gathering evidence without a question to focus it; communicating without first concluding clearly; concluding without checking whether the evidence supports the conclusion.

One common refinement: students should articulate sub-questions before launching into research. A main question like "Should Ontario adopt MMP?" naturally decomposes into "How does FPTP work and what are its current effects?", "How does MMP work?", "What happened when New Zealand changed from FPTP to MMP in 1996?", "What did Ontario voters say in the 2007 referendum, and why?", "What constraints does the Charter or the Constitution place on electoral reform?", "What do current Ontario civil-society groups (Fair Vote Canada, the Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform) say?" Each sub-question opens an evidence trail. The main question is answered by synthesising the sub-question answers. Without the sub-questions, the inquirer either drowns in too much undifferentiated material or stops too early.

  • Inquiry question — open-ended, contestable, bounded question that drives investigation.
  • Fact-recall question — answerable by lookup (used to scaffold but rarely drives inquiry).
  • Concept-application question — applies learned ideas to a new case.
  • Sub-question — narrower question that contributes to answering the main question.
  • Open-ended / bounded / contestable — three core features of strong inquiry questions.
  • Political inquiry process — formulate → gather → analyse → evaluate → communicate.
  • Iteration — returning to earlier inquiry stages as new evidence demands.

Worked Example — Sharpening a Civic Inquiry Question

First draft: "Is voting good?"

Problem: not contestable among informed people; not bounded; not actionable. Sharpen: "Why is voter turnout in Ontario municipal elections (around 38%) lower than in federal elections (around 67%), and what should be done about it?" Better — bounded to Ontario municipal, identifies a specific puzzle, invites multiple perspectives. Even better: "Would lowering the voting age to 16 in Ontario municipal elections increase long-term lifetime turnout, and what evidence supports each side?" — bounded (Ontario, municipal, voting age 16), contestable (informed people disagree), significant (real policy debate in Ontario), multi-perspective (research from Scotland's 16+ voting, Austrian elections, sceptics' counter-evidence). Sub-questions: "What did Scotland's experience show?"; "What is the Canadian Charter status of voting-age limits?"; "What did the Ontario Citizens' Assembly recommend?"; "Who benefits and who loses if the voting age is lowered?" Now the inquiry has direction.

  • Asking a yes/no question and treating the answer as the inquiry.
  • Asking a question with no place or time bound — "Is democracy good?" is too big.
  • Confusing strong-feeling questions with strong-inquiry questions — passion is not contestability.
  • Skipping sub-questions and trying to research the main question directly.
  • Stopping at the first plausible answer instead of stress-testing it against counter-evidence.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments of civic importance."

📊 3.2 Gathering & Evaluating Evidence (Source Credibility)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An inquiry is only as good as its evidence. Political inquiry uses many kinds of evidence — statistics, court rulings, legislative texts, government reports, journalism, expert testimony, lived-experience accounts, photographs, polling, archival documents — and the inquirer's job is to gather a diverse, credible set and to weigh each piece appropriately. Diversity matters because a single source — however credible — can only see part of the issue. Credibility matters because political topics attract motivated actors who publish slanted or false material to influence the conclusion.

The classic frame is the distinction between primary sources (created at the time and place of the event: court rulings, government data releases, original interviews, photographs, treaties, news reports during the events) and secondary sources (interpretations after the fact: textbooks, news commentary, academic articles, encyclopedia entries). A well-evidenced inquiry uses both — primary sources for the underlying facts and secondary sources for context, interpretation, and synthesis. Wikipedia is a useful starting point but never a citable end-point; follow its footnotes to the primary sources and cite those.

Source-credibility evaluation should be habitual, not a check-box. Adapt the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) or Caulfield's SIFT from Chapter 2.3 to civic sources. Currency: when was the source published, and have important events since changed the picture? Relevance: does the source actually address the inquiry question, or only a tangential issue? Authority: who wrote it, what is their track record on this topic, what institution backs them? Accuracy: are the claims supported with citations to evidence I can check? Purpose: what is the author's apparent goal — to inform, to persuade, to sell, to recruit? An advocacy organisation can produce excellent evidence — but its purpose must be named so the reader can adjust for the angle. A think-tank's funding model matters; an academic article in a peer-reviewed journal has gone through one form of vetting; an op-ed has not.

For Canadian civic inquiry, a particularly useful map of credible sources includes: Government data — Statistics Canada, Elections Canada, Auditor General of Canada, Parliamentary Budget Officer, Statistics Ontario; Court rulings — CanLII (Canadian Legal Information Institute, canlii.org); Legislative material — Hansard (debates), bills, statutes (laws-lois.justice.gc.ca, ontario.ca/laws); Journalism — CBC News, The Globe and Mail, Toronto Star, The Walrus, The Tyee, regional papers; Academic — Canadian Journal of Political Science, Canadian Public Policy, peer-reviewed sources via library databases; Civil society / NGO — Canadian Civil Liberties Association, Amnesty International Canada, Fair Vote Canada, Ecojustice, Amnesty Indigenous-led organisations (e.g., the Centre for First Nations Governance, the Yellowhead Institute); Indigenous sources — Indigenous-led media (APTN News, IndigenousWatchdog) and academic centres. The discipline is not to favour one of these categories over the others but to triangulate across multiple categories so no single perspective dominates.

Note specifically the role of opinion polls. Polls are evidence about public preference at a moment in time, but they are not facts about what is true (a majority can be wrong about an empirical question) and they are not facts about what is right (a majority can hold a discriminatory view). Polls should be used to characterise public opinion, not to settle whether a policy is wise. Always check a poll's sample size, margin of error, sampling method (random vs. opt-in), and the exact wording of the question — small wording changes can shift results by 10 points or more.

  • Primary source — created at the time and place of the event.
  • Secondary source — interpretation or synthesis of primary sources after the fact.
  • CRAAP test — Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose source-evaluation framework.
  • Triangulation — confirming a claim across multiple independent sources.
  • Peer review — academic vetting process; presence is one (but not only) credibility signal.
  • Citation — formal attribution of a source so a reader can check it.
  • Margin of error — statistical uncertainty in a poll, often ±3% at 19 times out of 20.
  • Selection bias — error introduced when the sample over-represents some part of the population.

Worked Example — Triangulating a Civic Claim

Question: How is voter turnout among Indigenous voters in federal elections changing over time?

Step 1 — primary sources: Elections Canada publishes a post-election report each cycle that includes special analyses of Indigenous turnout in on-reserve and First Nations identification surveys. Pull the 2015, 2019, 2021, and 2025 reports. Step 2 — secondary academic source: the Canadian Journal of Political Science published peer-reviewed analyses (e.g., Ladner & McCrossan) on Indigenous electoral participation; look in your library database. Step 3 — Indigenous-led source: APTN News and the Yellowhead Institute publish analytical pieces on Indigenous voter participation, often highlighting contextual factors (treaty politics, distrust of the federal Crown, lateral participation in Band Council and self-government elections rather than federal). Step 4 — triangulate: do the three sources agree on the direction and magnitude of change? Where they disagree, what explains the disagreement (methodology, sample, definition of "Indigenous")? Step 5 — write the answer with citations. Note that Indigenous voter participation in federal elections has historically been lower than the national average but rose between 2011 and 2015 by 14 points on-reserve (Elections Canada Special Report, 2016) and has since plateaued. Acknowledge the analytical view that participation in federal elections is not the only or even the most-meaningful measure of Indigenous civic engagement.

  • Treating Wikipedia as a citable source — use it as a finder, cite the footnoted primary source.
  • Relying on a single source however credible; triangulation is the discipline.
  • Confusing an opinion poll with a factual finding.
  • Ignoring sample size and methodology when comparing polls.
  • Citing an advocacy organisation's view as if it were unbiased — name the purpose; the evidence may still be credible.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations" and A1.2 — "select and organize relevant evidence, data, and information on issues of civic importance from a variety of primary and secondary sources."

🧭 3.3 The Four Concepts of Political Thinking
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Ontario civics curriculum frames analysis through four concepts of political thinking: Political Significance; Objectives and Results; Stability and Change; and Political Perspective. These four concepts are the disciplinary toolkit — the same way a scientist uses hypothesis/experiment/inference, a civics inquirer uses these four lenses to investigate any civic issue. Students should be able to define each and apply each to a real issue.

Political Significance asks: why does this issue, event, decision, or person matter? Significance is not the same as scale (a small local decision can be highly significant if it affects rights, sets precedent, or shifts the political conversation). Significance is also not fixed (an event's significance can grow or shrink in retrospect, as we see its consequences). To analyse significance, ask: significant for whom? significant for what — rights, institutions, future precedent? significant now or in the long run? An overlooked significance signal is the response of marginalised groups whose voices were not initially heard but who recognised the implications first.

Objectives and Results asks: what did the actors involved set out to do, and what actually happened? Intended consequences and actual consequences often diverge. The federal carbon-pricing system, for instance, has the objective of reducing greenhouse-gas emissions while protecting low-income households through a rebate. Analysts ask: are emissions falling at the projected rate? Are rebates reaching the intended households? Are unintended consequences (e.g., shifting consumption patterns, regional political backlash) appearing? Distinguishing objectives from results is the discipline of empirical evaluation, and it lets a citizen judge a policy on whether it delivers what its designers promised — separate from whether its goals were worth pursuing in the first place.

Stability and Change asks: what has remained constant in the political system, and what has been transformed? This is a comparative lens — you can only describe change against the background of stability. Constitutional fundamentals (parliamentary government, federalism, the Charter) are remarkably stable; particular policies (immigration levels, tax rates, energy choices, social-program design) can change rapidly with elections. The lens also forces the inquirer to identify the drivers of change — popular mobilisation, demographic shifts, technological change, court rulings, economic shocks, intergovernmental conflict — and not to mistake one driver for the whole story.

Political Perspective asks: whose voices, beliefs, and values shape this issue? It is the most distinctively political of the four concepts because politics is, fundamentally, the contest of perspectives. A health-care policy looks different to a frontline nurse, a hospital administrator, a Ministry official, a patient with a chronic condition, an Indigenous community elder, a public-health professor, and an opposition critic. Each is partial; together they are richer than any single view. The civic inquirer's job is not to flatten perspectives into "a balanced view" (which often privileges loud over right) but to map them — who holds which view, why, what evidence and values support it, what trade-offs the view accepts.

The four concepts work together. A civic inquiry on Indigenous-Crown reconciliation might apply: significance (constitutional, moral, ongoing); objectives and results (the federal apology of 2008 was the objective; results include the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation and the UNDRIP Act, but also continued unmarked-grave revelations and the persistence of boil-water advisories on reserves); stability and change (the Crown-Indigenous treaty relationship is foundational; the practical content of that relationship has been reshaped by the TRC, the courts, and Indigenous-led mobilisation); political perspective (First Nations, Métis, Inuit, federal Crown, provincial Crown, non-Indigenous Canadians, settler-allied movements). A strong civic essay uses all four concepts; a weak essay uses only one (typically significance or perspective).

  • Political significance — why an issue or event matters, for whom, and when.
  • Objectives and results — what actors intended vs. what actually happened.
  • Stability and change — what stays constant vs. what is transformed, and what drives the change.
  • Political perspective — whose voices, beliefs, and values shape the issue.
  • Unintended consequence — outcome not planned by the actor, but caused by their action.
  • Driver of change — the underlying force (movement, demographic, technology, court ruling, shock).
  • Counterfactual — what would have happened had a different action been taken; a tool for evaluating significance.

Worked Example — Applying All Four Concepts to a Single Case

Case: Canada's 1969 White Paper on Indian policy (the proposed elimination of the Indian Act and the Department of Indian Affairs, presented by Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau and Minister Jean Chrétien).

Significance: highly significant — it crystallised Indigenous political mobilisation in twentieth-century Canada by proposing to assimilate First Nations into "equal" Canadian citizenship while abolishing the rights, treaties, and reserves that maintained Indigenous sovereignty. The pushback (notably Harold Cardinal's The Unjust Society and the National Indian Brotherhood's Red Paper, 1970) launched the modern Indigenous-rights movement. Objectives and results: the federal objective was assimilation under a liberal-individualist frame ("we cannot recognise Aboriginal rights"); the result was the opposite — the policy was withdrawn in 1971, section 35 of the 1982 Constitution explicitly recognised Aboriginal and treaty rights, and Indigenous self-government became a constitutional principle. Stability and change: what stayed stable was the existence of treaties and the underlying nation-to-nation legal status; what changed was the political and public consciousness of those treaties and the willingness to defend them. Perspective: Trudeau's perspective held that "individual rights" were the universal framework; Cardinal's perspective insisted that Indigenous rights were collective, treaty-based, and constitutionally distinct. A complete civic analysis of the 1969 White Paper requires holding all four concepts at once; reducing it to "Trudeau was wrong" misses the structural lesson about objectives, perspectives, and what drove change.

  • Using only one concept (usually significance or perspective) when all four would deepen the analysis.
  • Confusing objectives with results — "the policy aimed to reduce emissions" is not "the policy reduced emissions."
  • Treating "stability and change" as a description rather than a question about drivers.
  • Flattening perspectives into a single "balanced" view rather than mapping who holds what view and why.
  • Failing to write down the four concepts as a checklist before drafting an analysis.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.5 — "use the concepts of political thinking (i.e., political significance, objectives and results, stability and change, and political perspective) when analysing and evaluating evidence, data, and information and formulating conclusions and/or judgements about issues of civic importance."

🔬 3.4 Analysing & Evaluating: Drawing Conclusions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Once evidence is gathered, the inquirer must analyse what the evidence shows, evaluate its quality, and draw conclusions. Analysis and evaluation are different. Analysis takes evidence apart — pattern-finding, comparison, classification, identification of trends and outliers. Evaluation puts evidence together against a standard — judging the strength of the support for a conclusion, comparing options against criteria, weighing trade-offs. Both are essential, and weak civic writing typically collapses them into "what I think" without showing the work.

Useful analytical tools include: comparison tables (rows = options or cases, columns = criteria — useful for comparing electoral systems, government levels, policy proposals); cause-and-effect chains (event A → consequence B → consequence C, with the strength of each arrow specified); stakeholder matrices (who is affected, in what direction, by what magnitude); before/after timelines (what was the situation before the intervention, what was it after, what is the counterfactual); SWOT-style frames (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats applied to a policy or campaign). The goal is not to use all tools always but to choose the tool that fits the question.

Evaluation requires explicit criteria. Asking "is policy X good?" is too vague; asking "does policy X advance equity, fiscal responsibility, climate goals, and Indigenous rights as the speaker promised, given the evidence in 2024–2025 implementation reports?" is concrete. Naming the criteria upfront forces the inquirer to be honest about which trade-offs they accept. Two analysts with the same evidence can reach different conclusions, and both can be defensible — if each is clear about the criteria they applied. This is the part of civics that distinguishes opinion from informed judgement.

A particular discipline: consider the strongest version of the view you disagree with. The principle of steel-manning (the opposite of strawmanning) is that an analysis is only strong if it has engaged with the best version of the opposing case. If a student is writing about whether Ontario should rejoin a national electricity grid, they should not only summarise the case for joining; they should articulate the strongest case for staying independent (autonomy in pricing decisions, regulatory simplicity, existing investment, the political cost of integration) and then explain why, on the evidence, the joining case is stronger. Steel-manning protects against motivated reasoning and produces conclusions readers — including those who initially disagreed — find more persuasive.

The conclusion itself should be precise. Vague conclusions ("more action is needed") do not advance public conversation. Specific conclusions ("Based on the evidence reviewed, Ontario should pilot ranked-ballot voting in three municipalities in the 2026 cycle and require an independent assessment by 2030 before broader adoption") are actionable. The conclusion should also identify what would change the analyst's mind — the discipline of stating one's own falsifying conditions. This is what informed citizens do that ideologues do not.

  • Analysis — taking evidence apart to identify patterns, trends, and relationships.
  • Evaluation — judging evidence and conclusions against explicit criteria.
  • Criteria — the standards against which a policy or argument is judged.
  • Cause-and-effect chain — sequence of causal links from input to outcome.
  • Stakeholder matrix — table of affected parties, direction of impact, magnitude.
  • Steel-manning — engaging the strongest version of an opposing view.
  • Counterargument — explicit acknowledgment and rebuttal of an opposing position.
  • Falsifying condition — what evidence would change the analyst's conclusion.

Worked Example — From Evidence to Conclusion

Question: Should the City of Hamilton install a separated bike lane on Cannon Street?

Step 1 — gather evidence: Hamilton's pilot bidirectional Cannon Street bike lane was opened in 2014 and made permanent in 2018; the city's 2017 evaluation found a 290% increase in cyclists; collisions involving cyclists fell; some businesses reported concerns about parking; the 2018 council vote made the lane permanent. Step 2 — name criteria: safety (cyclists and motorists); local-business impact; accessibility (people with disabilities, including parking for accessible vehicles); equity (who benefits across the city's neighbourhoods); cost. Step 3 — analyse against criteria: safety strongly favours installation (evidence shows reduced collision severity); business impact is mixed (some lost short-term, but several measured-impact studies showed neutral-to-positive longer-term); accessibility requires deliberate design (designated accessible-vehicle pull-ins); equity favours installation (high transit use in adjacent neighbourhoods, but advocates emphasise the lane should connect to a wider network); cost requires phasing. Step 4 — steel-man the opposition: the strongest opposing case is that converting on-street parking to a separated lane shifts the cost onto local businesses without a clear benefit-distribution plan. Step 5 — conclude: based on the criteria and evidence, the analysis supports installation with accessibility design built in and a business-transition support program. Falsifying condition: if the 5-year post-installation business impact study shows sustained loss of local revenue greater than 5%, the conclusion should be revisited.

  • Skipping the criteria step and reaching "I think X" without explicit standards.
  • Listing evidence without analysing it (no pattern, no trend, no comparison).
  • Strawmanning the opposing view instead of steel-manning it.
  • Vague conclusions ("more action needed") instead of specific, actionable ones.
  • Never identifying what evidence would change one's own mind — a sign of motivated reasoning.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.6 — "evaluate evidence and draw conclusions about issues, events, and/or developments of civic importance" and A1.4 — "interpret and analyse evidence, data, and information relevant to their investigations, using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for political inquiry."

📣 3.5 Communicating Conclusions (Op-Ed, Speech, Infographic)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An inquiry without communication does not become civic action. The final stage of political inquiry is to share findings clearly and credibly with an intended audience. Different audiences and purposes call for different forms: an op-ed for a general newspaper readership; a formal speech or deputation for a council; an infographic for social media; a position paper for a decision-maker; a podcast for engaged listeners; a video for visual learners; a Twitter / X / Bluesky thread for rapid public reach. Choosing the form is itself an analytical decision — match form to audience.

Across forms, certain communication discipline applies. State the main claim early and clearly — readers should know what the inquirer concludes within the first 50 words. Support the claim with evidence and explicit reasoning — show how the evidence supports the claim, do not assume it is obvious. Engage the strongest counterargument and explain why your conclusion still holds. Use the right register — formal for a council deputation; conversational for an op-ed; visual for an infographic; informal-but-clear for social media. Cite sources using a recognised system (MLA, APA, Chicago, or hyperlinks for digital). Respect the time or word limit; a 5-minute deputation is 700–900 words spoken, no more; a typical op-ed is 700–1000 words; an infographic should be readable in 30 seconds.

For a formal speech / deputation (council, board, committee): open with respect for the procedural body ("Madam Chair, members of council, thank you"); state your name, role, and the residence/affiliation that connects you to the issue; state your specific ask in one sentence; provide three to four supporting reasons with evidence; close with the ask repeated. The deputation is not a debate — it is a presentation; expect 5 minutes of speaking and possibly 5 minutes of questions. Practice the timing.

For an op-ed: hook the reader in the first sentence with a vivid concrete image or sharp claim; state the policy/civic ask in the second or third paragraph; give two to three argumentative paragraphs, each anchored in evidence (a number, a quotation, an example); engage the strongest counterargument; close with a single sentence the reader will remember. Op-eds are not essays — they are persuasive opinion, with a single argumentative through-line. Local outlets often want a Canadian or local angle; national outlets want broader resonance with a specific local example.

For an infographic: choose three (not more) data points or claims; render each visually with an icon, a number, or a chart; use restricted colour palette (2–3 colours); name sources at the bottom; ensure mobile-readability at small size. Infographics are most powerful when they shift behaviour or perception with a single image — "1 in 3 Canadian municipal elections has turnout below 35%" is a clean infographic claim with one number and one comparison.

Across all forms, a single discipline matters most: clarity. Civic communication that the average reader cannot follow is not democratic — it requires the privilege of specialised training. The job of the civics graduate is to be the person who can communicate complex issues clearly to neighbours, friends, colleagues, and elected representatives. That clarity, multiplied by many citizens, is what a healthy democracy needs.

  • Op-ed — short persuasive opinion piece, typically 700–1000 words.
  • Deputation — formal address to a council or committee, usually 5 minutes.
  • Position paper — one-page summary of problem, evidence, and recommended action.
  • Infographic — visual presentation of data and claims for rapid comprehension.
  • Citation system — formal way to credit sources (MLA, APA, Chicago, or digital links).
  • Audience analysis — identifying who the communication is for and what they need.
  • Call to action — the specific next step the communication asks the audience to take.
  • Plain language — accessible vocabulary and sentence structure for general readers.

Worked Example — Drafting a Five-Minute Council Deputation

Issue: support for a city council motion to install a separated bike lane on Main Street West.

Open (15s): "Madam Chair, members of council, thank you for the opportunity to speak. My name is Sara Patel; I am a Grade 10 student at Westdale Secondary, and I live and bike daily on Main Street West." Ask (10s): "I am here to ask council to vote yes on Item 8 — the separated bike-lane installation pilot on Main Street West." Reason 1 — safety (90s): cite the city's 2023 traffic-safety report showing X cyclist-vehicle collisions on this corridor, the comparable Cannon Street data showing severity reductions after the 2014 separated lane, the school's own data on student commutes. Reason 2 — equity and access (60s): the corridor connects to four schools and three transit stops in some of the city's lower-car-ownership neighbourhoods; bike lanes are an equity issue. Reason 3 — business impact (60s): cite the city's own 2017 Cannon Street post-installation business survey showing neutral-to-positive impact; address the local-business concern directly. Reason 4 — community partnership (30s): the local resident association and the school council have endorsed the proposal; this is community-supported. Close (15s): "I urge council to vote yes on Item 8. Thank you." Total: about 4:45; under the 5-minute limit. Practice with a stopwatch; rewrite to fit.

  • Burying the main claim in the middle or end — state it within the first 50 words.
  • Listing evidence without showing how it supports the claim.
  • Ignoring the strongest counterargument — addressing it makes the conclusion more credible.
  • Choosing the wrong form for the audience (a long essay for a 5-minute council slot).
  • Forgetting the call to action — communication without a clear ask drifts.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.7 — "communicate judgements, decisions, conclusions, predictions, and/or plans of action clearly and logically, using appropriate forms" and A1.9 — "use appropriate terminology when communicating the results of their investigations."
