GLE3O — Advanced Learning Strategies: Skills for Success in Secondary School, Grade 11 Open

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 to 12: Guidance and Career Education (2006, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: GLE3O  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Type: Open  |  Prerequisite: None  |  Discipline: Guidance and Career Education

1. Course Description

This course helps students develop and apply the skills, including critical literacy and numeracy skills, that are necessary for success in secondary school and beyond. Students will examine their own learning styles, identify their interests, skills, and strengths, develop a personal strategy plan for academic and personal success, and explore the connections between learning, communication, and goal-setting. They will learn how to apply learning strategies in their own course of study, how to monitor their own progress, and how to plan a successful pathway through secondary school and into a postsecondary destination. GLE3O is a single-credit Grade 11 Open course typically taken by students who want to strengthen their learning habits as they enter senior-grade course loads — including those who have used board-mandated learning strategies in earlier grades and now wish to build on that work at a more sophisticated level.

3. Overall Goals of the Course

By the end of the course, students will:

  • identify their personal learning strengths, preferences, and areas for growth using a range of self-assessment tools;
  • apply effective reading, writing, numeracy, and information-management strategies in their other courses;
  • apply effective memory, study, and test-taking strategies in their other courses;
  • communicate effectively in oral, written, and interpersonal contexts, including in groups and in digital environments;
  • set personal academic and career-related goals using SMART or equivalent goal-setting frameworks, plan time effectively, and monitor progress;
  • investigate Ontario Secondary School Diploma (OSSD) requirements and postsecondary pathways relevant to their interests.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Learning Skills Inventory & Self-Awareness (A): Knowing how you learn — your preferences, strengths, mindset, and what motivates you — is the foundation on which every other strategy is built.
  • Strategies for Academic Success (B): Reading, writing, numeracy, memory, and study skills are not innate talents; they are learnable habits that improve with the right practice.
  • Communication & Interpersonal Skills (C): Strong communicators — in classrooms, group projects, digital spaces, and conflicts — succeed because they have built deliberate habits, not because of personality.
  • Pathways & Transitions (D): Realistic goal-setting, careful time management, and informed knowledge of OSSD & postsecondary options turn a vague future into a deliberate plan.

5. Strand A — Learning Skills Inventory and Self-Awareness

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Self-Assessment: use a range of self-assessment tools (learning-style inventories, multiple-intelligences quizzes, mindset surveys, motivation profiles, personal strengths inventories) to identify their own learning preferences, strengths, and areas for growth;
  • A2. Metacognition & Mindset: demonstrate an understanding of metacognition (thinking about one's own thinking), of growth versus fixed mindset, and of self-regulated learning, and apply these concepts to their own learning;
  • A3. Motivation & Goal Orientation: analyse what motivates them as learners using established frameworks (Self-Determination Theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness), and use this analysis to set and monitor personal goals.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 use multiple self-assessment tools (e.g., VARK, Kolb Learning Style Inventory, Gardner's Multiple Intelligences, RIASEC) to develop a balanced learner profile;
  • A1.2 identify personal strengths and areas for growth using evidence from coursework, feedback from teachers and peers, and reflection;
  • A1.3 examine the limitations of any single inventory and explain why triangulation across multiple sources produces a more accurate profile;
  • A2.1 define metacognition and describe its three phases (planning, monitoring, evaluating);
  • A2.2 distinguish between growth mindset and fixed mindset (Dweck) and explain how each affects effort and outcome;
  • A2.3 describe the cycle of self-regulated learning (forethought → performance → self-reflection);
  • A2.4 apply metacognitive strategies (e.g., think-alouds, learning journals, error analysis) to their own coursework;
  • A3.1 distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation;
  • A3.2 describe the three psychological needs in Self-Determination Theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness) and identify when each is met or unmet in their school experience;
  • A3.3 distinguish mastery-oriented from performance-oriented goal pursuits and identify which best suits a given learning task.
Cross-curricular focus: Self-awareness work in GLE3O transfers directly to every other subject — the metacognitive habits students build in this unit underpin all subsequent academic strategy work and feed forward into Career Studies (GLC2O), the Individual Pathways Plan (IPP), and senior-grade courses.

6. Strand B — Strategies for Academic Success

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Reading & Information Strategies: apply effective reading strategies (e.g., SQ3R, active reading, annotation, Cornell notes) and information-management strategies to academic texts in their other courses;
  • B2. Writing Strategies: apply structured writing strategies (e.g., thesis development, expository vs persuasive forms, revision protocols) to academic writing tasks;
  • B3. Numeracy Strategies: apply numeracy strategies (e.g., estimation, multi-step problem solving, reading graphs and tables, managing math anxiety) to mathematical and quantitative tasks;
  • B4. Memory & Study Strategies: apply evidence-based memory and study strategies (e.g., spaced repetition, retrieval practice, mnemonic devices, concept mapping) to course content;
  • B5. Test-Taking & Assessment Strategies: apply test-preparation, in-test, and post-test strategies appropriate to different test formats (MC, short answer, essay) and manage test anxiety.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 demonstrate the SQ3R method (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) on a textbook chapter;
  • B1.2 apply active-reading habits including annotation, marginalia, and Cornell-style note-taking;
  • B1.3 use vocabulary-building strategies (root analysis, context inference, glossary use) on academic texts;
  • B1.4 evaluate the credibility of digital information sources;
  • B2.1 distinguish expository from persuasive writing and select the appropriate form for a task;
  • B2.2 develop a thesis statement and supporting paragraphs that argue rather than summarise;
  • B2.3 apply a multi-stage revision protocol distinguishing content edits, structural edits, sentence-level edits, and proofreading;
  • B3.1 apply estimation strategies before computing a final answer;
  • B3.2 break multi-step problems into substeps using a structured approach;
  • B3.3 read and interpret tables and graphs from real-world sources;
  • B3.4 recognise math anxiety and apply specific strategies to manage it (e.g., reappraisal, breathing, retrieval practice);
  • B4.1 distinguish working memory from long-term memory and use techniques (chunking, spaced repetition) that respect working-memory limits;
  • B4.2 apply the testing effect (retrieval practice) to course content;
  • B4.3 use mnemonics and concept maps appropriate to the type of content (factual recall vs conceptual relationships);
  • B5.1 develop a study schedule for a specific upcoming test using backwards planning;
  • B5.2 apply format-specific test-taking strategies (e.g., elimination on MC, structure on essays);
  • B5.3 review returned tests to identify error patterns and use them for the next study cycle.
Anchor research: John Hattie, Visible Learning (effect sizes); Robert Bjork (desirable difficulties); Henry Roediger & Mark McDaniel, Make It Stick; Carol Dweck, Mindset; Barbara Oakley, A Mind for Numbers; Sian Beilock, Choke.

7. Strand C — Communication and Interpersonal Skills

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Communication Styles: identify their own and others' communication styles (passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, assertive) and use assertive communication in academic and personal settings;
  • C2. Active Listening & Non-Verbal Communication: demonstrate active listening and accurate interpretation of non-verbal cues in face-to-face and digital settings;
  • C3. Working in Groups: apply strategies for effective group work, including role clarification, constructive feedback, and conflict resolution;
  • C4. Digital & Cross-Cultural Communication: apply digital communication etiquette and demonstrate cultural sensitivity in diverse classroom and online settings.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 distinguish passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, and assertive communication;
  • C1.2 use the "I-statement" structure to express needs and concerns assertively;
  • C2.1 demonstrate active-listening behaviours (paraphrasing, asking clarifying questions, summarising);
  • C2.2 interpret common non-verbal cues (eye contact, posture, facial expression) with awareness of cultural variation;
  • C3.1 apply group-work strategies (clear roles, shared documents, regular check-ins) to a course project;
  • C3.2 give and receive constructive feedback using a structured protocol (e.g., "two stars and a wish," WWW/EBI, SBI);
  • C3.3 identify their preferred conflict style (avoiding, accommodating, competing, compromising, collaborating) using a Thomas-Kilmann-style framework;
  • C3.4 apply a conflict-resolution process to a low-stakes disagreement;
  • C4.1 apply digital-communication etiquette appropriate to email, text, video conferencing, and discussion forums;
  • C4.2 demonstrate cultural sensitivity in classroom discussions and group work.
Cross-curricular focus: Communication and group skills are recognised by the report card's six Learning Skills and Work Habits — particularly Collaboration, Initiative, and Self-Regulation. The strategies in this strand make those reported skills concrete and improvable.

8. Strand D — Pathways and Transitions

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Goal-Setting: set short-term and long-term goals using SMART or equivalent frameworks and monitor progress;
  • D2. Time Management: apply time-management techniques (backwards planning, time-blocking, Pomodoro) to a typical semester's work;
  • D3. OSSD & Pathways: investigate OSSD requirements and postsecondary destinations (apprenticeship, college, university, workplace) relevant to their interests;
  • D4. Job-Search & Transition Skills: produce a résumé, cover letter, and structured interview answers; reflect on transition planning into Grade 12 and beyond.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 set SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) in academic and personal domains;
  • D1.2 apply backwards planning from a goal date to a present-day next action;
  • D1.3 monitor goal progress using a structured journal, checklist, or app;
  • D2.1 apply time-blocking and the Pomodoro Technique (25-minute focused intervals with breaks) to a study session;
  • D2.2 use a planner (digital or paper) to track assignments, tests, and personal commitments;
  • D2.3 identify their own time-management tendencies (procrastination triggers, peak-energy windows) and adjust;
  • D3.1 describe OSSD requirements (30 credits, 40 community involvement hours, OSSLT, compulsory credits) and identify which they have completed and which are outstanding;
  • D3.2 describe the four post-secondary pathways (apprenticeship, college, university, workplace) and identify one program in each that aligns with their interests;
  • D3.3 identify the role of the Ontario Student Assistance Program (OSAP) and other financial-aid resources;
  • D4.1 produce a Grade 11–appropriate résumé and cover letter;
  • D4.2 prepare structured answers to common behavioural interview questions (using STAR: Situation–Task–Action–Result);
  • D4.3 develop and maintain a digital portfolio that captures evidence of learning and progress;
  • D4.4 develop a transition plan for the move from Grade 11 to Grade 12.
Anchor documents: Creating Pathways to Success (Ontario Ministry of Education, 2013); the Individual Pathways Plan (IPP); Ontario Skills Passport; OSSD policy documents; OUAC and OCAS application portals; Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program (OYAP); Job Bank Canada; Ontario Skills Passport.

11. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprising unit tests, applied strategy tasks (real annotated readings, real study plans, real revision protocols), communication tasks (group-work logs, structured-feedback exchanges), and a pathways portfolio. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprising a Personal Strategy Portfolio (15%) and a Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

12. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, learning-style inventories, mindset surveys, strategy-fit checklists, goal-tracking journals).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., a baseline reading-strategy diagnostic, an early-semester goal-setting check, a draft-stage feedback exchange on a writing piece).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, applied-strategy assignments, final portfolio, final exam).

13. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (text, audio, video, infographics, screencasts), multiple means of engagement (student-selected courses for applied tasks, choice of goal-domain, choice of communication format), and multiple means of expression (written reflection, audio journal, visual concept map, video pitch) to meet the needs of diverse learners. Where appropriate, the course incorporates Indigenous learning frameworks (e.g., the Medicine Wheel as a balanced learner-development model) and recognises that the four-quadrant balance of mental, physical, emotional, and spiritual learning has direct counterparts in self-regulated-learning research.

14. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. GLE3O is the course in which these six Learning Skills are most explicitly the object of instruction — students don't just demonstrate them, they study them and improve them deliberately.
