💰 GLE4O: Unit 4 — Financial Literacy and Independent Living

50/30/20 budgeting on a real Ontario student income; credit scores and the credit life-cycle; OSAP repayment math; renting in Ontario under the RTA; insurance basics; the T1 General, RRSPs, and TFSAs; banking and OHIP; transportation cost comparison

Unit 4: Financial Literacy and Independent Living

You are months away from receiving a first paycheque, an OSAP statement, a rent demand, a tax slip, an OHIP card transition, and a phone bill all in the same week. None of that paperwork is hard once you understand the structure; all of it is overwhelming when you do not. This unit gives you the working math, the Ontario-specific legal frame, and the numerical literacy to walk into adult financial life without surprise. All numbers are current as of 2026 — verify before acting.

💵 4.1 The 50/30/20 Budget on an Ontario Student Income
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A budget is not a restriction. It is a written allocation of finite money to specific categories, made in advance, so that money flows where you have decided rather than where the moment pushes it. Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 50/30/20 rule (All Your Worth, 2005) is the simplest workable framework: 50% of after-tax income to needs (housing, groceries, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments, utilities), 30% to wants (entertainment, dining out, subscriptions, hobbies, gifts), and 20% to savings and debt repayment beyond minimums. The percentages are starting points, not absolute rules; in high-cost cities they bend. The discipline is the allocation in advance, not the specific split.

Real numbers for an Ontario eighteen-year-old in 2026. Ontario's minimum wage rose to $17.20 per hour on 1 October 2024 and indexed-adjusts each October. A 40-hour-per-week minimum-wage job earns \(\$17.20 \times 40 = \$688\) per week gross, \(\$35,776\) per year gross. After federal income tax (15% on the first $55,867 in 2026), provincial income tax (Ontario's bottom bracket is 5.05%), CPP contributions (5.95%), and EI premiums (1.66%), the take-home is approximately $30,000 per year, or about $2,500 per month after deductions. Apply 50/30/20: needs $1,250, wants $750, savings $500. In Toronto, where a single-bedroom apartment averages roughly $2,400 per month (CMHC Rental Market Report, fall 2025), the math fails immediately — $1,250 cannot cover rent. The bend in high-cost cities is roommates (a shared 2BR splits to maybe $1,400 each), parental support, or living with family longer. In smaller Ontario centres (Kingston, Peterborough, Sudbury, Thunder Bay), the same 50/30/20 lands closer to workable — a 1BR averages $1,300–$1,700 in those markets.

The other essential math is the emergency fund. Personal-finance research (Suze Orman, Ramit Sethi, the Canadian Financial Consumer Agency) converges on the same number: 3 months of essential expenses in liquid savings before any other goal. For a $2,500-take-home student with $1,250 of needs, that is roughly $3,750. The function is not investment return — it is buffer against the four-figure surprise (car breakdown, dental emergency, sudden job change, family obligation). The under-savers go into credit-card debt for the surprise at 19.99–22.99% interest; the over-savers leave money idle past the function. The 3-month target is the equilibrium.

Tracking is the practice. Three approaches work. Envelope budgeting (cash in physical envelopes, one per category) is concrete but inconvenient in 2026. Spreadsheet tracking (Google Sheets or Excel; monthly columns; categories as rows) is free and gives full control. App-based tracking (KOHO, Mint Canadian alternative since Mint's 2024 shutdown, Wealthsimple Cash, your bank's app) automates transaction categorisation. All three work; the best system is the one you actually update weekly. The minimum-viable practice is a 15-minute Sunday check: pull up last week's transactions, sort by category, compare to the budget, decide next week's adjustments. Over a year, that practice produces specific spending awareness and protects future you from quiet drift.

  • 50/30/20 rule (Warren and Tyagi, 2005) — needs, wants, savings as 50/30/20 of after-tax income.
  • Ontario minimum wage (Oct 2024) — $17.20/hour; indexes each October.
  • Full-time minimum take-home — about $30,000 net per year, $2,500/month after deductions.
  • Toronto 1BR average (Fall 2025) — ~$2,400/month; small-city Ontario ~$1,300–1,700.
  • Emergency fund — 3 months of essential expenses, liquid.
  • Weekly review — 15 minutes; the single highest-leverage budgeting habit.

Worked Example — Two Real Budgets

Lucas, age 19, in Kingston: Works 30 hours/week at $17.50 (slightly above minimum). Gross \(\$17.50 \times 30 \times 52 = \$27{,}300\)/year; net ≈$23,500/year or $1,960/month. Rents a shared 2BR room for $850. Groceries $300, transit pass $90 (Kingston Transit), phone $40, internet (split with roommate) $30, insurance (tenant) $20 = total needs $1,330 (68% — above 50%, normal for a student). Wants: takeout, gym, streaming, hobbies = $300 (15%). Savings: $330 (17%). Budget is workable; Lucas exceeds 50% needs because rent is structurally high relative to income, but he is saving 17% — close to target. Aisha, age 22, in Toronto: Works full-time at $19/hour entry-level office job. Gross $39,520/year; net ≈$32,800/year or $2,733/month. Shares a 2BR Toronto apartment for $1,500 her share. Groceries $400, TTC pass $158 ($156 monthly Metropass), phone $50, internet $30, insurance $25, OSAP minimum payment $200 (after grace period) = $2,363 needs (86%! — Toronto-rent problem). Wants $200 (7%); savings $170 (6%). Aisha's budget is broken; her three options are: more roommates (3 roommates in 2BR drops her rent to $1,000), move outside Toronto core (room in Etobicoke at $1,200), or higher income (negotiate raise, second job). The arithmetic forces the conversation.

  • Treating 50/30/20 as immutable — adjust for context but stay aware of the deviation.
  • Skipping the emergency fund to "start investing" — the fund is the foundation; investing comes after.
  • Tracking once a month rather than weekly — by month-end, the drift has already happened.
  • Forgetting that minimum-wage take-home is roughly 80% of gross — the gap is real and must be planned.
  • Living "month to month with rent + Uber Eats" — the small leaks compound; a $15 lunch out four times a week is $3,120 a year.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "construct a personal budget using the 50/30/20 framework appropriate to their projected post-secondary or workplace income."

💳 4.2 Credit, Credit Scores, and OSAP
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A credit score is a number between 300 and 900 (in Canada) that summarises your borrowing history into a single comparable risk metric. Two credit bureaus operate in Canada — Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada. Both produce slightly different scores from slightly different data. Each lender pulls one or both when you apply for credit. Generally, scores above 760 are excellent, 725–759 are very good, 660–724 are good, 560–659 are fair, and below 560 are poor. The score affects whether you can get approved for credit, what interest rate you pay, and — increasingly — whether you can rent an apartment, get a phone contract, get certain jobs, or access car insurance at lower rates.

The score is built from five components (FICO/Beacon weightings, used by both Canadian bureaus): payment history (~35%), credit utilisation (~30%), length of credit history (~15%), new credit and inquiries (~10%), credit mix (~10%). The biggest lever for a new adult is payment history: pay on time every time. A single missed payment of more than 30 days reported to the bureau can drop a 750 score by 80–110 points and stays on the file for six years. The second biggest lever is utilisation: the ratio of current balances to credit limits. The widely-recommended target is to keep utilisation below 30% of limit at any reporting date; under 10% is better. Maxing a $500 student credit card produces 100% utilisation and a score drop, even if you pay it off by the due date — the score reports based on the statement balance, not the payment-day balance.

The first credit card matters because length of history matters and only opens by being open. The recommended first card for an Ontario eighteen-year-old: a low-limit student credit card from your primary bank (often called "Student Visa" or "Cash Back for Students"). Use it for one to two small recurring purchases (phone bill, one streaming subscription) set to autopay from the chequing account in full each month. Two years of perfect on-time autopay history at low utilisation builds a foundation score in the 700s by age 21. Do not use the card for purchases you cannot pay off the same month. The going credit-card interest rate in Canada in 2026 is 19.99% to 22.99% APR; carrying a $1,000 balance for a year costs roughly $230 in interest, more than the savings from any cashback rewards.

OSAP — the Ontario Student Assistance Program — is the dominant student-debt vehicle. As of 2026, OSAP funding is a mix of federal Canada Student Loans (about 60% of the loan portion) and provincial Ontario loans (about 40%), plus grants that do not need to be repaid (Canada Student Grant, Ontario Student Grant). Loans accrue no interest during studies, and as of April 2023 federal Canada Student Loans permanently stopped accruing interest after graduation — a structural change. Ontario provincial loans still accrue interest after the six-month grace period at the prime rate (no longer prime + 1% as of November 2022). Repayment starts six months after you stop being a full-time student. A typical four-year-degree borrower in Ontario in 2026 finishes with $20,000–$30,000 in federal+provincial OSAP debt; the monthly repayment on a 9.5-year amortisation is roughly \(\$280-\$420\) per month. The federal Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) reduces or pauses payments when income is low (income threshold roughly $40,000 single in 2026, indexed annually).

Two things to know about credit and OSAP. First, OSAP loans do affect your credit, but only if you default — make minimum payments and the loan is reported as in good standing. Second, OSAP is generally one of the cheapest debt instruments you will ever have access to. Pay it on time, but don't prioritise paying it down ahead of credit-card debt at 22%, payday loans at 47%, or even the missed-emergency-fund target. The math of debt prioritisation almost always says: highest-interest debt first.

  • Credit-score range — Canadian scores 300–900; above 760 excellent, below 560 poor.
  • Two Canadian bureaus — Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada.
  • Five score components — payment history (35%), utilisation (30%), length (15%), new credit (10%), mix (10%).
  • Utilisation target — under 30% of limit at statement date; under 10% is better.
  • Credit-card APR in Canada (2026) — 19.99–22.99% on most cards.
  • OSAP federal interest — zero on Canada Student Loans permanently since April 2023.
  • OSAP Ontario loans — interest at prime rate after 6-month grace; lower than most consumer debt.
  • Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) — federal program reducing payments under income threshold.

Worked Example — Two Credit-Card Paths

Path A: The textbook path. At 18, Hana gets a TD Student Cash Back Visa with a $500 limit. She puts her $50/month phone bill on it, autopaid in full from her chequing account. Two years later (age 20), her score is in the high 700s; utilisation has averaged 10% (she has actually never used more than $100 on the $500 card). She has a clean 24-month on-time history, the only history she has. At 22 she applies for a regular adult Visa, increases her limit to $3,000, and uses it for groceries paid off each month. By 25, her score is 800+, and when she applies for a mortgage at 28 she gets the best available rate. Path B: The cautionary path. At 18, Marc gets the same card. He uses it for things he wants but can't quite afford this month: a Christmas gift, a concert ticket, a trip with friends. By 20 he is carrying a $400 balance against a $500 limit (80% utilisation). He pays the minimum, which on a $400 balance at 22.99% is roughly $25/month; about $7.66 of that is interest. After a missed payment in second-year exams, his score drops 90 points. At 22 his application for an apartment is rejected because the landlord runs credit; he sublets a worse apartment from a friend instead. At 25, the missed payment has fallen off his file but the year of high utilisation cost him in interest about $90. The same card, two outcomes: behaviour, not luck.

  • Treating the credit-card minimum payment as the suggested payment — it is a debt-trap optimised for the bank.
  • Closing your oldest credit card to "simplify" — it shortens history and may raise utilisation; usually keep it open.
  • Applying for many cards at once — each hard inquiry costs 5–10 points; accumulated inquiries signal risk.
  • Believing OSAP repayment ruins your credit — only default ruins it; in-good-standing OSAP is neutral or slightly positive.
  • Confusing OSAP grants (non-repayable) with OSAP loans (repayable); both come in the same disbursement, only loans repay.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "describe the structure of Canadian credit scores, OSAP, and consumer debt; calculate interest on a worked example."

🏠 4.3 Renting in Ontario: Leases and Tenant Rights
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Ontario Residential Tenancies Act, 2006 (RTA) governs the relationship between landlords and tenants in residential rental units in Ontario. Unlike most legal frameworks, the RTA is highly favourable to tenants — Ontario tenants have stronger rights than tenants in most US states or most other Canadian provinces. Knowing those rights is the difference between paying $400 more in rent than you legally owe and not, between getting your last-month's deposit back and not, between an illegal eviction and a lawful one.

The starting framework: every tenancy in Ontario governed by the RTA must use the standard Form 2229E "Residential Tenancy Agreement (Standard Form of Lease)". A landlord who refuses to provide this form in a written lease cannot collect the rent in the same way and the tenant gains certain remedies. The standard lease cannot contain illegal terms — for instance, a clause requiring the tenant to pay for damages caused by ordinary wear, a clause forbidding pets (no-pet clauses are void in Ontario residential tenancies, with narrow condo-board exceptions), or a clause permitting the landlord to enter without proper notice. Always read every line before signing, but understand that illegal terms are simply void — even if you signed, you do not have to honour them.

Critical money rules. The landlord may collect only one month's rent as a deposit, which is legally a "last month's rent" deposit and must be applied to the actual last month of tenancy. Damage deposits and "key deposits in excess of replacement cost" are not legal in Ontario residential tenancies. The landlord must pay interest on the last-month's-rent deposit each year (rate set annually; 2.5% in 2024, currently 2.5% for 2025 — verify each January). Rent increases are capped at the annual Rent Increase Guideline set by the province (2.5% for 2025; new figure published each summer for the following year), with one increase per 12 months and 90 days' written notice required. Units first occupied after 15 November 2018 are exempt from the guideline cap — a structural carve-out that has shaped Toronto and Hamilton rental markets in particular.

Critical eviction rules. A landlord cannot evict a tenant for "no reason." Eviction requires a valid ground under the RTA — non-payment of rent, persistent late payment, illegal activity, damage to property, owner's-own-use of the unit, or similar. The landlord serves a notice (Form N-series; e.g., N4 for non-payment of rent), and if the tenant does not move out, the landlord must apply to the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB) for a hearing. The tenant has the right to attend, contest, and present evidence. Self-help eviction — changing the locks, removing the tenant's belongings, shutting off utilities — is illegal under the RTA and exposes the landlord to substantial fines. "N12" notices (owner's own use) require the landlord to actually move in or have a family member move in for at least 12 months; tenants illegally evicted under bad-faith N12s may apply for damages of up to 12 months of rent.

Practical roommate logistics. If you and roommates are joint tenants on a single lease, you are jointly responsible for the full rent — if a roommate moves out, the landlord can claim the full rent from any one of the remaining tenants. If you and your roommates are separate tenants (each with own lease with the landlord), only your own portion is your responsibility. Subletting requires landlord permission but cannot be unreasonably refused; assigning the lease likewise. The Ontario Tribunals Ontario Portal (tribunalsontario.ca/ltb/) provides forms, hearing schedules, and a tenant-friendly guide. Free legal help is available through Community Legal Clinics (locate one through legalaid.on.ca) and through campus student-legal-services at most Ontario universities.

  • Residential Tenancies Act 2006 (RTA) — Ontario's tenancy framework; tenant-favouring.
  • Standard Form Lease (Form 2229E) — required for all RTA-governed tenancies.
  • One month's deposit only — last-month's-rent; damage deposits are illegal.
  • Rent Increase Guideline 2025 — 2.5%; one increase per 12 months; 90-day notice.
  • Post-15-Nov-2018 carve-out — newer units exempt from the guideline cap.
  • Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB) — adjudicator for tenancy disputes.
  • No-pet clauses void — narrow condo exceptions; the lease clause is unenforceable.
  • Joint vs separate tenants — full vs portion responsibility for rent.

Worked Example — A Lease Read-Through

Sahil is signing a lease for a $1,800/month room in a Toronto house. He reads the lease line by line. Clause 4 says "Tenant will pay first and last month's rent at signing ($3,600)." — legal. Clause 7 says "No pets of any kind allowed." — void under the RTA; he could keep a cat anyway, but he doesn't intend to. Clause 12 says "Tenant agrees to pay $500 damage deposit." — illegal; he calls the landlord and politely explains. Clause 15 says "Landlord may enter the unit at any time." — illegal; under the RTA, entry requires 24-hour written notice except for emergencies. Clause 18 says "Rent may be increased by 10% each January." — void; only the Rent Increase Guideline applies (2.5% in 2025). Sahil agrees to the lease in writing but with three clauses struck out, and the landlord signs an amended version. The struck clauses do not bind him regardless of whether they appear in the lease, but striking them avoids future confusion. Total time: 25 minutes. Money protected over the year if the clauses had been enforced unchallenged: roughly $1,000.

  • Believing the lease is a contract where all clauses bind — illegal clauses are simply void.
  • Paying a "damage deposit" when only last-month's-rent is legal.
  • Letting the landlord refuse pets when the clause is void (note: condos may have legitimate carve-outs).
  • Accepting an "any-time entry" clause without 24-hour written notice protection.
  • Not contesting an N12 — the LTB hearing is the place to contest; failing to attend means the eviction proceeds.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "describe the major provisions of Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act and apply them to a sample lease."

🛡️ 4.4 Insurance Basics: Tenant, Auto, Health
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Insurance is the act of paying a small known cost (the premium) to avoid an unpredictable potentially-catastrophic cost (the claim). The economics are simple — the insurance company prices the premium so that expected payouts plus overhead are less than total premiums collected — but the personal-finance logic is clear: insure against losses you cannot afford to absorb, do not insure against losses you can. For a young Ontario adult, four insurance products warrant attention.

Tenant insurance (also called renter's insurance) covers your possessions if lost in fire, theft, water damage, or other covered events, and — critically — provides personal liability coverage if you accidentally injure someone or damage someone's property. A typical Ontario tenant policy costs $15–$30 per month for $30,000 in contents coverage plus $1 million in personal liability. Many Ontario landlords now require tenant insurance as a lease condition (this requirement is legal, unlike many other lease clauses). Even without the requirement, the math overwhelmingly favours having it: a single bathroom water leak through a downstairs ceiling can produce a liability claim in the tens of thousands. The annual $200–$360 premium is among the highest-leverage spends a young adult makes.

Auto insurance in Ontario is mandatory and expensive. Ontario operates a regulated private insurance market; insurance is mandatory under the Highway Traffic Act and the Compulsory Automobile Insurance Act, with statutory accident benefits in every policy. As of 2026, a young driver (under 25) with full G licence and clean record can expect to pay $3,500–$6,000 per year in the GTA, somewhat less in other Ontario regions. A driver under 21 or with G2 only is at the high end. Key factors that move the premium: age, location (postal code), driving record (every ticket and at-fault collision raises rates for several years), vehicle (newer and high-value vehicles cost more), kilometres driven, and use (commuting vs leisure). Shopping multiple quotes through Ontario brokers can save $500–$2,000 per year on identical coverage. Comparing options annually is one of the most-skipped financial moves.

Health and dental insurance outside of OHIP. OHIP covers physician visits, hospital stays, and most medically-necessary tests (more in §4.6) but does not cover most prescription drugs, dental work, vision, mental-health counselling, physiotherapy, or chiropractic. Most full-time university students are auto-enrolled in their student union's health and dental plan (typical $200–$350 per year, covering prescription drugs at 80%, dental at 60–80% to an annual maximum). Most apprenticeship and trade jobs provide employer benefits packages. First-job entry-level workers should confirm whether benefits apply on day one or after a probation period (often 3 months); if uncovered, an individual plan from Manulife, Sun Life, or Green Shield runs $30–$80 per month depending on coverage. For routine prescription costs and dental cleanings, the math is usually break-even; for one filling or one course of expensive antibiotics, the math favours having coverage.

One commonly-misunderstood product: life insurance. Most eighteen-year-olds do not need life insurance because no one is financially dependent on them. The product is sold aggressively to young people who do not need it (often via "buy now while you're cheap" pitches); the cost is real and the benefit is conditional. The honest rule: if no one's livelihood depends on your income, you do not need life insurance. If you later have children, a spouse who depends on your income, or a mortgage shared with a partner, term life insurance (the cheap kind) becomes useful — but not before. The exception is some long-term disability and critical-illness coverage offered as group benefits — these are often valuable even for single young adults.

  • Insurance principle — small known cost to avoid unpredictable catastrophic cost.
  • Tenant insurance (Ontario) — $15–$30/month for $30,000 contents + $1M liability; often required by lease.
  • Auto insurance (Ontario young driver) — $3,500–$6,000/year GTA full G clean record; shop annually.
  • Student health and dental plan — $200–$350/year; covers drugs, dental, often vision; included in tuition for most full-time students.
  • Individual health plan — $30–$80/month if no employer or student coverage.
  • Life insurance at 18 — usually unnecessary; product oversold to dependents-free young adults.

Worked Example — Why Tenant Insurance Pays

Jenna, 20, pays $22/month for tenant insurance ($264/year) on her $1,400/month room. One night her roommate's pasta water boils over on the stove, runs onto the carpeted floor, soaks through to the downstairs unit, and damages the downstairs tenant's laptop ($1,800), Persian rug ($3,400), and electronics ($2,400) — total claim $7,600 plus restoration costs. The downstairs tenant's insurance pays the downstairs tenant's loss, then sues the upstairs unit for recovery. The roommate's insurance handles the roommate's share. Without tenant insurance, Jenna would face a $7,600 share of liability she cannot pay; with tenant insurance, her policy's $1M liability coverage handles it. Annual cost: $264. Avoided expense: $3,800 (half of the $7,600). Lifetime value of having had the policy: the policy paid for itself fifteen times over in one accident — and tenant insurance is the most reliable financial-protection purchase a renter makes.

  • Skipping tenant insurance because "my roommate said his is enough" — separate policies cover separate people.
  • Buying high-premium auto insurance without shopping competitors — savings are typical and substantial.
  • Letting student health coverage lapse without checking what employer-benefits will cover and when.
  • Buying life insurance at 18 because of a sales pitch — most under-25s do not need it.
  • Confusing insurance with investment — universal-life products are usually expensive insurance plus poor investment; term + index funds is almost always better.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "evaluate insurance products (tenant, auto, health/dental, life) for relevance to an Ontario young adult."

📊 4.5 Taxes, RRSPs, TFSAs, and the T1 General
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every Canadian over 18 with income files a personal income tax return — the T1 General — annually by 30 April (15 June if self-employed; payment still due 30 April). Filing is the act of computing total income from all sources, subtracting allowable deductions, applying tax credits, and reconciling against what was already withheld by employers. The CRA's online My Account service holds your tax slips automatically by mid-March each year; Auto-Fill My Return via certified software (TurboTax, Wealthsimple Tax (free), H&R Block, StudioTax) imports them in one click. A student return with one T4 (employment income), one T2202 (tuition slip), and standard credits takes 20–40 minutes the first time and 10–15 minutes thereafter. The CRA's Community Volunteer Income Tax Program (CVITP) provides free filing for low-income filers; campus tax clinics at most Ontario universities staff CVITP volunteers in March.

The basic structure of the tax computation. Total income includes employment income (T4), self-employment income, interest and dividend income, capital gains, OSAP grants (taxable in some years), and other reportable sources. Deductions (the most common are RRSP contributions, union dues, professional fees, child-care expenses, moving expenses for new students relocating 40+ km, employment expenses) reduce taxable income. Tax is computed on taxable income at federal (15% on the first $55,867 for 2026) and Ontario provincial (5.05% on the first $52,886 for 2026) brackets. Non-refundable tax credits (the basic personal amount of $15,705 federally in 2026, tuition credits from the T2202, donations, medical expenses above the threshold) reduce the calculated tax. Refundable credits (the GST/HST credit, the Canada Workers Benefit, the Ontario Trillium Benefit) are paid out regardless of tax owed. The reconciliation: tax owed minus tax withheld equals refund (positive — pleasant) or balance due (negative — unpleasant). The math, despite intimidating form names, is mostly arithmetic.

Two registered accounts every Canadian eighteen-year-old should know. The TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account) opens the year you turn 18. Contributions are not tax-deductible, but all growth and withdrawals are tax-free forever. Annual contribution room is $7,000 for 2026 (the amount is indexed). Unused room accumulates, so a Canadian who turns 18 in 2026 and contributes nothing has $7,000 of room in 2027 plus that year's allowance. The TFSA is the right home for short-term savings (emergency fund, eventual down-payment fund) and for any taxable-account investing you do as a young adult. The RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) can be opened at any age and contributions reduce current-year taxable income — useful when you start earning enough that you are in a meaningful tax bracket. Annual contribution room is 18% of prior-year earned income up to a 2026 maximum of $32,490, less any pension adjustment. RRSPs are pre-tax money; withdrawals (other than under the Home Buyers' Plan or Lifelong Learning Plan) are taxable as income. For an eighteen-year-old in a 20% effective tax bracket, contributing to an RRSP today saves $0.20 of tax now and pays $0.30+ in tax later in retirement when total income is higher — usually backwards. The young-adult rule: max your TFSA first, RRSP later when your income has risen.

Two more things. Tuition tax credits from the T2202 are non-refundable, carry forward indefinitely, and transfer up to $5,000 federally to a parent or grandparent in the year earned. A first-year university student with $10,000 in tuition will accumulate tax credits worth roughly $1,500 federally and $500 provincially — useful for the first year you have taxable income. Capital gains on TFSA-held investments are tax-free; capital gains on non-registered investments are 50% taxable above thresholds. The investing-account hierarchy for young adults: TFSA first (free), employer-matched RRSP if available (almost certainly worth the match), RRSP next, FHSA (First Home Savings Account, available since April 2023 for prospective first-time home buyers under 18+) where applicable, non-registered taxable last.

  • T1 General — personal income tax return; due 30 April annually.
  • CRA My Account + Auto-Fill — automates most of the routine work.
  • Basic personal amount (2026) — $15,705 federal; you owe federal tax only above this.
  • TFSA contribution room (2026) — $7,000 annually; unused accumulates.
  • RRSP contribution room — 18% of prior-year earned income, max $32,490 for 2026.
  • Young-adult hierarchy — TFSA first, employer-matched RRSP, RRSP later, FHSA, non-registered last.
  • Tuition tax credits (T2202) — carry forward; up to $5,000 transferable to parent in year earned.
  • CVITP (CRA) — free filing for low-income; campus tax clinics in March.

Worked Example — A Student Tax Return

Mira, age 19, in second-year university, files her 2026 taxes in March 2027. Her income: $14,000 in T4 employment from her summer and part-time work. She paid $2,800 in CPP and EI (deducted at source). Her T2202 shows $7,500 in tuition for the year. She made no RRSP contributions, no TFSA withdrawals. Her T1: total income $14,000. Taxable income $14,000 (no deductions). Federal tax owed before credits: \(\$14{,}000 \times 0.15 = \$2{,}100\). Federal non-refundable credits: basic personal amount $15,705, tuition $7,500, CPP/EI ~$1,200 — credits exceed taxable income, so federal tax payable is $0. Ontario tax: similar computation, $0 owed. Provincial tax credits include the Ontario Trillium Benefit (roughly $360/year for low-income singles, paid quarterly). She files using Wealthsimple Tax (free) in 25 minutes. Refund: the federal and provincial taxes withheld from her T4 (~$1,100) plus the OTB. Refund cheque arrives within 2 weeks of e-filing: roughly \(\$1{,}460\). She uses $700 to top up her emergency fund and contributes $760 to her TFSA in a Wealthsimple all-equity portfolio. The 25-minute tax-filing exercise produced $1,460 of cash flow plus future tax-free growth on the TFSA investment. Tax-credit carry-forward: she has roughly $6,000 of unused tuition credits to apply against future tax bills.

  • Not filing because "I didn't earn enough" — even with $0 owed, filing triggers the GST credit and Trillium Benefit.
  • Maxing RRSP at 18 when your tax rate is 0% — you save no current tax and pay tax later; the math is backwards.
  • Using TFSA room for ultra-conservative high-interest savings only — the tax-free growth shelter is more powerful for invested funds you won't touch for 5+ years.
  • Forgetting to transfer up to $5,000 of tuition credit to a parent in the year earned — only that year's credit can be transferred; carried-forward credits cannot.
  • Paying an accountant or H&R Block for a simple student return — Wealthsimple Tax is free and works.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "demonstrate knowledge of basic Canadian personal income tax, registered accounts (TFSA, RRSP), and the T1 General form."

🏦 4.6 Banking, OHIP, and Transportation Choices
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Three smaller-but-essential systems round out the financial-and-independent-living unit. Banking first. The Canadian banking market is dominated by the "Big Five" (RBC, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, CIBC), with significant share from National Bank, the credit-union sector (Meridian, DUCA, Servus), and digital-only banks (Tangerine, Simplii, EQ Bank). Most chequing accounts are free for students with a valid student card (no monthly fee for 4–6 years); after that, monthly fees range $4–$17/month at a Big Five branch with packages, $0 at most digital banks. The right starter setup for an Ontario eighteen-year-old: one no-fee chequing account (digital like Tangerine or a Big Five student account), one high-interest savings account at a digital bank (EQ Bank and Wealthsimple Cash currently pay 2.5–3.5% on demand savings depending on rate environment), and one investing account (Wealthsimple, Questrade, or your bank's brokerage). Maintain only what you use. The fee-creep on neglected accounts is real — a $4/month fee paid for ten years on an unused account is $480.

Two banking concepts every adult should grasp. Compound interest works in both directions. On your savings, money grows by the formula \(A = P(1+r)^n\): $1,000 invested at 7% real return for 40 years becomes \(\$1{,}000 \times 1.07^{40} \approx \$14{,}974\). On your debt, the same formula runs against you: a $5,000 credit-card balance at 20% APR carried for 5 years if only the minimum is paid balloons to roughly $9,500 if reduced to zero by paying $200/month for ~33 months. Compound interest is the most powerful financial fact for a teenager: every dollar saved at 18 is worth roughly 15 dollars at 58 in long-term equity markets. The corollary: every dollar of high-interest debt at 18 is roughly equally costly. The interaction explains why the "no debt + max savings" mantra is so often repeated.

OHIP (Ontario Health Insurance Plan) covers most medically-necessary services for Ontario residents — physician visits, hospital stays, emergency care, surgeries, most tests, mental-health services through a physician, prenatal and obstetric care, and (since 2020) virtual care from registered physicians. Your OHIP card at 16 (the photo card replacing the old red-and-white card) remains valid into adulthood. Renew your card by the expiry date — typically every 5 years — and notify ServiceOntario of any address change. What OHIP does not cover: most prescription drugs (covered by the Ontario Drug Benefit at age 24 and under if low-income through OHIP+ until 24, otherwise by employer or student plans), most dental care, vision (except in specific medical circumstances), most physiotherapy, chiropractic, registered massage therapy, and most mental-health counselling provided by registered psychotherapists rather than physicians. Outside-Canada travel is largely not covered — get travel insurance for any trip. If you study out-of-province for under one year, OHIP travels with you; longer than one year, you may need to switch provincial coverage.

Transportation choices have huge long-term financial impact. The Canadian Automobile Association's Driving Costs Calculator estimates the full cost of operating a small car in Ontario at $10,000–$13,000 per year (depreciation, insurance, fuel, maintenance, parking, financing) for a relatively new vehicle driven 15,000 km annually. A monthly Toronto TTC pass is $156 in 2026, or $1,872 annually. A Hamilton or Ottawa transit pass is roughly $115/month. A bicycle plus winter transit pass in many Ontario cities covers transportation for under $2,000/year. The cost difference of \(\$10{,}000 - \$2{,}000 = \$8{,}000\) per year, invested at 7% real return, becomes about $170,000 over 40 years (using the compound formula above). The decision "do I own a car?" is one of the largest single financial decisions a young adult makes. The honest answer: in dense urban Ontario (downtown Toronto, Mississauga, Hamilton, Ottawa), car ownership is generally not financially rational for a young adult; transit, cycling, walking, occasional Uber, and car-share (Communauto, Zipcar) cover most needs. In suburban or rural Ontario, the calculation differs because transit is sparse and a vehicle becomes necessary for work and groceries. The point is to make the choice consciously with the numbers in front of you.

  • Big Five banks vs digital banks — Big Five branch convenience vs digital no-fee accounts.
  • Compound interest formula — \(A = P(1+r)^n\); works for savings and debt.
  • 40-year multiplier at 7% — $1 today is worth roughly $15 in 40 years.
  • OHIP coverage — physician, hospital, emergency, tests; does not cover most drugs, dental, vision, paramedical.
  • OHIP+ to age 24 — prescription drug coverage for low-income Ontarians up to 24.
  • Car ownership annual cost — $10,000–$13,000 Ontario small car CAA estimate.
  • Transit pass annual cost — TTC $1,872; Hamilton/Ottawa ~$1,380.
  • 40-year compounding cost of car — roughly $170,000 of foregone investment growth.

Worked Example — The Car Question

Devon, 19, is about to start a job in downtown Toronto. He has a savings account with $4,500, expects to earn $42,000 gross in his first year, and is deciding whether to buy a used 2018 Honda Civic for $14,000 (financed) or to use the TTC. Option A — Buy the car: $14,000 financed over 60 months at 8% = $284/month loan payment. Insurance for a 19-year-old urban driver: $5,200/year ($433/month). Fuel and maintenance: $200/month. Parking near apartment (downtown): $200/month. Total: \(\$284 + \$433 + \$200 + \$200 = \$1{,}117/month\). Annual: $13,400. Option B — Use TTC + occasional Uber: Monthly Metropass $156 + ~$50 in occasional Ubers = ~$206/month or $2,470/year. Difference: $10,930/year. If Devon invests the $911/month difference at 7% real return for 10 years (until he might genuinely need a car for a suburban life), it grows to about $158,000. The decision compounds. Devon's specific situation — downtown apartment, downtown job, no kids, no rural family obligations — clearly favours Option B. If he were instead working in suburban Vaughan or rural Eastern Ontario, the calculation would reverse: there, transit is sparse, work is car-dependent, and the implicit cost of not having a car (refused jobs, taxi reliance, missed appointments) would tip the math. The point is the conscious calculation, not the universal answer.

  • Letting bank fees compound on unused accounts — close what you don't use.
  • Treating high-interest savings as "good enough investing" — it preserves purchasing power roughly; long-term wealth requires equity exposure.
  • Treating "I want a car" as a need rather than a want — the math often favours alternatives in dense urban Ontario.
  • Letting OHIP coverage lapse on a long out-of-province move without checking new-province requirements.
  • Buying travel insurance only for international trips — OHIP coverage gaps in other provinces apply too.
