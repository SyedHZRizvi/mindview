CLU3M — Understanding Canadian Law, Grade 11 University/College Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Canadian and World Studies (2015, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CLU3M  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: None (open course)

1. Course Description

This course explores Canadian law, with a focus on legal issues that are relevant to the lives of people in twenty-first-century Canada. Students will gain an understanding of laws relating to rights and freedoms in Canada; our criminal, civil, and family law; and employment and tort law. Students will develop legal-reasoning skills and will apply the concepts of legal thinking and the legal-studies inquiry process when investigating a range of legal issues to determine how they affect society. Students will explore why laws change and the role of citizens in supporting legal change.

3. Concepts of Legal Thinking

ConceptApplication in CLU3M
Legal SignificanceDetermining the importance, lasting impact, and historical weight of a law, court decision, or legal change (e.g., the patriation of the Constitution, R v Oakes).
Continuity & ChangeExamining what aspects of the legal system have endured (common-law tradition, presumption of innocence) and what has shifted (Indigenous legal recognition, same-sex marriage, cannabis legalisation).
Cause & ConsequenceTracing why a law was enacted (e.g., YCJA replacing the YOA) and the intended and unintended consequences for individuals and society.
Legal PerspectiveRecognising that different groups (Crown, accused, victim, Indigenous nations, marginalised communities) may interpret the same law or case differently and that perspective shapes legal argument.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Legal Inquiry & Citizenship Skills: Applying the legal-studies inquiry process — framing legal questions, gathering and weighing evidence, evaluating arguments — develops critical thinking and informed citizenship.
  • Strand B — Heritage of Canadian Law: Canada's legal system inherits from multiple traditions (Indigenous legal orders, ancient codes, British common law, French civil law) and is shaped by the Constitution Acts of 1867 and 1982.
  • Strand C — Rights and Freedoms: The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms protects individual and collective rights, balanced against reasonable limits and the public interest, and has reshaped Canadian society since 1982.
  • Strand D — Criminal Law and Procedure: Criminal law defines wrongs against society, prescribes procedures for fair adjudication, and balances the rights of the accused with the protection of the public.
  • Strand E — Regulation and Dispute Resolution: Civil law, family law, employment law, and alternative dispute resolution govern private relationships and provide mechanisms for resolving conflicts without recourse to criminal sanction.

5. Strand A — Legal Inquiry and Citizenship Skills (embedded across all strands)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Legal Inquiry: use the legal-studies inquiry process and the concepts of legal thinking when investigating issues, events, and developments in Canadian law;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills, including spatial-literacy skills, developed through investigations related to Canadian law, and identify careers in which a background in legal studies might be useful.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in Canadian law;
  • A1.2 select and organize relevant evidence and information on legal issues from a variety of primary and secondary sources;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations (e.g., bias, perspective, expertise);
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence and information relevant to their investigations, using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for legal inquiry (e.g., IRAC, case-brief format);
  • A1.5 use the concepts of legal thinking (legal significance, continuity and change, cause and consequence, legal perspective) when analysing, evaluating evidence about, and formulating conclusions and/or judgements regarding issues, events, and/or developments in Canadian law;
  • A1.6 evaluate and synthesize their findings to formulate conclusions and/or make informed judgements or predictions about the issues, events, and/or developments they are investigating;
  • A1.7 communicate their ideas, arguments, and conclusions using various formats and styles, as appropriate for the audience and purpose;
  • A2.1 describe ways in which legal studies has helped them develop skills, including the essential skills in the Ontario Skills Passport, that they can apply in everyday life.
Skill focus: Case-brief writing (IRAC), legal-citation conventions, statutory interpretation, mooting and persuasive legal argument, evaluating media reporting of legal cases.

6. Strand B — Heritage of Canadian Law (Chapter 1)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Sources and Principles: analyse historical and philosophical sources of Canadian law, and the contributions of various people, groups, and ideas to Canadian law;
  • B2. Development of Canadian Law: analyse how the rule of law and key principles of justice have developed in Canada;
  • B3. Structure and Operation: analyse the structure and operation of the Canadian legal system, with reference to the division of powers and the role of various legal actors.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 analyse contributions to the heritage of Canadian law from a variety of sources (e.g., Indigenous legal traditions, the Code of Hammurabi, the Mosaic Code, Roman law, Greek philosophy, the Magna Carta);
  • B1.2 analyse the influence of various people on the development of Canadian law (e.g., Sir John A. Macdonald, Pierre Trudeau, Bertha Wilson, Beverley McLachlin, Indigenous legal scholars);
  • B1.3 explain key concepts and principles related to Canadian law (rule of law, equality before the law, due process, presumption of innocence, judicial independence);
  • B2.1 describe how the rule of law has developed in Canada and how it is reflected in the structure and operation of the legal system;
  • B2.2 explain the relationship between common law and statute law and how each is created and applied;
  • B3.1 describe the division of powers between federal and provincial governments as set out in sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867;
  • B3.2 explain the structure of the Canadian court system (provincial courts, superior courts, courts of appeal, Supreme Court of Canada);
  • B3.3 describe the roles of various legal actors (judges, lawyers, Crown attorneys, paralegals, justices of the peace, jurors).
Anchor sources: Constitution Act, 1867 (ss. 91–92); Constitution Act, 1982; Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 217 (rule of law); the Royal Proclamation of 1763; Indigenous legal traditions (Anishinaabe, Cree, Haudenosaunee).

7. Strand C — Rights and Freedoms (Chapter 2)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Charter Rights and Freedoms: analyse the rights and freedoms protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms;
  • C2. Human-Rights Issues: analyse human-rights issues in Canada and the legal mechanisms used to address them;
  • C3. Legal Change: analyse how the law evolves in response to changing social, political, and economic circumstances.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 describe the rights and freedoms protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (fundamental, democratic, mobility, legal, equality, official-language, and minority-language education rights);
  • C1.2 explain the role of section 1 of the Charter (reasonable limits) and the test established in R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103;
  • C1.3 explain the role of section 33 of the Charter (the notwithstanding clause) and analyse situations in which it has been invoked;
  • C1.4 analyse landmark Charter cases (R v Big M Drug Mart, R v Morgentaler, Vriend v Alberta, Carter v Canada);
  • C2.1 describe the role of the Ontario Human Rights Code and the Canadian Human Rights Act in protecting against discrimination;
  • C2.2 analyse the duty to accommodate and identify protected grounds under human-rights legislation;
  • C3.1 analyse how laws change in response to social movements (women's equality, LGBTQ2S+ rights, Indigenous rights, disability rights).
Anchor cases: R v Oakes [1986]; R v Big M Drug Mart [1985]; R v Morgentaler [1988]; Vriend v Alberta [1998]; Carter v Canada (AG) [2015]; Ford v Quebec (AG) [1988] (s. 33).

8. Strand D — Criminal Law and Procedure (Chapter 3)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Criminal Offences: analyse the legal elements of a crime and the categories of criminal offences in Canada;
  • D2. Criminal Procedure: analyse the procedural steps in a criminal trial, from arrest to sentencing or acquittal;
  • D3. Youth Criminal Justice: analyse the legal framework for youth in conflict with the law under the Youth Criminal Justice Act.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 explain the two essential elements of a crime — actus reus (the guilty act) and mens rea (the guilty mind) — and their components;
  • D1.2 distinguish between summary-conviction offences, indictable offences, and hybrid offences;
  • D1.3 analyse the legal definitions of selected Criminal Code offences (assault, theft, fraud, homicide);
  • D2.1 describe police powers of arrest, detention, and search, and the corresponding rights of the accused under sections 7–14 of the Charter;
  • D2.2 describe the criminal trial process, from bail hearing through plea, trial, verdict, and sentencing;
  • D2.3 analyse the role and types of defences (justifications, excuses, mental disorder, automatism, self-defence, intoxication);
  • D2.4 analyse the principles and purposes of sentencing under section 718 of the Criminal Code (denunciation, deterrence, rehabilitation, reparation);
  • D3.1 explain the principles and procedures of the Youth Criminal Justice Act (YCJA) and contrast it with adult criminal procedure.
Anchor cases / statutes: R v Hill [1986] (mens rea); R v Lavallee [1990] (self-defence / battered-woman syndrome); R v Gladue [1999] (Indigenous sentencing); Criminal Code of Canada (RSC 1985, c. C-46); Youth Criminal Justice Act (SC 2002, c. 1).

9. Strand E — Regulation and Dispute Resolution (Chapter 4)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. Tort Law: analyse the principles of tort law and the legal remedies available for civil wrongs;
  • E2. Contract and Consumer Law: analyse the formation and enforcement of contracts and the legal protections afforded to consumers;
  • E3. Family and Employment Law: analyse the legal framework governing family relationships and employment;
  • E4. Dispute Resolution: analyse mechanisms of alternative dispute resolution (negotiation, mediation, arbitration) and the role of administrative tribunals.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 distinguish between intentional torts (assault, battery, trespass, defamation) and negligence;
  • E1.2 analyse the elements of negligence (duty of care, standard of care, causation, damages) as set out in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932];
  • E1.3 explain the role of damages and the calculation of compensation in tort claims;
  • E2.1 identify the essential elements of a valid contract (offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, legality, intention to create legal relations);
  • E2.2 analyse consumer-protection legislation (Ontario Consumer Protection Act, 2002) and unconscionable contracts;
  • E3.1 describe the legal framework for marriage, divorce, and the division of property under the Family Law Act and the federal Divorce Act;
  • E3.2 analyse employment standards under the Ontario Employment Standards Act, 2000 and the law of wrongful dismissal;
  • E4.1 compare and contrast the methods of alternative dispute resolution (ADR): negotiation, mediation, arbitration;
  • E4.2 describe the structure and role of administrative tribunals (Landlord and Tenant Board, Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario, Workplace Safety and Insurance Appeals Tribunal).
Anchor sources: Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562; Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. [1893] (offer/acceptance); Ontario Family Law Act (RSO 1990, c. F.3); Ontario Employment Standards Act, 2000; Ontario Consumer Protection Act, 2002.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, case briefs, position papers, moot court / role-play assessments, and in-class participation. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of the Independent Legal Inquiry / Position Paper (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice case briefs, peer-feedback on argument paragraphs, terminology self-quizzes, reflection on inquiry process).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., diagnostic case-brief, prereq civics check, position-paper proposal feedback).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, formal case briefs, position papers, moot performances, final exam).

14. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

The course offers multiple means of representation (textual case excerpts, audio summaries, video re-enactments, courtroom site visits), multiple means of engagement (student-selected case for the Independent Legal Inquiry, choice of moot side, choice of position-paper topic), and multiple means of expression (essay, podcast, moot performance, infographic legal-info bulletin) to meet the needs of diverse learners and to honour the principles of Equity and Inclusive Education in Ontario Schools.

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CLU3M for sustaining the multi-week Independent Legal Inquiry, for collaborative moot work, and for ethical handling of sensitive case material.
