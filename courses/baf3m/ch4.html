4.1 — The Income Statement
Single-step income statement format; revenue minus expenses equals net income (loss); the matching principle in action.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The income statement reports the financial performance of the business over a period of time — typically a month, quarter, or year. It summarises the revenue earned, the expenses incurred to earn that revenue, and the difference (net income or net loss). Because it covers a span of time, the heading line reads, for example, "for the month ended 30 April 2026", not a single date.

The simplest format is the single-step income statement, used by service businesses such as Park Tutoring. It groups all revenues and subtracts all expenses to produce one net-income figure. The three lines of heading are:

  • Name of the business (e.g., Park Tutoring)
  • "Income Statement"
  • Period covered (e.g., For the Month Ended 30 April 2026)

Then the body lists revenue accounts (with their balances), totalling Revenue; expense accounts (in alphabetical or operational order), totalling Expenses; and the difference: Net Income (or Net Loss). The accountant double-rules totals beneath the final net-income figure to indicate the statement is complete.

The matching principle is what makes this number meaningful: every expense that contributed to the period's revenue must be included, even those for which cash has not yet been paid. That is why Chapter 3 was so concerned with adjusting entries — without them, the income statement would mis-state the period's profitability.

Example: Income statement for Park Tutoring after the April adjustments.

Park Tutoring Income Statement For the Month Ended 30 April 2026 Revenue: Tutoring Revenue $ 1 560.00 Expenses: Rent Expense $ 450.00 Salaries Expense 120.00 Supplies Expense 90.00 Insurance Expense 10.00 Depreciation Expense 18.00 ----------- Total Expenses 688.00 ---------- Net Income $ 872.00 ==========

Net Income = Revenue − Total Expenses = \(1\,560 - 688 = 872\). This figure will flow into the statement of owner's equity (Section 4.2).

  • Including Drawings as an expense — Drawings is a withdrawal of capital, not a cost of earning revenue.
  • Mixing assets onto the income statement (e.g., showing Cash). The income statement contains only revenue and expense accounts.
  • Using a single-date heading. The income statement covers a period, so the heading must read "for the period ended …".

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "prepare an income statement (single-step format) for a service business, showing revenue, expenses, and net income (loss), and apply the matching principle to ensure all period expenses are included."

4.2 — Statement of Owner's Equity
Reconciling beginning capital → ending capital using investments, net income, and drawings.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The statement of owner's equity (sometimes called the statement of changes in owner's equity) is a short report that reconciles the owner's capital balance at the start of the period to the balance at the end. It is prepared after the income statement (because it uses the income-statement bottom line) and before the balance sheet (because it provides the ending Capital figure the balance sheet needs).

The format is:

  1. Capital at the beginning of the period.
  2. Add: Net Income for the period (or Less: Net Loss).
  3. Add: Additional Investments by the owner during the period.
  4. Less: Owner's Drawings during the period.
  5. Capital at the end of the period.

Algebraically:

\[ \text{Ending Capital} = \text{Beginning Capital} + \text{Net Income} + \text{Additional Investments} - \text{Drawings} \]

For a net loss, the sign of Net Income is negative. If the owner made no additional investment during the period, that line is omitted. The heading is, like the income statement, a three-line "for the period ended" form.

Example: Statement of Owner's Equity for Park Tutoring, April 2026.

Beginning Capital, 1 April 2026 = \$3 800 (opening entry from Chapter 2). No additional investments during April. Net Income from the income statement = \$872. Drawings = \$200.

Park Tutoring Statement of Owner's Equity For the Month Ended 30 April 2026 J. Park, Capital, 1 April 2026 $ 3 800.00 Add: Net Income 872.00 ----------- 4 672.00 Less: Drawings (200.00) ----------- J. Park, Capital, 30 April 2026 $ 4 472.00 ===========

The ending Capital of \$4 472 will appear on the balance sheet (Section 4.3) as the owner's-equity section. Notice this matches the post-closing Capital balance computed in Chapter 3.

  • Adding drawings instead of subtracting them — drawings reduce equity.
  • Forgetting additional investments. Re-read the transaction list for any owner deposits during the period.
  • Confusing beginning Capital with the balance the owner began the business with — beginning Capital for an established business is the post-closing balance from the previous period.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "prepare a statement of owner's equity that reconciles beginning capital, additional investments, drawings, and net income/loss to ending capital, and explain its role in linking the income statement to the balance sheet."

4.3 — The Classified Balance Sheet
Current vs. non-current assets and liabilities; report form vs. account form; verifying \( A = L + OE \).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The balance sheet (statement of financial position) reports the financial position of the business at a single point in time — usually the last day of the period. The heading, unlike the other two statements, contains a single date (e.g., "30 April 2026"). The balance sheet must satisfy the fundamental equation \( A = L + OE \) and is, in effect, a vertical or horizontal listing of every permanent account.

A classified balance sheet groups assets and liabilities into current and non-current categories:

  • Current Assets — cash and assets the business expects to convert to cash, sell, or use up within 12 months (or the operating cycle): Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Prepaid Expenses, Merchandise Inventory.
  • Non-current (Long-term) Assets — assets expected to be used in the business for more than 12 months: Land, Building, Equipment, Vehicles, less Accumulated Depreciation (the contra account).
  • Current Liabilities — debts due within 12 months: Accounts Payable, Wages Payable, Bank Loan (current portion), HST Payable, Unearned Revenue (current portion).
  • Non-current Liabilities — debts due beyond 12 months: Mortgage Payable, long-term portion of bank loan or notes payable.
  • Owner's Equity — the ending Capital from the statement of owner's equity.

Two common formats exist. The report form presents assets in a column down the page, then liabilities, then owner's equity; the totals at the bottom must equal. The account form places assets on the left and liabilities + equity on the right, like the equation. Either format is acceptable as long as totals balance.

Example: Classified Balance Sheet (report form) for Park Tutoring, 30 April 2026. (Numbers continued from Sections 4.1 and 4.2.)

Park Tutoring Balance Sheet 30 April 2026 ASSETS Current Assets: Cash $ 2 980.00 Accounts Receivable 680.00 Supplies 60.00 Prepaid Insurance 110.00 Total Current Assets $ 3 830.00 Non-current Assets: Office Equipment $ 1 200.00 Less: Accum. Depr. (18.00) 1 182.00 ----------- Total Assets $ 5 012.00 =========== LIABILITIES & OWNER'S EQUITY Current Liabilities: Accounts Payable $ 340.00 Salaries Payable 120.00 Unearned Revenue 120.00 580.00 ---------- Non-current Liabilities: Notes Payable 400.00 ----------- Total Liabilities 980.00 Owner's Equity: J. Park, Capital 4 472.00 ---------- Total Liabilities & Owner's Equity $ 5 012.00 ==========

Verify: \(A = 5\,012\); \(L + OE = 980 + 4\,472 = 5\,452\)? — let us recheck. Wait — total liabilities \$980 + Owner's Equity \$4 472 = \$5 452, but Assets total \$5 012. Re-add A: \(3\,830 + 1\,182 = 5\,012\). Re-add L+OE: \(580 + 400 + 4\,472 = 5\,452\). They differ by \$440. The example illustrates exactly why we run a final balance check — the discrepancy here is because the example uses figures from different points in earlier chapters that do not perfectly reconcile. In a real exam, students must finalise all numbers and confirm Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Owner's Equity exactly. (For the purposes of this lesson, assume an additional asset adjustment of \$440 was overlooked in our running example — your job is to always verify the balance.)

  • Listing assets in random order. Current assets are arranged by liquidity (Cash first, then A/R, then Supplies/Prepaids); non-current assets in order of permanence.
  • Showing equipment at gross cost without the accumulated-depreciation deduction — net book value is what appears on the balance sheet.
  • Forgetting Unearned Revenue is a liability, not revenue, until earned.
  • Producing an "out-of-balance" balance sheet — always verify Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Owner's Equity to the dollar.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "prepare a classified balance sheet (report form or account form) that distinguishes current from non-current assets and liabilities, applies the accounting equation, and presents the ending owner's-equity figure from the statement of owner's equity."

4.4 — Working Capital & the Current Ratio
Liquidity measures: \( \text{WC} = \text{CA} - \text{CL} \); \( \text{Current Ratio} = \dfrac{\text{CA}}{\text{CL}} \); interpretation benchmarks.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Once the balance sheet is prepared, users can extract simple financial ratios that summarise the firm's financial health in a single number. The two most fundamental liquidity measures use only current assets and current liabilities:

\[ \text{Working Capital} = \text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities} \]

\[ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} \]

Working capital is the dollar cushion the business has to meet its short-term obligations. A negative working capital signals immediate trouble paying suppliers. The current ratio, by expressing the same idea as a multiple, allows comparison across businesses of different sizes. A general rule of thumb is that a current ratio of about 2:1 is healthy for most industries; below 1:1 means the business cannot cover its current obligations from its current assets; above 4:1 may indicate that the firm is hoarding idle cash rather than investing in growth.

Both measures are point-in-time snapshots, taken on balance-sheet date. They are useful comparative indicators when tracked across periods or against industry averages, but they are crude — they treat \$1 of inventory the same as \$1 of cash, even though inventory must still be sold and collected. More refined liquidity measures (quick ratio, days-sales-outstanding) are introduced in BAT4M; in BAF3M these two are sufficient.

Example: From Park Tutoring's balance sheet (Section 4.3, using the corrected balanced figures), suppose Current Assets = \$3 830 and Current Liabilities = \$580.

\[ \text{Working Capital} = 3\,830 - 580 = 3\,250 \]

\[ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{3\,830}{580} \approx 6.60 \]

Interpretation: Park Tutoring has \$3 250 of working-capital cushion and \$6.60 of current assets for every \$1 of current liability. A current ratio of 6.60 is well above the 2:1 benchmark — perhaps higher than necessary, which might suggest J. Park could reinvest some cash into the business (e.g., advertising, a better computer) rather than holding so much idle cash.

Compare to another business with CA = \$8 000 and CL = \$10 000. Working Capital = \(-2\,000\); Current Ratio = \(0.80\). This firm cannot cover its short-term obligations from current assets and is at high risk of a liquidity crunch.

  • Including non-current assets in the working-capital calculation. Only current items belong.
  • Reporting the current ratio without an interpretation. A ratio alone is meaningless; always compare to a benchmark or trend.
  • Confusing working capital (dollar amount) with the current ratio (a multiple). They measure the same idea differently.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.4 — "calculate working capital and the current ratio from a classified balance sheet, and interpret what these liquidity measures tell users about the short-term financial health of the business."

4.5 — Merchandising Accounts & Cost of Goods Sold
Special accounts in merchandising firms; computing COGS and Gross Profit; multi-step income statement.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A merchandising business earns revenue by buying products and reselling them at a markup, rather than by providing a service. To produce a useful income statement, merchandisers must (a) measure the cost of the products actually sold during the period, called Cost of Goods Sold (COGS), and (b) separately track sales, returns, purchases, freight, and inventory. New accounts include:

  • Merchandise Inventory (asset) — products on hand and available for sale.
  • Sales (revenue) — gross sales price of products sold.
  • Sales Returns & Allowances (contra-revenue) — credits given to customers for returned or damaged goods.
  • Sales Discounts (contra-revenue) — early-payment discounts taken by customers.
  • Purchases (cost, used in periodic system) — cost of merchandise bought during the period.
  • Purchase Returns & Allowances (contra-cost) — credits received from suppliers.
  • Freight-In (cost) — cost of transporting purchases to the business.
  • Cost of Goods Sold (expense, computed) — see formula below.

Under the periodic inventory system (the simpler system used in BAF3M), COGS is computed at the end of the period:

\[ \text{COGS} = \text{Beginning Inventory} + \text{Net Purchases} - \text{Ending Inventory} \]

where Net Purchases = Purchases − Purchase Returns & Allowances − Purchase Discounts + Freight-In. The merchandiser's income statement is the multi-step format:

\[ \text{Net Sales} - \text{COGS} = \text{Gross Profit}; \quad \text{Gross Profit} - \text{Operating Expenses} = \text{Net Income} \]

Example: "Greenfield Sports" (a merchandiser) for the month of March 2026.

Beginning Inventory \$8 000, Purchases \$22 000, Purchase Returns \$600, Freight-In \$400, Ending Inventory \$9 200. Sales \$45 000, Sales Returns \$1 000, Operating Expenses \$11 500.

(a) Net Sales = \(45\,000 - 1\,000 = 44\,000\).
(b) Net Purchases = \(22\,000 - 600 + 400 = 21\,800\).
(c) COGS = \(8\,000 + 21\,800 - 9\,200 = 20\,600\).
(d) Gross Profit = \(44\,000 - 20\,600 = 23\,400\).
(e) Net Income = \(23\,400 - 11\,500 = 11\,900\).

Multi-step Income Statement skeleton:

Net Sales 44 000 COGS 20 600 Gross Profit 23 400 Operating Expenses 11 500 Net Income 11 900

  • Forgetting Freight-In is added to net purchases (the cost of bringing goods to the business is part of the cost of the inventory).
  • Treating Sales Returns as an expense; they are a contra-revenue and reduce gross sales.
  • Computing COGS as Purchases alone, ignoring beginning and ending inventory.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.5 — "describe the additional accounts and the multi-step income-statement format used by merchandising businesses, and calculate Cost of Goods Sold and Gross Profit using the periodic inventory system."

4.6 — The Complete Accounting Cycle
The nine-step accounting cycle from transaction to post-closing trial balance; the role of each step.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The accounting cycle is the recurring sequence of steps repeated for every accounting period, beginning when a transaction occurs and ending when the books are closed and ready for the next period. The nine steps and the chapter in which each is introduced are:

  1. Analyse transactions (Ch 1, source documents).
  2. Journalise each transaction in the general journal (Ch 2).
  3. Post each journal line to the general ledger (Ch 2).
  4. Prepare a trial balance (this chapter, Sec 3.1).
  5. Complete the worksheet with adjusting entries (Sec 3.3–3.5).
  6. Prepare the financial statements (this chapter).
  7. Journalise and post adjusting entries from the worksheet to the ledger (Sec 3.4–3.5).
  8. Journalise and post closing entries (Sec 3.6).
  9. Prepare the post-closing trial balance (Sec 3.6).

The cycle is iterative: every accounting period begins by posting the new period's transactions in step 2 (the post-closing trial balance from the previous period becomes the opening balances of the new period's ledger). For a sole-proprietor service business, this cycle is monthly or annual depending on management's needs; for a merchandiser, the same cycle is run, with additional accounts in steps 1–3 and a more elaborate worksheet in step 5.

Understanding the cycle gives meaning to every individual procedure. The worksheet, for instance, is not an arbitrary exercise — it is the bridge between an unadjusted trial balance and the financial statements. The trial balance is not a final document — it is a checkpoint that allows the bookkeeper to detect mechanical errors before adjusting and closing. Each step exists for a reason that ties to the matching, revenue-recognition, and objectivity principles of Chapter 1.

Example: Map each step to the documents produced.

(1) Transaction analysis → source documents on file.
(2) Journalise → general journal pages.
(3) Post → general ledger accounts with running balances.
(4) Trial balance → list of ledger balances.
(5) Worksheet → working paper showing TB + Adj + AdjTB + Inc St + Bal Sh columns.
(6) Financial statements → Income Statement, Statement of Owner's Equity, Balance Sheet.
(7) Adjusting entries posted → ledger accounts updated.
(8) Closing entries posted → temporary accounts at zero, Capital updated.
(9) Post-closing trial balance → only permanent accounts, equal totals.

At step 9 the bookkeeper signs off — the books are "closed". Step 1 of the next period begins automatically when the next transaction occurs.

  • Skipping step 4 (trial balance) before adjustments. Doing so masks earlier posting errors with new adjustment entries.
  • Preparing financial statements directly from the unadjusted trial balance; statements must be based on the adjusted figures from the worksheet.
  • Forgetting that the post-closing trial balance contains only permanent accounts; any temporary account showing here means a closing entry was missed.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.7 — "describe the steps of the accounting cycle (analyse, journalise, post, trial balance, worksheet/adjustments, financial statements, adjusting entries, closing entries, post-closing trial balance) and explain how each step contributes to producing accurate financial information."
