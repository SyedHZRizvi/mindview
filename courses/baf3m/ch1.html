1.1 — Purpose of Accounting & Its Role in Society
Accounting as the "language of business"; primary users (owners, managers, lenders, investors, governments) and the decisions they make.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Accounting is the systematic process of identifying, measuring, recording, summarising, and communicating the financial information of a business so that interested parties can make informed economic decisions. It is often called the "language of business" because every business activity that has a measurable financial effect must eventually be expressed in the standardised vocabulary of debits, credits, accounts, and financial statements. Without accounting, owners would not know whether they were making a profit, lenders would not be willing to advance credit, and governments would have no reliable basis on which to assess income or sales tax. The discipline therefore underpins not only individual firms but the functioning of capital markets and the public sector.

Two broad branches exist. Financial accounting produces general-purpose statements (income statement, balance sheet, statement of owner's equity, statement of cash flows) for external users — investors, banks, the Canada Revenue Agency, and prospective creditors — and is governed by generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) so that one company's results can be compared to another's. Managerial accounting, by contrast, produces customised reports (budgets, variance analyses, product-cost breakdowns) for internal decision-makers and is not bound by GAAP. BAF3M focuses chiefly on the financial-accounting side, because the journals, ledgers, trial balances, and statements you will master form the input to every managerial report.

  • Accounting: the process of identifying, measuring, recording, and communicating financial information.
  • Financial accounting: external reporting under GAAP.
  • Managerial accounting: internal reports for planning, control, and decision-making.
  • Bookkeeping: the mechanical recording phase only — a subset of accounting.
  • External user: investor, creditor, government, customer.
  • Internal user: owner, manager, employee.

Example: A bank is deciding whether to approve a \$60 000 loan to a small retail store.

The loan officer asks for the most recent income statement and balance sheet. She uses the income statement to check whether the store has been profitable enough to make monthly payments and the balance sheet to verify the existence of assets that could secure the loan. She is acting as an external user of financial accounting information. The owner, meanwhile, looks at a managerial-accounting cash budget for the next twelve months to estimate whether the loan payments will fit alongside payroll and rent — an internal use of the same underlying data, but presented in a custom format.

  • Confusing accounting with bookkeeping — bookkeeping is only the mechanical recording step.
  • Believing accounting "produces money". Accounting measures economic activity; it does not create it.
  • Treating all reports as confidential. Public financial statements are deliberately published; only managerial reports are restricted.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "describe the purpose of accounting and explain the role accounting plays in business and society, including its function as the 'language of business' for internal and external users."

1.2 — Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)
Business entity, going concern, cost, time-period, objectivity, revenue recognition, and the matching principle.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

So that one set of financial statements may be compared to another, accountants follow a body of conventions called Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP). In Canada, the GAAP for private enterprises is set by the Chartered Professional Accountants of Canada and published as Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises (ASPE); publicly traded companies follow International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). For BAF3M we focus on seven foundational principles that drive every transaction analysis:

(1) The business-entity principle keeps the financial affairs of the owner separate from those of the business — a personal grocery purchase is never recorded in the company's books. (2) Going concern assumes the business will continue operating long enough to use its assets and pay its liabilities, which is why we record a building at cost rather than at break-up value. (3) The cost principle records assets at the price actually paid, not at later market values, because that price is objective and verifiable. (4) The time-period principle divides the indefinite life of the business into fixed reporting periods (typically months and years). (5) The objectivity principle requires that every entry be supported by a source document. (6) Revenue recognition records revenue when it is earned (delivery of goods or services), not when cash changes hands. (7) The matching principle pairs each period's revenues with the expenses incurred to earn them, producing a meaningful measure of net income.

  • Business-entity: the business is separate from the owner.
  • Going concern: the business will continue indefinitely.
  • Cost principle: record assets at acquisition cost.
  • Time-period: finite reporting periods.
  • Objectivity: entries require verifiable evidence.
  • Revenue recognition: revenue when earned, not when collected.
  • Matching: expenses recognised in the same period as related revenue.

Example: Identify the GAAP that applies in each situation.

(a) The owner of "Riverdale Cleaners" buys a personal vacation laptop and the bookkeeper records it as a company asset. Violation of the business-entity principle.
(b) The shop's delivery van was purchased for \$32 000 in 2024 and its market value today is \$25 000; it remains on the books at \$32 000. Cost principle.
(c) On 30 June, Riverdale delivers cleaning services worth \$1 200; the customer will pay in July. The \$1 200 is recorded as revenue in June. Revenue-recognition principle.
(d) In December, the bookkeeper records December salaries even though employees are paid on 5 January. Matching principle.

  • Recording revenue when cash is received instead of when earned (a cash-basis error that violates revenue recognition).
  • Mixing personal and business transactions (violates business entity).
  • Restating asset values upward each year — Canadian GAAP for most assets still respects the cost principle.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "explain key generally accepted accounting principles (business entity, going concern, cost, time period, objectivity, revenue recognition, matching) and apply them to typical business transactions."

1.3 — Categories of Accounts & Forms of Business Organisation
Assets, liabilities, owner's equity, revenues, expenses, drawings; sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every transaction recorded in accounting affects one or more accounts. An account is simply a label under which all changes of a single type are accumulated. The six categories you must know cold are:

Assets — economic resources the business controls that are expected to produce future economic benefit (cash, accounts receivable, supplies, equipment, land, building). Liabilities — present obligations to outside parties (accounts payable, bank loan, mortgage payable). Owner's Equity — the residual claim of the owner against the assets after liabilities are deducted (Capital). Revenue — the inflow of value from selling goods or providing services (sales, fees earned, service revenue). Expenses — the cost of resources used up while earning revenue (rent expense, salaries expense, utilities). Drawings — withdrawals of cash or other assets by the owner for personal use; they reduce owner's equity but are not an expense. Together, revenue, expenses, and drawings are temporary accounts that are closed at the end of each reporting period; assets, liabilities, and the capital account are permanent and carry their balances forward.

The accounts you create depend on the form of business organisation. A sole proprietorship is owned by one person, easy to set up, unlimited personal liability, and has one Capital and one Drawings account. A partnership has two or more owners and a separate Capital/Drawings account for each. A corporation is a separate legal entity owned by shareholders; owner's equity is split between Common Shares and Retained Earnings, and drawings become dividends. BAF3M focuses primarily on the sole-proprietorship model because it isolates the accounting concepts without legal complications.

  • Asset: resource controlled by the business with future economic benefit.
  • Liability: present obligation to outsiders.
  • Owner's Equity (Capital): owner's residual claim.
  • Revenue / Expense: increases / decreases in owner's equity from operations.
  • Drawings: owner withdrawals (not an expense).
  • Sole proprietorship / Partnership / Corporation: the three legal forms.

Example: Classify each item for "Tessa's Tutoring", a sole proprietorship.

Cash \$3 200 — Asset; Supplies \$180 — Asset; Notebook computer \$1 400 — Asset; Bank loan \$1 000 — Liability; Accounts Payable (textbooks) \$240 — Liability; Tessa, Capital \$3 540 — Owner's Equity; Tutoring Revenue \$1 800 (since opening) — Revenue; Rent Expense \$200 — Expense; Tessa, Drawings \$300 — Drawings (reduces equity, not an expense).

Verify the equation: Assets (\$3 200 + \$180 + \$1 400 = \$4 780) = Liabilities (\$1 000 + \$240 = \$1 240) + Owner's Equity (\$3 540). Then \$4 780 = \$1 240 + \$3 540 ✓.

  • Recording Drawings as an Expense (it is not — it reduces Capital directly).
  • Listing Accounts Receivable as a liability (it is owed to the business, so it is an asset).
  • Forgetting that a corporation's "owner's equity" is split between Common Shares and Retained Earnings.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "identify and describe the categories of accounts (assets, liabilities, owner's equity, revenue, expenses, drawings) and distinguish among the three legal forms of business organisation (sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation)."

1.4 — The Fundamental Accounting Equation
\( \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity} \); analysing the effect of transactions on the equation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every bookkeeping system on earth, whether kept in a leather-bound ledger or in cloud-based software, rests on a single algebraic identity called the fundamental accounting equation:

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity} \]

The equation expresses the obvious truth that everything the business owns (its assets) must have been financed by money owed to outsiders (liabilities) or by money invested or earned by the owner (owner's equity). It is sometimes restated as \( \text{Owner's Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} \), making explicit that equity is the residual claim — what remains for the owner after all outside claims have been satisfied.

Because the equation must always balance, every transaction has at least two effects on the books — this is the rule of double-entry bookkeeping. The transaction may (1) cause two changes on the same side that net to zero (e.g., buy supplies for cash: supplies up, cash down), (2) cause equal increases on both sides (e.g., borrow money from the bank: cash up, bank loan up), or (3) cause equal decreases on both sides (e.g., pay an account payable: cash down, accounts payable down). In every case, after the transaction is recorded the equation still balances; if it does not, an error has been made.

  • Fundamental equation: \( A = L + OE \).
  • Double-entry: at least two account changes per transaction.
  • Residual claim: owner's equity is what is left after creditors are paid.
  • Equation always balances after every transaction.

Example: Show the effect of three transactions on the equation. Starting position: Cash \$2 000, Equipment \$3 000, Accounts Payable \$1 000, R. Singh Capital \$4 000.

Check: \(A = 2\,000 + 3\,000 = 5\,000\); \(L + OE = 1\,000 + 4\,000 = 5\,000\). ✓

(1) Borrowed \$2 500 from the bank. Cash \(+2\,500\); Bank Loan Payable \(+2\,500\). New totals: \(A = 7\,500\), \(L = 3\,500\), \(OE = 4\,000\). Equation: \(7\,500 = 3\,500 + 4\,000\) ✓.

(2) Paid \$600 cash on Accounts Payable. Cash \(-600\); Accounts Payable \(-600\). New: \(A = 6\,900\), \(L = 2\,900\), \(OE = 4\,000\). \(6\,900 = 2\,900 + 4\,000\) ✓.

(3) Singh invested an extra \$1 000. Cash \(+1\,000\); R. Singh Capital \(+1\,000\). New: \(A = 7\,900\), \(L = 2\,900\), \(OE = 5\,000\). \(7\,900 = 2\,900 + 5\,000\) ✓.

  • Adjusting only one side of the equation — every transaction has at least two effects.
  • Confusing Capital with Cash. Capital is a claim against assets; Cash is an asset.
  • Misreading "purchased supplies on account" as a cash transaction — it increases an asset and a liability.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — "use the fundamental accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity) to analyse the effect of business transactions on the financial position of the business."

1.5 — T-Accounts & the Rules of Debit and Credit
The T-account shape; debit-left/credit-right convention; the six rules; normal balances by category.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Although the accounting equation will always be tested in dollars and cents, bookkeepers do not literally rewrite the equation after every transaction. Instead, they record changes in T-accounts — one T-shaped page per account — and use the conventions debit (Dr.) for the left side and credit (Cr.) for the right side. Debit and credit are nothing more than direction labels; whether they mean "increase" or "decrease" depends on which side of the equation the account lives on.

Here are the six rules of debit and credit that follow directly from \( A = L + OE \):

  • Assets: increase with a debit (left side of the T), decrease with a credit.
  • Liabilities: increase with a credit, decrease with a debit.
  • Owner's Capital: increase with a credit, decrease with a debit.
  • Owner's Drawings: increase with a debit, decrease with a credit (drawings reduce capital).
  • Revenue: increase with a credit, decrease with a debit (revenue grows capital).
  • Expense: increase with a debit, decrease with a credit (expense shrinks capital).

The side on which an account normally carries its balance is called its normal balance. Assets, drawings, and expenses normally have debit balances; liabilities, capital, and revenue normally have credit balances. For any single transaction, the dollar total of debits must equal the dollar total of credits — this is the bookkeeping expression of the rule that the equation must balance.

Example: A landscaping firm receives \$800 cash for a finished lawn-mowing contract.

Identify the accounts and rules: Cash (Asset) increases by \$800 → debit Cash \$800. Service Revenue (Revenue) increases by \$800 → credit Service Revenue \$800. The T-accounts look like:

Cash Service Revenue Dr. | Cr. Dr. | Cr. 800 | | 800

Total debits (\$800) = total credits (\$800). The accounting equation: Assets +\$800 = Liabilities + (Owner's Equity +\$800). The equation still balances.

  • Memorising "debit = increase" universally. Debit only increases asset, drawings, and expense accounts; for liabilities, capital, and revenue, a debit means decrease.
  • Forgetting that drawings, while part of owner's equity, behaves like an asset in the debit/credit sense (debit to increase).
  • Recording dollar amounts whose debits and credits do not match — every entry must keep debits = credits.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.5 — "apply the rules of debit and credit to record transactions in T-accounts; identify the normal balance of each account category."

1.6 — Analysing Transactions with T-Accounts
Step-by-step transaction analysis; revenue, expense, and drawing transactions and their joint effect on capital.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The skill that distinguishes a strong BAF3M student is transaction analysis — the disciplined four-step routine that converts any business event into the correct debits and credits. The steps are:

  1. Identify the two (or more) accounts affected.
  2. Classify each account by category (asset, liability, capital, revenue, expense, drawings).
  3. Determine whether each account is increasing or decreasing.
  4. Apply the rules of debit and credit; verify debits = credits.

The four steps work for every transaction, no matter how exotic. When revenue or an expense is involved, students often forget that the second half of the entry is hidden inside owner's equity. Revenue increases equity (so it is credited like Capital); expense decreases equity (so it is debited, just like Drawings would be). Drawings themselves do not appear on the income statement — they are an owner's-equity reduction recorded outside the revenue/expense system.

Below is a worked sequence of typical transactions that mixes assets, liabilities, capital, revenue, expenses, and drawings.

Example: For "Maple Mobile Detailing" (sole proprietorship), analyse and record:

(a) Owner M. Lin invests \$5 000 cash to start the business.
Cash (A, ↑) Dr 5 000; M. Lin, Capital (OE, ↑) Cr 5 000.

(b) Buys cleaning supplies for \$320 cash.
Supplies (A, ↑) Dr 320; Cash (A, ↓) Cr 320.

(c) Buys a pressure washer for \$900 on account.
Equipment (A, ↑) Dr 900; Accounts Payable (L, ↑) Cr 900.

(d) Detailing services performed for cash, \$750.
Cash (A, ↑) Dr 750; Service Revenue (R, ↑) Cr 750.

(e) Paid \$120 cash for gasoline used in operations.
Gasoline Expense (E, ↑) Dr 120; Cash (A, ↓) Cr 120.

(f) Owner withdrew \$200 cash for personal use.
M. Lin, Drawings (OE contra, ↑) Dr 200; Cash (A, ↓) Cr 200.

After all six transactions: total debits = total credits = \$7 290. Cash final balance: \(+5\,000 - 320 + 750 - 120 - 200 = 5\,110\) (debit). The accounting equation balances: \(A = 5\,110 + 320 + 900 = 6\,330\); \(L + OE = 900 + (5\,000 + 750 - 120 - 200) = 900 + 5\,430 = 6\,330\) ✓.

  • Skipping step 2. Without classifying each account first, the debit/credit decision becomes guesswork.
  • Recording the owner's investment as revenue. Invested capital is a credit to Capital, not to Revenue.
  • Treating Drawings as an expense in transaction (f), thereby double-counting it on the income statement.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.6 — "analyse business transactions using T-accounts and apply the rules of debit and credit to record them, including transactions involving revenue, expenses, and owner's drawings."
