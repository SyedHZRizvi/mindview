2.1 — Source Documents & the Objectivity Principle
Sales invoice, purchase invoice, cheque copy, cash-register summary, bank statement, memo; their function as audit-trail evidence.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Before any number can be written into a journal, the bookkeeper needs evidence that the transaction actually happened. That evidence is called a source document. The objectivity principle (Chapter 1) requires that every entry rest on independent, verifiable documentation, and source documents provide exactly that. They also create the audit trail auditors follow to confirm that reported numbers match underlying reality.

The most common source documents you will meet in BAF3M are:

  • Sales invoice — issued when the business sells goods or services on account. The original goes to the customer; the duplicate stays in the seller's files and supports a debit to Accounts Receivable.
  • Purchase invoice — received from a supplier when goods or services are bought on account. Supports a credit to Accounts Payable.
  • Cash-sales slip / cash-register tape — supports cash received from over-the-counter sales (debit Cash, credit Sales Revenue).
  • Cheque copy — supports payments made by the business (credit Cash for the amount drawn).
  • Bank advice / debit memo / credit memo — supports bank service charges, NSF-cheque charges, or interest earned on the company's bank account.
  • Petty-cash voucher — supports small cash disbursements when the office maintains a petty-cash fund.
  • Internal memo — for adjusting entries or transactions without external documents (e.g., supplies used at month-end).

Every source document must be numbered consecutively, dated, and stored. After the journal entry is made, the document is filed; later, anyone wishing to verify the entry can pull the document from the file and confirm the figures.

Example: Match the source document to the journal entry it supports.

(a) Cheque #214, \$420, paid to Hydro One for monthly electricity. Cheque copy → Dr. Utilities Expense 420; Cr. Cash 420.

(b) Sales Invoice #1052, \$1 200, for landscape design completed for E. Tan, on account. Sales invoice (duplicate) → Dr. Accounts Receivable 1 200; Cr. Service Revenue 1 200.

(c) Purchase Invoice #88-A from Grand & Toy for \$340 of office supplies, on account. Purchase invoice → Dr. Supplies 340; Cr. Accounts Payable 340.

(d) Bank debit memo for \$15 monthly service charge. Bank memo → Dr. Bank Charges Expense 15; Cr. Cash 15.

  • Recording a transaction with no source document attached (violates objectivity).
  • Throwing away duplicates of sales invoices after the entry is made — the audit trail then breaks.
  • Confusing a credit-card slip with a cheque. A credit-card slip is a special form of cash sale; the bank pays immediately, less a fee.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "identify common source documents (invoices, cheques, cash-register receipts, memos, bank statements) and describe their purpose in providing objective evidence for accounting entries."

2.2 — The Two-Column General Journal
Journal format: date, account titles & explanation, posting reference (P.R.), debit column, credit column; the explanation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The general journal is the book of original entry — the first place a transaction is recorded chronologically. It is the diary of the business in accounting language. A standard two-column general journal has five columns: Date, Account Titles and Explanation, P.R. (Posting Reference), Debit, and Credit. Each entry contains:

  • The transaction date (year and month written once per page; day repeated each entry).
  • The account(s) debited, written flush left.
  • The account(s) credited, indented about one centimetre.
  • A short explanation, indented further, written in past-tense narrative ("Cheque #214 for hydro").
  • The P.R. column — left blank when the entry is made; filled in later with the ledger account number as each line is posted.
  • The debit and credit dollar amounts on the appropriate rows.

A line is left blank between entries to keep the journal readable. When more than one account is debited or credited (a compound entry), each account is listed separately so that the audit trail is preserved. Compound entries occur often — for example, the owner deposits both cash and equipment as an opening investment, or a sale produces cash plus accounts receivable.

Although today's accountants use software, the underlying structure of every transaction (date, account, debit, credit, explanation, P.R.) is exactly what the manual general journal forces you to understand. Computerised systems still produce a journal listing in this five-column format on demand.

Example: Record the transactions of "Riverside Print Shop" for 3 March 2026 in general-journal form.

(a) Mar 3 — Cash sale, \$420, supported by cash-register tape #14.
(b) Mar 3 — Bought stationery on account from Grand & Toy, \$120 (PI #G-9).

Date | Account Titles & Explanation | P.R. | Debit | Credit -------+---------------------------------------------+------+----------+--------- Mar 3 | Cash | | 420.00 | | Sales Revenue | | | 420.00 | (Cash-register tape #14 — daily sales) | | | | | | | 3 | Supplies | | 120.00 | | Accounts Payable — Grand & Toy | | | 120.00 | (PI #G-9 — stationery on account) | | |

Total debits on the page so far: \$540; total credits: \$540 ✓.

  • Forgetting to indent the credited account — this is a long-standing convention readers rely on.
  • Writing the explanation in the wrong column — it belongs below the account titles, indented further still.
  • Combining unrelated transactions into one entry. Each transaction gets its own entry, separated by a blank line.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "record routine business transactions in a two-column general journal using correct debit/credit entries, a clear narrative explanation, and the proper journal format."

2.3 — Compound Journal Entries & Common Transactions
Entries with multiple debits or credits; sample transactions for service businesses; opening entries.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A compound journal entry is any entry that contains more than one debit or more than one credit. Real-world transactions frequently require compound entries because a single business event affects three or more accounts. The accounting equation must still balance, so the total of debits must equal the total of credits.

The most common compound entry you will write in Chapter 2 is the opening entry — the first entry made when a new set of books is opened, listing every asset on the debit side and every liability and capital account on the credit side. The opening entry establishes the equation \( A = L + OE \) inside the journal in one transaction.

Other typical compound entries include a sale that is part cash and part on account, paying multiple suppliers with a single cheque (rare but possible), or an investment of more than one type of asset by the owner.

Example 1 — Opening entry. On 1 April 2026, J. Park opens "Park Tutoring" and contributes: Cash \$3 000, Office Equipment \$1 200, and a note payable to her brother for \$400. Capital is the residual.

First compute Capital: \(OE = A - L = (3\,000 + 1\,200) - 400 = 3\,800\).

Apr 1 | Cash | 3 000.00 | | Office Equipment | 1 200.00 | | Notes Payable | | 400.00 | J. Park, Capital | | 3 800.00 | (Opening entry — owner contributions and note)

Total debits \$4 200 = total credits \$4 200 ✓.

Example 2 — Compound sale entry. On 12 April Riverside Print Shop sells \$900 of printing: \$300 cash and \$600 on account.

Apr 12 | Cash | 300.00 | | Accounts Receivable | 600.00 | | Sales Revenue | | 900.00 | (Cash & on-account sale, INV #117)

Debits \$900 = credits \$900 ✓.

  • Forgetting that compound entries still balance — every debit dollar must be matched by a credit dollar.
  • Mis-classifying a note payable as a long-term liability without checking the due date.
  • Listing assets and liabilities in random order in the opening entry — convention is assets first (in liquidity order), then liabilities, then capital.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "record routine business transactions, including compound entries (e.g., opening entries and split-payment transactions), in the general journal, ensuring total debits equal total credits."

2.4 — The General Ledger & Chart of Accounts
Three-column ledger account; chart of accounts; the standard numbering 100s–500s; running balance format.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The general ledger is the book of final entry. Whereas the journal records transactions chronologically, the ledger collects all the changes of a single account into one place so that an up-to-date balance can be read at any moment. Each account has its own page (or screen in software), and the manual three-column ledger account consists of: Date, Particulars, P.R., Debit, Credit, and Balance (sometimes with a Dr/Cr tag column). The running-balance format means the new balance is computed and written down after every posting, so the figure on the last line is always current.

To navigate the ledger efficiently, accountants use a chart of accounts — a numbered list of every account the business maintains. A common Canadian high-school convention is:

  • 100–199: Asset accounts (101 Cash, 110 Accounts Receivable, 115 Supplies, 120 Prepaid Insurance, 150 Equipment, 155 Accum. Depr. — Equipment, 160 Building, 170 Land …)
  • 200–299: Liability accounts (200 Accounts Payable, 210 GST/HST Payable, 220 Notes Payable, 230 Bank Loan Payable, 240 Mortgage Payable …)
  • 300–399: Owner's Equity accounts (300 Capital, 310 Drawings, 350 Income Summary)
  • 400–499: Revenue accounts (400 Sales Revenue, 410 Service Revenue, 420 Interest Revenue …)
  • 500–599: Expense accounts (500 Rent Expense, 510 Salaries Expense, 520 Utilities Expense, 530 Supplies Expense, 540 Insurance Expense, 550 Depreciation Expense …)

The chart is set up in the order in which accounts will eventually appear on the financial statements, which makes statement preparation easier. New accounts are added (and gaps are deliberately left, like 105 between 101 and 110) so that the firm can insert future accounts in their natural sequence.

Example: A partial ledger account for Cash (101) for the first three days of April 2026 of Park Tutoring (after the opening entry above and one transaction).

Account: Cash Account No. 101 ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- Date | Particulars | P.R. | Debit | Credit | DR/CR | Balance -------+---------------------------+------+---------+--------+-------+---------- Apr 1 | Opening entry | J1 | 3 000.00| | Dr | 3 000.00 Apr 2 | Tutoring fees received | J1 | 180.00| | Dr | 3 180.00 Apr 3 | Cheque #001 — rent | J1 | | 450.00| Dr | 2 730.00

After Apr 3, the running balance is \$2 730 Dr — the cash on hand. Posting reference "J1" means the source entry is found on page 1 of the general journal.

  • Forgetting to update the running balance — the column must be recomputed after every line.
  • Using the chart-of-accounts title instead of the number in the journal P.R. column.
  • Numbering accounts consecutively with no gaps — gaps allow future accounts to be inserted in proper sequence.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "post journal entries to a general ledger using a chart of accounts, maintain running balances in three-column accounts, and explain how account numbers organise the ledger in financial-statement order."

2.5 — Posting & Cross-Referencing
The four-step posting process; how P.R. fields create the audit trail; locating a posting from journal page to ledger account.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Posting is the act of transferring each debit or credit from a general-journal entry to the corresponding general-ledger account. Posting must be done one line at a time, in the same order the entries appear in the journal. The four-step posting routine for a single journal line is:

  1. Find the ledger account named in the journal line (e.g., "Cash").
  2. Copy the date and journal page (J1, J2, …) into the ledger account's Date and P.R. columns.
  3. Enter the dollar amount in the ledger account's debit (or credit) column, then update the running balance.
  4. Return to the journal and write the ledger-account number in the journal's P.R. column to confirm that the line has been posted.

The P.R. columns are the heart of the audit trail. In the journal, the P.R. tells the reader "this line has been posted, and you will find it in account #101." In the ledger, the P.R. tells the reader "this number originated from page 3 of the journal." A line in the journal with an empty P.R. is a warning sign — either it was missed during posting, or it was posted to the wrong account.

The benefit of cross-referencing is that any auditor — internal or external — can pick a number on a financial statement and trace it back through the ledger to the journal and finally to the source document. Conversely, any source document can be traced forward to its impact on the financial statements. This bidirectional traceability is the meaning of the phrase "the books reconcile."

Example: Post the following journal entry and complete the P.R. cross-reference.

General Journal — Page 1 Date | Account Titles & Explanation | P.R. | Debit | Credit -------+---------------------------------------------+------+----------+-------- Apr 10 | Office Equipment | 150 | 850.00 | | Accounts Payable — Office Plus | 200 | | 850.00 | (PI #OP-44 — printer on account) | | |

Posting steps: (1) Open Office Equipment account #150; (2) date Apr 10, P.R. J1, debit \$850, new balance debit \$850; (3) write 150 in the journal's P.R. column for the debit line. (4) Open Accounts Payable #200; date Apr 10, P.R. J1, credit \$850, balance credit \$850. Write 200 in the journal's P.R. column for the credit line. Audit trail complete.

  • Updating the journal P.R. before posting; you must post first, then return and complete the journal P.R., so an unfinished P.R. column always reveals an un-posted line.
  • Posting both legs of an entry without recomputing the running balance — the most common source of trial-balance errors.
  • Writing the account title instead of the number in the P.R. column — the column is reserved for numbers.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "post journal entries from the general journal to the general ledger, cross-reference posting references between books of original and final entry, and explain how this process creates the audit trail."

2.6 — Subsidiary Ledgers & Control Accounts
Accounts Receivable subsidiary ledger; Accounts Payable subsidiary ledger; control-account reconciliation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

When a business has many customers or many suppliers, listing every individual in the general ledger would make it unmanageable. Instead, accountants use a single control account in the general ledger (e.g., Accounts Receivable #110) that holds the total owed by all customers, and a separate subsidiary ledger that contains one page per customer with full detail. The same is done for suppliers using Accounts Payable as the control and an A/P subsidiary ledger that lists each supplier.

The rule that keeps everything in sync is the reconciliation rule: the balance of the control account at any moment must equal the sum of the balances of all the individual subsidiary-ledger accounts. Whenever a journal entry affects accounts receivable or payable, the bookkeeper makes two postings — one to the control account in the general ledger, and one to the named individual in the subsidiary ledger. A small "✓" or the name of the customer is written beside the posting reference to remind the bookkeeper that both postings have been made.

The subsidiary-ledger approach (a) gives detailed account information for collections and payments without cluttering the general ledger, (b) supports segregation of duties because one clerk can manage the subsidiary ledger while another runs the general ledger, and (c) facilitates internal control by making it impossible for a single dishonest employee to alter only one ledger without producing a mismatch.

Example: At month-end, the Accounts Receivable control account shows a debit balance of \$2 450. The A/R subsidiary ledger shows: E. Tan \$1 200, M. Singh \$700, R. Brown \$550. Does the ledger reconcile?

Sum of individual customers: \(1\,200 + 700 + 550 = 2\,450\).

Compare to control: \$2 450 (Dr.).

They agree — the subsidiary ledger reconciles to the control account. If they had differed (say, customer total was \$2 350), the bookkeeper would have to find the missing or duplicated \$100 posting before producing financial statements.

  • Posting to the control account but forgetting the subsidiary-ledger card (or vice-versa) — the most common cause of an out-of-balance reconciliation.
  • Combining customer balances of zero with active balances — keep active customers separate, even at \$0, until the account is formally closed.
  • Treating the subsidiary ledger as part of the general ledger. It is a separate, parallel book.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "describe the function of subsidiary ledgers (Accounts Receivable, Accounts Payable) and explain how control-account reconciliation supports the integrity of the general ledger."
