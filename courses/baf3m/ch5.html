5.1 — Principles of Internal Control
Establishing responsibility, segregation of duties, documentation, physical safeguards, independent verification, bonding.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Internal control is the system of policies and procedures a business puts in place to protect its assets, ensure the reliability of its accounting records, promote operational efficiency, and encourage compliance with management's directives. Strong internal controls are the difference between a business that survives a dishonest or careless employee and one that is bankrupted by a single fraud. The principles you must know for BAF3M are:

  • Establishment of responsibility. Each task should be assigned to a single, identifiable person. When something goes wrong, accountability is clear.
  • Segregation of duties. No single employee should handle a transaction from start to finish. The classic split is: (1) authorisation of the transaction, (2) custody of the asset, and (3) record-keeping. If one employee holds all three, fraud is much easier.
  • Documentation procedures. Pre-numbered source documents (cheques, sales invoices, purchase orders) ensure that every transaction is traceable; missing numbers raise alarms.
  • Physical, mechanical, and electronic controls. Safes, locked storage rooms, alarmed cash registers, password-protected accounting software, surveillance cameras.
  • Independent internal verification. One employee's work is reviewed by another, or by a periodic audit. Cash on hand is independently counted; the count is compared to the ledger.
  • Other human-resource controls. Bonding of cashiers (insurance against employee theft); mandatory vacations and rotation of duties; background checks on new hires.

A control weakness is any situation where one of these principles is violated — for example, the same employee who opens the mail also records receivable payments and reconciles the bank account. A determined dishonest employee in such a role could steal customer payments and cover them up by altering the books and the bank reconciliation.

Example: Identify the internal-control weaknesses at "Maple Café" and suggest improvements.

Scenario: Cashier C. Lee handles cash sales, deposits the day's cash in the bank, prepares the journal entry, posts to the general ledger, and reconciles the bank statement each month. The owner relies on monthly statements from the bookkeeper to monitor profitability.

Weaknesses: (1) C. Lee performs custody (handling cash), record-keeping (journal & ledger), and independent verification (bank reconciliation) — three duties that must be segregated. (2) No one independently counts the cash drawer at end of shift. (3) Mandatory vacation is absent. Improvements: (a) split the cash-deposit and bank-reconciliation tasks to a second employee; (b) require an independent count of the cash drawer at every shift change; (c) the owner herself should review the bank reconciliation each month; (d) require all employees handling cash to be bonded.

  • Believing internal control means complete prevention of fraud — controls reduce risk; collusion among multiple employees can still defeat them.
  • Treating internal controls as "the auditor's job". They are management's responsibility; auditors test whether they exist and work.
  • Skipping cost-benefit analysis. A small business may not segregate duties to the extent a large firm does, but the owner must compensate by performing periodic personal reviews.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "describe the principles of internal control (establishment of responsibility, segregation of duties, documentation, physical/mechanical/electronic controls, independent verification, bonding) and identify control weaknesses in a given business scenario."

5.2 — Bank Reconciliation
Reconciling the bank statement to the book balance; outstanding cheques, deposits in transit, NSF cheques, service charges, errors.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A bank reconciliation is the monthly procedure of comparing the cash balance shown on the bank statement with the cash balance in the company's general ledger and explaining any difference. The two figures rarely agree initially because: (a) the business has issued cheques that the recipients have not yet cashed (outstanding cheques), (b) the business has made deposits the bank has not yet recorded (deposits in transit), (c) the bank has charged service fees or earned interest on the balance, or (d) a cheque deposited by the business has bounced (NSF — non-sufficient funds).

The standard two-column reconciliation has the bank-statement balance on the left and the book (ledger) balance on the right. Adjustments are made to each side until both adjusted balances equal. The rules are:

  • Bank side: Add deposits in transit; subtract outstanding cheques; correct bank errors (e.g., a cheque the bank charged to the wrong account).
  • Book side: Add credits the company has not yet recorded (interest earned, EFT collections); subtract debits not yet recorded (service charges, NSF cheques); correct book errors (e.g., a cheque journalised in the wrong amount).

After reconciliation, the company must journalise every adjustment made on the book side (the bank side adjustments are simply timing differences that will clear by themselves). Common book-side journal entries are: Dr. Bank Charges Expense / Cr. Cash for service charges; Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Cash for NSF cheques received from customers (the original deposit is reversed); Dr. Cash / Cr. Interest Revenue for interest earned.

Example: Greenfield Sports' books show Cash \$8 420 on 31 March 2026. The bank statement shows \$9 050. Reconciling items: deposit in transit \$1 200; outstanding cheques #207 \$540 and #211 \$1 280; bank service charge \$15; interest credited by bank \$25; NSF cheque returned (customer R. Brown) \$200.

Greenfield Sports Bank Reconciliation 31 March 2026 Bank Balance 9 050 Add: Deposit in transit +1 200 Less: Outstanding cheques #207 540 #211 1 280 - 1 820 -------- Adjusted bank balance 8 430 Book Balance 8 420 Add: Interest earned 25 Less: Service charge 15 Less: NSF cheque (R. Brown) 200 ------ Adjusted book balance 8 230

The two columns do not yet agree (\$8 430 ≠ \$8 230) — a \$200 discrepancy. Re-check: the bank shows \$9 050, less outstanding cheques \$1 820, plus deposit in transit \$1 200 → \$8 430 ✓. The book side: \$8 420 + 25 − 15 − 200 = \$8 230. These would reconcile only if we had spotted, for instance, a book error of \$200 — possibly a deposit recorded twice, or a cheque misposted. The reconciliation has done its job: it has identified \$200 that needs investigation. The bookkeeper would track down the error, correct it (Dr. or Cr. Cash by \$200 as appropriate), then re-prepare the reconciliation to confirm equality.

Journal entries (assuming the \$200 error was, on investigation, a duplicated entry that overstated cash): Dr. Bank Charges Expense 15 / Cr. Cash 15; Dr. Cash 25 / Cr. Interest Revenue 25; Dr. Accounts Receivable — R. Brown 200 / Cr. Cash 200; Dr. (whatever account was wrongly credited) 200 / Cr. Cash 200 to fix the duplicate.

  • Journalising adjustments from both sides of the reconciliation. Only the book-side items need entries; bank-side items clear themselves.
  • Subtracting deposits in transit (which haven't yet hit the bank — they are added to the bank balance).
  • Forgetting to follow up on errors. A reconciliation that doesn't balance after the standard adjustments points to a real error somewhere.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "prepare a monthly bank reconciliation that identifies outstanding cheques, deposits in transit, bank service charges, NSF cheques, and interest revenue, and journalise the resulting adjustments to the Cash account."

5.3 — Petty Cash & the Imprest System
Establishing, replenishing, and adjusting a petty-cash fund; petty-cash vouchers; cash-over-and-short.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Some small business expenses (postage stamps, parking, courier tips) are impractical to pay by cheque. To handle these the business maintains a petty-cash fund under the imprest system: a fixed amount of cash is kept in a locked drawer, and every disbursement is supported by a numbered petty-cash voucher. At all times, "cash + signed vouchers = the imprest amount". When cash runs low, the fund is replenished by writing a cheque for the total of the vouchers — bringing the fund back to its original amount.

The three petty-cash journal entries are:

  • Establish the fund: Dr. Petty Cash (asset) for the imprest amount; Cr. Cash. (Made only once, the day the fund is created.)
  • Replenish the fund: Dr. each expense account for which a voucher exists (Postage Expense, Supplies Expense, Travel Expense …); Cr. Cash for the total. The Petty Cash account is not touched during replenishment.
  • Increase or decrease the fund: Dr. Petty Cash (or Cr. Petty Cash) for the change; Cr. (or Dr.) Cash.

If the cash count plus vouchers does not equal the imprest amount, a temporary account Cash Over and Short absorbs the discrepancy. A debit balance (shortage) is an expense; a credit balance (overage) is miscellaneous revenue.

Example: Petty-cash transactions for "Maple Café".

(a) 1 May 2026: Establish a petty-cash fund of \$200 by cashing a cheque.
Dr. Petty Cash 200; Cr. Cash 200.

(b) 28 May: Cash on hand \$48. Vouchers: Postage \$45, Office Supplies \$60, Travel \$45, Misc \$3. Replenishment cheque to bring fund back to \$200.
Vouchers total: \(45 + 60 + 45 + 3 = 153\). Cash on hand \$48. Total = \$201, which exceeds imprest \$200 by \$1 — a \$1 cash overage.
Dr. Postage Expense 45; Dr. Office Supplies Expense 60; Dr. Travel Expense 45; Dr. Miscellaneous Expense 3; Cr. Cash Over and Short 1; Cr. Cash 152.
(Replenishment cheque is for \$152 — the amount needed to bring the cash drawer from \$48 back to \$200.)

(c) 1 June: Increase the petty-cash fund from \$200 to \$300.
Dr. Petty Cash 100; Cr. Cash 100.

  • Debiting Petty Cash during a normal replenishment — only expense accounts are debited.
  • Forgetting to handle Cash Over and Short. If vouchers + cash ≠ imprest, the difference must be recognised explicitly.
  • Treating petty cash as a "slush fund". Every disbursement must be vouched and approved like any other cheque.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "establish, replenish, and adjust a petty-cash fund using the imprest system; journalise petty-cash transactions, including any cash over or short."

5.4 — Ethics in Accounting & the CPA Code of Conduct
Ethical dilemmas, fraudulent reporting, conflict of interest, the CPA Canada Code; a five-step ethical-decision model.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Accountants are entrusted with information that affects investors, lenders, employees, governments, and the public. The accuracy and integrity of that information depends on the ethics of the people who prepare and audit it. The accounting scandals of the past — Enron and WorldCom in the United States, Nortel Networks and Livent in Canada — destroyed retirement savings of thousands of people and put thousands of employees out of work. They all hinged on accountants who knowingly mis-reported revenues or expenses.

The four most common ethical issues in accounting are:

  • Fraudulent financial reporting. Deliberately recording revenues earlier than earned, or expenses later than incurred, to inflate net income.
  • Misappropriation of assets. Employee theft of cash, inventory, or other resources — often hidden through phony journal entries.
  • Conflict of interest. An accountant has a personal financial stake in the outcome of a transaction or audit (e.g., owning shares in a client company).
  • Breach of confidentiality. Sharing the client's financial information with third parties without authorisation.

Professional accountants in Canada are governed by the CPA Code of Professional Conduct, published by CPA Canada and enforced by provincial CPA bodies (CPA Ontario, CPA Alberta, etc.). The Code's five core values are integrity, due care, objectivity, professional competence, and confidentiality. Violations can lead to fines, suspension of the CPA designation, or expulsion from the profession.

When an ethical dilemma arises, a useful five-step decision model is: (1) recognise the ethical issue and identify the stakeholders; (2) gather all relevant facts; (3) generate alternative courses of action; (4) evaluate each alternative against the Code, the law, and the likely consequences for each stakeholder; (5) choose, document, and act — and be prepared to explain and defend the choice.

Example: Mei, a junior accountant at "Northern Lumber", is told by the controller to record \$80 000 of unfinished delivery orders as revenue this December so the company meets its annual bonus target. Apply the five-step model.

(1) Issue: Recognising revenue before it is earned violates the revenue-recognition principle and would mislead the bank, owners, and tax authorities. Stakeholders: owners, bank, employees, CRA, Mei herself.
(2) Facts: Orders are not yet delivered; cash has not been received; the bonus is conditional on net income. Code references: integrity, objectivity, due care.
(3) Alternatives: (a) record as instructed; (b) refuse and explain; (c) consult the senior partner; (d) report internally via whistle-blower process; (e) resign.
(4) Evaluation: (a) violates the Code and could constitute fraud; (b) preserves integrity but risks employment; (c) is consistent with both Code and CPA mentor relationships; (d) appropriate if (c) fails; (e) is a last resort.
(5) Decision: pursue (b) and (c) — refuse the entry, explain to the controller why it violates GAAP and the Code, and escalate to the senior partner if pressure continues. Document the conversation in writing.

  • Treating ethical issues as "grey areas" when they are clearly defined by the Code — the Code is specific about confidentiality, objectivity, and reporting.
  • Believing that "everyone does it" is a defence. CPA discipline cases have established that following an unethical instruction from a superior is never a complete defence.
  • Stopping at step 4 without acting. The hardest step is taking the decision through.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "describe the role of a professional code of conduct (CPA Canada Code of Professional Conduct) in guiding ethical behaviour, and apply a model for ethical decision making to a workplace scenario involving accounting."

5.5 — Users of Accounting Information & Their Decisions
Internal vs external users; what each uses the financial statements for; the role of accounting in capital markets.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Financial statements are not produced for their own sake — they exist to support decisions. Understanding who uses accounting information and what they use it for is essential because it shapes how financial statements are designed, audited, and regulated.

Internal users are people inside the business who use accounting information to manage operations:

  • Owners / managers — set prices, decide on hiring, evaluate the success of departments or product lines, decide whether to invest in new equipment or close an unprofitable branch.
  • Employees — understand the company's financial stability, evaluate bonus plans, negotiate compensation.

External users stand outside the business and rely chiefly on published financial statements:

  • Investors and prospective shareholders — decide whether to buy, hold, or sell shares; compare the firm's profitability and risk to competitors.
  • Creditors and lenders — decide whether to extend credit; set the interest rate; monitor loan covenants. They focus on liquidity, leverage, and cash-flow ratios.
  • Suppliers — decide credit terms (net 30, net 60, COD); assess the risk of default.
  • Customers — particularly for large purchases (cars, appliances) where warranties depend on the seller staying in business.
  • Governments — Canada Revenue Agency assesses income and HST/GST owed; municipalities assess property tax; Statistics Canada compiles national economic data.
  • Regulators and the public — securities regulators (Ontario Securities Commission) protect investors from misleading information; the public uses statements to evaluate the social and environmental impact of corporations.

Because the same statements serve such different decision-makers, financial-accounting standards (GAAP/IFRS) work hard to balance relevance and reliability. Information must be detailed enough to inform decisions, yet objective enough that users can rely on it.

Example: Match the user to the financial-statement focus.

(a) Bank loan officer evaluating a 5-year loan request → balance sheet for collateral and leverage; income statement and historical earnings to evaluate debt-service capacity; cash flow to verify cash generation. Key ratios: current ratio, debt-to-equity, interest coverage.
(b) Prospective shareholder researching a stock → primarily income statement for earnings per share trend and profit margins; statement of changes in equity for dividend history; balance sheet for asset quality.
(c) CRA auditor checking a return → income statement (revenues, deductions); reconciliation between accounting income and taxable income; subsidiary ledgers and source documents.
(d) Supplier offering trade credit → A/R aging, current ratio, days-payable; the firm's payment history with other suppliers.

  • Forgetting that suppliers and customers are external users — they make decisions just like investors and lenders.
  • Believing that internal users do not need GAAP statements. Internal managerial reports use the same underlying data; the only difference is presentation.
  • Treating accounting as a closed system. The point of every statement is to inform a decision by some user.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.1 — "identify the internal and external users of accounting information (owners, managers, employees, lenders, investors, customers, governments) and describe the typical decisions each user makes from the financial statements."

5.6 — Information Technology in Accounting
Manual vs computerised systems; spreadsheets, QuickBooks, Sage, cloud platforms; how IT changes internal controls and emerging trends.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In practice, virtually every Canadian business above the smallest level now uses accounting software rather than paper journals and ledgers. The conceptual framework you have studied — debits, credits, journals, ledgers, trial balances, financial statements — is unchanged. What computerisation changes is speed, accuracy of arithmetic, and the nature of internal controls.

Common platforms used in Canadian small business and education:

  • Spreadsheets (Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets) — useful for ad-hoc calculations, worksheets, what-if scenarios, but do not enforce double-entry and lack an audit trail.
  • Desktop accounting packages (Sage 50, QuickBooks Desktop) — full general-ledger systems with journals, subsidiary ledgers, financial statements, payroll, and inventory modules.
  • Cloud accounting platforms (QuickBooks Online, Xero, Wave) — same functionality delivered via a browser; data lives on the vendor's servers and can be accessed from any device.
  • Enterprise resource planning (ERP) suites (SAP, Oracle NetSuite, Microsoft Dynamics) — integrate accounting with operations for larger organisations.

Effects on internal control. Computerised systems eliminate arithmetic errors and automate posting, but introduce new risks: data hacking, ransomware, unauthorised user access, software bugs, and the loss of "paper trail". The new internal controls include user-ID + password access, role-based permissions, automatic backups, encryption, and routine penetration testing. The accountant's role shifts from "doing arithmetic" to "designing and reviewing controls".

Current trends: (a) IFRS adoption by publicly accountable enterprises; (b) sustainability reporting (ESG metrics) becoming required alongside financial statements; (c) artificial intelligence and data analytics automating bookkeeping, anomaly detection, and audit testing; (d) blockchain providing tamper-evident transaction records for some industries; (e) open banking enabling cloud accounting platforms to feed bank transactions directly into the books, eliminating manual entry of many cash transactions.

Example: Compare manual vs computerised accounting for the same set of transactions at "Maple Café".

Manual: Owner records ~120 transactions for the month in a paper journal; clerk posts each line to a paper ledger; clerk re-adds columns to verify; trial balance, worksheet, statements all hand-prepared. Estimated time: ~25 hours/month, with ~3 transposition errors typically caught only at trial-balance stage.

Computerised (QuickBooks Online): Bank transactions auto-imported via open banking; owner classifies each in 1–2 clicks. Sales-invoice software produces journal entries automatically; payroll module computes deductions. At month-end, QuickBooks generates the trial balance, financial statements, and HST report in seconds. Estimated time: ~6 hours/month. New risks: a bookkeeper with full administrator rights could delete entries silently; backup of cloud data is the vendor's responsibility; passwords must be strong and shared with care.

Result: the owner gains 19 hours/month and statements appear in real time, but must invest in user-access controls, password discipline, and regular review of the audit log.

  • Assuming software is error-proof. Software does what you tell it; a wrong entry produces wrong statements just as silently as on paper.
  • Skipping the reconciliation step because the software "automates everything". Bank reconciliation must still be performed and reviewed.
  • Believing IT eliminates the need to understand the accounting cycle. Every accounting-software screen mirrors a step in the cycle you have learned in BAF3M.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.2 — "compare manual and computerized accounting systems, describe the role of common platforms (spreadsheets, QuickBooks, Sage, cloud), explain how computerisation changes internal controls, and identify current trends (IFRS, sustainability, AI, blockchain) shaping accounting practice."
