3.1 — Preparing a Trial Balance
Listing accounts in chart-of-accounts order with debit and credit balances; verifying \( \sum \text{Dr} = \sum \text{Cr} \).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

After all journal entries for a period have been posted to the general ledger, the bookkeeper must verify that total debits equal total credits. This verification is performed by preparing a trial balance: a list of every account in the ledger, in chart-of-accounts order, with each account's final balance entered in either a debit column or a credit column depending on its normal balance. If the two columns add to the same total, the books are arithmetically balanced and the next step (worksheet and financial statements) can proceed.

A trial balance has three lines of heading: name of the business, the words "Trial Balance", and the date (a single point in time, not a period). Then the account titles are listed in order (assets, liabilities, capital, drawings, revenues, expenses) with balances in the appropriate column. Asset, drawings, and expense balances go in the debit column; liability, capital, and revenue balances go in the credit column. Closing each column with a double rule beneath identical totals confirms balance.

It is critical to understand what a balanced trial balance does not prove. It only shows that the dollar amounts of debits equal the dollar amounts of credits in the ledger. It cannot detect (a) a transaction omitted entirely, (b) the right amount posted to the wrong account on the correct side, or (c) a transaction recorded twice or not at all. Such errors require independent verification (e.g., bank reconciliation, A/R subsidiary-ledger reconciliation).

  • Trial balance: a list of all ledger accounts and their balances at a point in time.
  • Three-line heading: business name; "Trial Balance"; date.
  • Order: chart-of-accounts (asset → liability → capital → drawings → revenue → expense).
  • Arithmetical balance only: equal totals are necessary but not sufficient for correctness.

Example: Park Tutoring trial balance, 30 April 2026.

Park Tutoring Trial Balance 30 April 2026 Acct | Account | Debit | Credit -----+-------------------------+-----------+---------- 101 | Cash | 2 980.00 | 110 | Accounts Receivable | 600.00 | 115 | Supplies | 150.00 | 150 | Office Equipment | 1 200.00 | 200 | Accounts Payable | | 340.00 220 | Notes Payable | | 400.00 300 | J. Park, Capital | | 3 800.00 310 | J. Park, Drawings | 200.00 | 410 | Tutoring Revenue | | 1 240.00 500 | Rent Expense | 450.00 | 510 | Utilities Expense | 80.00 | 530 | Supplies Expense | 90.00 | 540 | Insurance Expense | 30.00 | -----+-------------------------+-----------+---------- | 5 780.00 | 5 780.00

Totals \$5 780 = \$5 780 ✓. The trial balance is in arithmetical balance, so the bookkeeping can be presumed mechanically correct for now.

  • Listing every account at zero — only accounts with non-zero balances need to be shown (though most software shows zeros for completeness).
  • Putting an asset balance in the credit column because the running balance was mistakenly carried that way; double-check normal balances.
  • Mistaking arithmetical balance for full correctness — many errors leave debits = credits.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "prepare a trial balance from the general ledger in chart-of-accounts order, demonstrating that total debits equal total credits and explaining what a balanced trial balance does and does not prove."

3.2 — Locating Errors: Transposition, Slide, Omission, & the Divide-by-Nine Test
Identifying common errors when debits do not equal credits; the "÷ 9 rule" for transpositions and slides.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

When the trial balance fails to balance, the bookkeeper must hunt down the error. Brute-force re-posting of every transaction wastes time; experienced practitioners run a checklist that targets the most common bookkeeping errors first.

Transposition error: two adjacent digits are swapped (e.g., entering \$71 instead of \$17, or \$540 instead of \$450). Slide error: the decimal is misplaced (entering \$12.40 as \$124.00, or \$3 200 as \$32 000). Both errors produce a difference that is always divisible by 9 — that is the famous "divide-by-nine test". If the difference between the debit and credit columns of an out-of-balance trial balance, divided by 9, gives a whole number, then a transposition or slide is the likely cause.

Other common errors: (a) omission — a transaction was journalised but not posted; (b) double posting — a single line was posted twice; (c) wrong column — a debit balance was placed in the credit column on the trial balance. A useful trick for (c) is to take the trial-balance difference, divide by 2, and search for an account with exactly that balance — it has probably been placed in the wrong column.

Once the cause is suspected, work through the books in this order: re-add the trial-balance columns; check the running balances in each ledger account; re-add the journal totals; finally, re-verify each posting line against the journal. Stop the moment the error is found and document the correction.

  • Transposition: swapped digits; difference divisible by 9.
  • Slide: displaced decimal; difference divisible by 9.
  • Omission: transaction journalised but never posted.
  • Wrong column: if difference ÷ 2 equals an account balance, that account is in the wrong column.
  • Search order: re-add columns → check ledger balances → re-add journal → verify postings.

Example: A trial balance shows totals: Debits \$12 460, Credits \$12 280. Difference: \$180. Test the rules.

(1) Is \$180 divisible by 9? \(180 \div 9 = 20\). Yes — so a transposition or slide is likely. (2) Re-examine recent postings for any pair like 84↔48 or 350↔530. After scanning the postings, the bookkeeper finds that Rent Expense was posted as \$450 in the journal but \$540 in the ledger — a transposition of \$90, but applied across two postings the running net difference is \$180. (3) Correct by re-running the Rent Expense ledger account with the correct figure and re-totalling the trial balance.

If the difference had been, say, \$200 (not divisible by 9), the bookkeeper would have applied the ÷-2 test: \(200 \div 2 = 100\). She would have searched for an account with a \$100 balance placed in the wrong column.

  • Searching transactions randomly. Always apply the ÷ 9 test and ÷ 2 test first — they immediately classify the error type.
  • Assuming a balanced trial balance means error-free books — it only means total debits equal total credits.
  • Erasing or overwriting an error. The professional convention is to draw a single line through the wrong figure, write the correction above, and initial the change.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "use the trial balance and the divide-by-nine and divide-by-two tests to locate common posting and transcription errors (transposition, slide, omission, wrong column)."

3.3 — The Eight-Column Worksheet
Structure: Trial Balance, Adjustments, Adjusted Trial Balance, Income Statement, Balance Sheet columns.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The eight-column worksheet (also called a spreadsheet) is a working paper that helps the accountant organise period-end adjustments and produce the financial statements without rewriting the entire ledger. The eight money columns are arranged in four pairs:

  1. Trial Balance (Dr / Cr) — the trial balance taken directly from the general ledger.
  2. Adjustments (Dr / Cr) — the period-end adjusting amounts that have not yet been posted.
  3. Adjusted Trial Balance (Dr / Cr) — the algebraic combination of columns 1 and 2.
  4. Income Statement (Dr / Cr) — revenue, expense, and the resulting net income (or loss).
  5. (also) Balance Sheet (Dr / Cr) — assets, liabilities, capital, drawings, and the offsetting net income (or loss).

Wait — that is five pairs, ten columns! In some Canadian textbooks the worksheet is presented as a ten-column worksheet. The eight-column version omits the explicit Adjusted Trial Balance pair (3) and instead carries the adjusted figures directly into either the Income Statement or Balance Sheet pair. For clarity we use the ten-column structure conceptually but most Canadian Grade 11 textbooks call it the "eight-column worksheet". Either way, the routine is the same:

  • Step 1 — Copy the trial balance into the Trial Balance columns.
  • Step 2 — Enter adjustments in the Adjustments columns (each adjustment labelled with a letter (a), (b), (c) … and a brief journal entry written below the worksheet).
  • Step 3 — Combine each row across (Trial Balance ± Adjustments) into the Adjusted Trial Balance.
  • Step 4 — Extend each row: revenue and expense accounts move into the Income Statement columns; assets, liabilities, capital, and drawings move into the Balance Sheet columns.
  • Step 5 — Total the Income Statement columns; the difference is net income (Dr) or net loss (Cr). Bring this amount down into the opposite side of the Balance Sheet columns so all four columns balance.

The worksheet is not a journal — adjustments still must be formally journalised and posted to the ledger after the worksheet is finished. The worksheet exists only to keep period-end work organised and to make statement preparation a near-mechanical task.

Example: For Park Tutoring at 30 April 2026, key worksheet rows (only a few accounts shown for illustration):

Account | TB Dr | TB Cr | Adj Dr| Adj Cr| Inc St Dr|Inc St Cr|Bal Sh Dr|Bal Sh Cr ----------------+-------+-------+-------+-------+----------+---------+---------+--------- Cash | 2 980 | | | | | | 2 980 | Supplies | 150 | | | (a)90| | | 60 | Tutoring Revenue| | 1 240 | | | | 1 240 | | Supplies Expense| 90 | | (a) 90| | 180 | | | Rent Expense | 450 | | | | 450 | | | Net Income → | | | | | 610 | | | 610

(Adjustment (a) is "Supplies used during April, \$90", which decreases the Supplies asset and increases Supplies Expense. The Income Statement columns total \$1 240 Cr and \$630 Dr (450 + 180), leaving net income of \$610 carried to Balance Sheet Cr.)

  • Treating worksheet entries as final journal entries; you must still journalise the adjustments separately.
  • Cross-extending an account into both Income Statement and Balance Sheet columns — each adjusted balance goes into exactly one statement pair.
  • Computing net income before the Adjusted Trial Balance balances — finish the adjusted trial balance first.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "complete an eight-column (or ten-column) worksheet by entering the trial balance, adjustments, adjusted trial balance, and extending the totals into income-statement and balance-sheet columns; use the worksheet to determine net income or net loss."

3.4 — Adjusting Entries: Supplies, Prepaid Expenses, and Depreciation
Deferred items needing period-end allocation; supplies used; insurance/rent expired; straight-line depreciation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The matching principle requires that each period's expenses be measured against that period's revenues. But many costs are paid before they are used (supplies, prepaid insurance, equipment), or earned/incurred but not yet recorded (accrued revenue or expense). Adjusting entries bring the accounts up to date at the end of each reporting period so that the income statement and balance sheet truly reflect economic reality.

Supplies used. The business buys office supplies and debits the Supplies asset account. At month-end, only some of the supplies remain; the difference is the expense. The adjusting entry is Dr. Supplies Expense, Cr. Supplies for the amount used.

Prepaid expenses. Insurance, rent, or licences paid in advance are debited to Prepaid Insurance (or Prepaid Rent). Each period, the portion that has expired must be transferred to an expense account: Dr. Insurance Expense, Cr. Prepaid Insurance.

Depreciation (straight-line). Long-lived assets (equipment, vehicles, buildings) lose service value as they are used. The straight-line method allocates an equal portion of cost to expense each period:

\[ \text{Annual Depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Estimated Residual Value}}{\text{Estimated Useful Life (years)}} \]

The entry is Dr. Depreciation Expense, Cr. Accumulated Depreciation — Equipment. Accumulated Depreciation is a contra-asset account: it has a credit balance and is shown on the balance sheet as a deduction from the related asset, producing the asset's net book value (NBV).

Example: Three April adjustments for Park Tutoring (year-end 30 April 2026).

(a) Supplies on hand 1 April \$150; physical count at 30 April \$60. Supplies used = \(150 - 60 = 90\).
Dr. Supplies Expense 90; Cr. Supplies 90.

(b) Prepaid Insurance balance \$120 (12-month policy paid on 1 April). One month expired = \(120 \div 12 = 10\).
Dr. Insurance Expense 10; Cr. Prepaid Insurance 10.

(c) Office equipment cost \$1 200, estimated residual \$120, estimated useful life 5 years. Monthly depreciation = \( \dfrac{1\,200 - 120}{5 \times 12} = \dfrac{1\,080}{60} = 18 \).
Dr. Depreciation Expense — Equipment 18; Cr. Accumulated Depreciation — Equipment 18.

After the three entries, Supplies asset is \$60, Prepaid Insurance is \$110, and Office Equipment shows on the balance sheet at \(1\,200 - 18 = 1\,182\) NBV.

  • Crediting the long-lived asset directly instead of Accumulated Depreciation — the asset stays at cost and the contra account accumulates depreciation.
  • Using the entire cost as the depreciable base; the residual value must be subtracted first.
  • Adjusting before the trial balance has been confirmed in balance; always reconcile then adjust.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "journalise and post period-end adjusting entries for supplies used, prepaid expenses, and depreciation on long-lived assets (using the straight-line method), and explain how the matching principle requires these adjustments."

3.5 — Accrued Revenue, Accrued Expenses, and Unearned Revenue
Revenue earned but not yet billed; expenses incurred but unpaid; cash received before revenue is earned.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Adjusting entries fall into two broad families: deferrals (cash already changed hands, the expense or revenue must now be allocated to the right period) and accruals (the expense or revenue has economically occurred, but cash has not yet changed hands and no source document exists). Section 3.4 covered the main deferrals. Section 3.5 covers accruals and the closely-related case of unearned revenue.

Accrued revenue. The business has earned revenue but has not yet invoiced the customer. The matching principle requires the revenue to be recorded in the period earned. Entry: Dr. Accounts Receivable (or Accrued Revenue), Cr. Service Revenue. When the invoice is later sent, the original receivable can be billed normally.

Accrued expenses. The business has incurred an expense but has not received the supplier's invoice yet. Common examples: salaries earned by employees but unpaid at month-end; interest on a loan that has accrued. Entry: Dr. Wages (or Interest) Expense, Cr. Wages (or Interest) Payable.

Unearned revenue. The business has collected cash before earning the revenue (a deposit, an annual subscription paid up front, a gift card sold). Cash is debited (it is the business's cash) but the credit goes to Unearned Revenue, a liability, because the business owes the customer either the service or the cash back. As the service is rendered each period, the adjusting entry transfers the earned portion into revenue: Dr. Unearned Revenue, Cr. Service Revenue.

Example: Three accrual/unearned-revenue adjustments at 30 April 2026.

(a) Park Tutoring has provided 4 hours of tutoring to a high-school student on 28–30 April, total \$80, not yet invoiced.
Dr. Accounts Receivable — Student 80; Cr. Tutoring Revenue 80.

(b) Salaries earned by a part-time assistant for the last three days of April: \$120. Pay day is 5 May.
Dr. Salaries Expense 120; Cr. Salaries Payable 120.

(c) On 1 April, a parent prepaid \$360 for a 6-week tutoring package. At month-end 4 weeks have elapsed, so \( \tfrac{4}{6} \cdot 360 = 240 \) has been earned.
Dr. Unearned Revenue 240; Cr. Tutoring Revenue 240. The remaining \$120 stays in the liability.

  • Treating Unearned Revenue as Revenue when cash arrives — it is a liability until earned.
  • Forgetting to reverse the accrual in the following period if the textbook uses reversing entries.
  • Recording the entire prepaid package as revenue at the moment of collection; this violates revenue recognition.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "journalise and post adjusting entries for accrued revenue, accrued expenses, and unearned revenue, and explain how each adjustment supports the matching and revenue-recognition principles."

3.6 — Closing Entries & the Post-Closing Trial Balance
Closing temporary accounts to Income Summary, then to Capital; the post-closing trial balance contains only permanent accounts.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The accounting period ends with closing entries that reduce the temporary accounts (revenue, expense, drawings) to zero so that next period starts fresh. The net effect of these entries is to transfer the period's net income or loss to the owner's Capital account. A clearing account called Income Summary is used to keep the closing process organised.

The four standard closing entries are:

  1. Close revenues to Income Summary: Dr. each Revenue account for its balance; Cr. Income Summary for the total. Revenue accounts now have zero balances.
  2. Close expenses to Income Summary: Dr. Income Summary for the total of expenses; Cr. each Expense account for its balance. Expense accounts now have zero balances.
  3. Close Income Summary to Capital: if Income Summary has a credit balance (net income), Dr. Income Summary, Cr. Capital. If Income Summary has a debit balance (net loss), reverse the entry.
  4. Close Drawings to Capital: Dr. Capital, Cr. Drawings, for the drawings balance.

After all four entries are posted, the only accounts with non-zero balances are the permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, capital). The post-closing trial balance lists these and once again must satisfy total Dr = total Cr. The Capital account in the post-closing trial balance is the new opening balance for the next period.

Example: Closing entries for Park Tutoring on 30 April 2026 (after adjustments). Adjusted balances: Tutoring Revenue \$1 560 Cr; Rent Expense \$450 Dr; Salaries Expense \$120 Dr; Supplies Expense \$90 Dr; Insurance Expense \$10 Dr; Depreciation Expense \$18 Dr; Drawings \$200 Dr; Capital \$3 800 Cr.

(1) Dr. Tutoring Revenue 1 560; Cr. Income Summary 1 560.

(2) Dr. Income Summary 688; Cr. Rent Expense 450; Cr. Salaries Expense 120; Cr. Supplies Expense 90; Cr. Insurance Expense 10; Cr. Depreciation Expense 18.

After (1) and (2), Income Summary has a credit balance of \(1\,560 - 688 = 872\) — the net income.

(3) Dr. Income Summary 872; Cr. J. Park, Capital 872. Income Summary now zero; Capital becomes \(3\,800 + 872 = 4\,672\).

(4) Dr. J. Park, Capital 200; Cr. J. Park, Drawings 200. Drawings now zero; Capital final balance \(4\,672 - 200 = 4\,472\). The post-closing trial balance will show this Capital balance.

  • Forgetting that Income Summary itself must end at zero — if it doesn't, an entry is incomplete.
  • Including Drawings in entry (2). Drawings is not an expense and is closed separately in entry (4).
  • Closing entries without first finishing all adjusting entries.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "journalise and post closing entries that transfer revenue, expense, and drawings balances to the owner's capital account through an Income Summary clearing account, and prepare a post-closing trial balance."
