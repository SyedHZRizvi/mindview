📖 1.1 What is Law? Functions, Categories, and the Rule of Law
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Law is the body of binding rules a society uses to order conduct, resolve disputes, and allocate the legitimate use of force. That definition does work in three directions. First, law is binding — it is not advisory; non-compliance attracts consequences enforced by the state. Second, law is public — it issues from recognised institutions (Parliament, legislatures, courts), not from private will. Third, law is general — it applies to categories of people and situations, not to named individuals. A grocery store's no-shoplifting sign is a private rule; section 322 of the Criminal Code, which defines theft, is law.

Canadian law performs four classic functions: it establishes rules of conduct (don't steal, drive on the right), it resolves disputes (courts and tribunals adjudicate competing claims), it protects rights and freedoms (the Charter, the Human Rights Code), and it provides for orderly change (legislation amends prior law, courts overrule prior cases). When students ask why something so trivial as a contract for a phone is "law," the answer is: because the same body of rules and procedures that prosecutes murder also enforces a missed payment, and the legitimacy of the whole system depends on consistency across that range.

Law is divided most usefully into public versus private. Public law governs relationships between the individual and the state: constitutional law (the supreme law), criminal law (offences against society), and administrative law (rules of government agencies). Private law governs relationships between individuals: contract law, tort law, property law, family law, and employment law. A speeding ticket is public (the Crown vs the driver); a slip-and-fall lawsuit is private (the injured person vs the store). The same physical event can spawn both — a drunk-driving collision can produce a criminal charge (public) and a civil suit for damages (private).

The rule of law is the principle that everyone — including the government — is subject to the law, that laws are applied predictably and equally, and that no one can be punished except for a breach of an established law. The Supreme Court reaffirmed this in the Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 217, identifying it as one of four unwritten constitutional principles. Distinguish the rule of law from rule by law: an authoritarian regime might still use laws to govern, but if the rulers themselves are not bound, and if the laws can be retroactively rewritten to punish enemies, the rule of law is absent.

  • Rule of law — government is bound by law; law is applied predictably and equally.
  • Public law — state vs individual: constitutional, criminal, administrative.
  • Private law — individual vs individual: contract, tort, property, family, employment.
  • Substantive law — defines rights and duties (theft, negligence).
  • Procedural law — defines how rights are enforced (arrest, trial, appeal).
  • Domestic law — law within one state; international law — between states.
  • Jurisprudence — the philosophy and theory of law.
  • Codified vs uncodified — written in a single statute book (e.g., Civil Code of Quebec) vs scattered across statutes and case law (common-law Canada).

Worked Example — Categorising a Single Incident

Scenario: Anna is driving and runs a red light, striking Ben's car. Ben suffers a broken arm. Anna had two beers earlier and blows over .08 on a roadside test.

This single incident triggers four distinct bodies of law:

  1. Criminal law (public, substantive): the Crown may charge Anna with impaired driving under s. 320.14 of the Criminal Code.
  2. Quasi-criminal / provincial regulatory (public): Anna also faces Highway Traffic Act charges (running a red, careless driving).
  3. Tort law (private, substantive): Ben can sue Anna in negligence for damages — broken arm, vehicle repair, lost income.
  4. Procedural law (public + private): the criminal trial follows the Criminal Code's procedural rules; the civil suit follows the Rules of Civil Procedure.

The same conduct produces independent proceedings. A criminal conviction does not automatically award Ben damages, and a successful civil suit does not put Anna in jail. Each system answers a different question.

  • Confusing the criminal and civil tracks ("she was acquitted, so he can't sue").
  • Treating "law" as identical to "police" or "court" — law is the rule; police enforce, courts adjudicate.
  • Forgetting that constitutional law sits above ordinary statute — Parliament cannot legislate around the Charter without invoking s. 33.
  • Calling the rule of law the same as "the law is always right." The rule of law means law applies equally; it does not guarantee a law is just.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "explain key concepts and principles related to Canadian law (e.g., rule of law, equality before the law, due process, presumption of innocence, judicial independence)."

🏛️ 1.2 Ancient Roots: Hammurabi, Mosaic Code, Roman Law, Magna Carta
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canadian law did not appear at Confederation in 1867; it inherits a sequence of legal innovations stretching back four thousand years. Understanding the heritage matters because it explains why we still use Latin terms (mens rea, habeas corpus, stare decisis), why an English common-law judgment is binding in Ontario, and why the presumption of innocence is treated as foundational rather than negotiable.

The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE, Babylon) is the oldest surviving written legal code. Its 282 provisions are inscribed on a basalt stele now in the Louvre. Hammurabi's contribution was not the content (lex talionis — "an eye for an eye" — is harsh by modern standards) but the form: the laws were public, written, and applied to categories of people. Citizens could read what the punishment for an offence would be, removing the king's arbitrary discretion. The principle that law must be promulgated — published in advance — descends from this innovation.

The Mosaic Code (the Ten Commandments and the broader Hebrew legal tradition of Exodus, Leviticus, Deuteronomy) introduced moral content alongside criminal prohibition. The Mosaic distinction between offences against persons and offences against property, and the requirement of multiple witnesses for capital offences (Deut. 19:15), prefigure modern evidentiary rules. Roman law, codified in the Corpus Juris Civilis of Emperor Justinian (529–534 CE), gave us much of the vocabulary of contract and property law, the structure of civil litigation, and the conceptual division between criminal and civil wrongs. Quebec's Civil Code descends directly from this Roman tradition via the Napoleonic Code of 1804.

The Magna Carta (1215) is often called the "Great Charter" because it constrained the English king for the first time. Forced on King John by his barons at Runnymede, it included clauses guaranteeing that "no free man shall be seized or imprisoned … except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land." This is the deep ancestor of two ideas central to Canadian law: habeas corpus (the right not to be detained arbitrarily, now in s. 10(c) of the Charter) and trial by jury (now in s. 11(f)). The Magna Carta is not a code Canadians cite directly, but the principles it crystallised — that the sovereign is bound by law, and that detention requires lawful authority — flow into the Constitution Act, 1982. Indigenous legal traditions, treated in the next topic, develop in parallel and have their own equally ancient sources.

  • Code of Hammurabi — c. 1754 BCE; first public, written legal code.
  • Lex talionis — "law of retaliation"; proportional retribution.
  • Mosaic Code — Hebrew legal tradition, moral content + procedure.
  • Roman law — codified in Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis; ancestor of civil-law systems.
  • Magna Carta — 1215; constrained the king, ancestor of habeas corpus and jury trial.
  • Habeas corpus — "you have the body"; right to challenge unlawful detention.
  • Promulgation — official publication of a law so people can know it.
  • Civil-law tradition — codified law (France, Quebec for private matters).
  • Common-law tradition — judge-made precedent (England, Canada outside Quebec for private matters).

Worked Example — Tracing a Modern Right to Its Source

Canadian Charter, s. 10(c): "Everyone has the right on arrest or detention … to have the validity of the detention determined by way of habeas corpus and to be released if the detention is not lawful."

Trace the lineage of this single sentence:

  1. Magna Carta (1215), clause 39: "No free man shall be seized or imprisoned … except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land."
  2. English Habeas Corpus Act (1679): codified the writ as a binding procedural remedy.
  3. Reception in colonial British North America: common-law writs travelled with British colonial law to Upper Canada and the Maritimes.
  4. Canadian Bill of Rights (1960): statutory recognition of habeas corpus.
  5. Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982): constitutional entrenchment in s. 10(c) — Parliament can no longer extinguish it by ordinary statute.

One Charter section, eight hundred years of pedigree. This is what "heritage of Canadian law" means concretely.

  • Treating Hammurabi as "primitive" — it is the formal innovation (written, public, categorical) that matters, not the cruelty of specific penalties.
  • Calling Roman law "the law of Italy today" — Roman law shaped civil-law systems globally; modern Italian law is one descendant among many.
  • Believing the Magna Carta gave rights to all English people — it bound the king only in relation to the barons; expansion came later.
  • Forgetting that Indigenous legal traditions have their own equally ancient sources, treated in the next topic.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "analyse contributions to the heritage of Canadian law from a variety of sources (e.g., Indigenous legal traditions, the Code of Hammurabi, the Mosaic Code, Roman law, Greek philosophy, the Magna Carta)."

🪶 1.3 Indigenous Legal Traditions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Indigenous legal traditions are not a single body. They are dozens of distinct legal orders developed by the Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee, Cree, Inuit, Mi'kmaq, Coast Salish, Métis, and many other nations over thousands of years — long before any European contact. Each has its own sources of authority, dispute-resolution practices, and ways of teaching law to the next generation. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 50 explicitly directs the federal government to fund the establishment of Indigenous law institutes, and the Supreme Court of Canada has increasingly treated Indigenous law as a source of Canadian law, not merely a subject for it.

Indigenous legal traditions typically share several features. Oral transmission — many legal principles are carried in story, ceremony, and song rather than written codes. This does not make them less binding; the Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa) has structured the Six Nations Confederacy since long before European contact. Restorative orientation — many traditions treat wrongdoing as a tear in the relationship between offender, victim, family, and community, repaired through circle processes, apology, restitution, and ceremony rather than primarily through punishment. Relational obligation — law extends beyond human beings to obligations toward the land, the water, and non-human relatives. Embedded learning — Elders, ceremonies, and stories are pedagogical institutions equivalent in function to courts and law schools.

Canadian law has historically suppressed Indigenous legal orders. The Indian Act of 1876 (and its various amendments) imposed band-council governance, criminalised the potlatch and sun dance (1885 amendment), and removed children to residential schools — all designed to displace Indigenous law with settler law. The 2008 Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented the harm, and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (2021) commits Canada to align federal law with UNDRIP, including its recognition of Indigenous self-government and legal traditions.

The Supreme Court has begun to give Indigenous law direct weight inside Canadian law. R v Gladue [1999] 1 SCR 688 requires sentencing judges to consider the unique circumstances of Indigenous offenders, including the impact of residential schools and intergenerational trauma. Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia [2014] 2 SCR 257 recognised Aboriginal title to a specific tract of land for the first time, treating Tsilhqot'in legal understanding of their territory as evidence of title. Reference re An Act respecting First Nations, Inuit and Métis children [2024] (Bill C-92) affirmed that Indigenous nations may exercise jurisdiction over child and family services. The direction is unmistakable: Canadian law increasingly recognises that there is not one Canadian legal heritage but many, and that Indigenous legal traditions are one of them.

  • Anishinaabe / Cree / Haudenosaunee / Inuit / Mi'kmaq — distinct nations with distinct legal traditions.
  • Kaianere'kó:wa (Great Law of Peace) — Haudenosaunee constitutional law.
  • Oral tradition — legal authority carried in story, ceremony, song.
  • Restorative justice — repair of relationships, not primarily punishment.
  • Gladue principles — sentencing must consider Indigenous offender circumstances.
  • Aboriginal title — communal Indigenous land right recognised in s. 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
  • UNDRIP — UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007); domesticated in Canada via 2021 Act.
  • Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) — investigated residential schools; issued 94 Calls to Action.

Worked Example — R v Gladue in Practice

Jamie Gladue, a 19-year-old Cree woman, pleaded guilty to manslaughter in the killing of her common-law partner. The trial judge sentenced her to three years without giving particular weight to her Indigenous identity. On appeal, the Supreme Court interpreted s. 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code — "all available sanctions … other than imprisonment that are reasonable in the circumstances should be considered for all offenders, with particular attention to the circumstances of Aboriginal offenders."

The Court held that sentencing judges must:

  1. Consider the unique systemic and background factors affecting Indigenous people (residential schools, dislocation, poverty, intergenerational trauma);
  2. Consider sentencing procedures and sanctions that may be appropriate in the circumstances of the offender because of his or her particular Aboriginal heritage or connection.

This is not a "discount" for Indigenous offenders — it is a recognition that the heritage of Canadian law includes Indigenous legal perspectives on accountability and repair, and that ignoring them produced systemic over-incarceration. Indigenous people make up about 5% of Canada's adult population but, in 2023, over 32% of federal inmates (Office of the Correctional Investigator). Gladue is a corrective.

  • Treating Indigenous legal traditions as a single homogenous body — there are dozens of distinct orders.
  • Calling oral tradition "informal" — orality is a method of transmission, not a measure of authority.
  • Treating reconciliation as a past project — the Supreme Court has called it "an ongoing process."
  • Confusing Aboriginal rights (s. 35) with Charter rights (ss. 1–34) — they are distinct constitutional categories.

🇨🇦 1.4 The Constitution Acts of 1867 and 1982
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Canadian Constitution is the supreme law: every statute, regulation, and government action must conform to it, and any that does not is, in the words of s. 52(1) of the Constitution Act, 1982, "to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect." The Constitution is not a single document but a set of documents and unwritten principles, the two most important of which are the Constitution Act, 1867 (originally the British North America Act) and the Constitution Act, 1982 (which includes the Charter and the patriation amending formula).

The Constitution Act, 1867 created Canada on July 1, 1867, by uniting the British colonies of Canada (split into Ontario and Quebec), Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick into a federal dominion. Its most consequential provisions are sections 91 and 92, which divide legislative authority between the federal Parliament (s. 91 — criminal law, banking, navigation, postal service, "Peace, Order, and good Government") and the provincial legislatures (s. 92 — property and civil rights, hospitals, municipalities, the administration of justice in the province). Federalism is therefore baked into the constitutional architecture: most disputes about which level of government can pass a particular law are resolved by reading sections 91 and 92 against each other.

From 1867 until 1982, Canada had a peculiar constitutional status: the BNA Act was a British statute, and constitutional amendments required a request to the UK Parliament. The Constitution Act, 1982 ended this dependency through patriation. Three innovations matter most. First, the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (ss. 1–34) entrenched fundamental rights as constitutional law, lifting them out of ordinary parliamentary control. Second, section 35 recognised and affirmed "the existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada." Third, the amending formula (Part V) prescribed how the Constitution can be amended without going to Westminster — generally, by resolutions of Parliament and at least seven provinces representing 50% of the population (the "7/50 rule"), with some matters requiring unanimous provincial consent.

Quebec did not sign the 1982 patriation, a fact still politically significant. Two attempts to bring Quebec into the constitutional family — the Meech Lake Accord (1987–1990) and the Charlottetown Accord (1992) — failed. The Supreme Court in the Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 217 nevertheless held that the Constitution applies in Quebec and that Quebec cannot unilaterally secede, while also recognising that a clear referendum result on a clear question would give rise to a duty to negotiate. The Reference is also the leading articulation of four unwritten constitutional principles: federalism, democracy, constitutionalism and the rule of law, and the protection of minorities. These principles are not just decorative; the Supreme Court has used them to fill gaps in the written text.

  • Constitution Act, 1867 — created Canada; ss. 91–92 divide federal/provincial powers.
  • Constitution Act, 1982 — patriated the Constitution; entrenched the Charter; recognised Aboriginal and treaty rights (s. 35).
  • Patriation — moving constitutional amending authority from Westminster to Canada.
  • Section 52(1) — the Constitution is "the supreme law of Canada."
  • Section 91 / Section 92 — federal / provincial heads of legislative power.
  • Amending formula — Part V of the 1982 Act; "7/50 rule" + unanimity for special matters.
  • Section 35 — Aboriginal and treaty rights (a constitutional category distinct from Charter rights).
  • Unwritten constitutional principles — federalism, democracy, rule of law, protection of minorities.

Worked Example — Section 91/92 Division-of-Powers Analysis

Suppose Ontario passes a statute prohibiting the sale of fireworks to minors. A retailer argues the statute is unconstitutional because regulating fireworks is "criminal law" under s. 91(27), a federal head of power.

Step 1 — Identify the pith and substance. Courts ask what the law is really about: its dominant purpose and effect. Here, the dominant purpose is consumer protection and public safety for minors. The dominant effect is to limit who can buy a product, not to prohibit its existence.

Step 2 — Assign to a head of power. Criminal law (s. 91(27)) requires a prohibition + penalty + valid criminal-law purpose (peace, order, security, health, morality). Provincial consumer protection (s. 92(13) — property and civil rights) covers regulation of who may transact, when, and how.

Step 3 — Apply doctrine. The Supreme Court's Reference re Firearms Act [2000] 1 SCR 783 confirms that prohibition with penalty can be criminal-law (federal); but a regulation merely restricting sale to adults, without a per-se prohibition, sits naturally under provincial property-and-civil-rights authority. Ontario can regulate this.

This analytical structure — pith and substance, then head of power, then doctrine — is the workhorse of Canadian division-of-powers jurisprudence.

  • Saying the Constitution is "just the Charter" — the Charter is one part of the 1982 Act; the 1867 Act and s. 35 are equally constitutional.
  • Treating ss. 91 and 92 as exhaustive — the residual "POGG" clause and the "double aspect" doctrine fill gaps.
  • Forgetting that ordinary statutes can be struck down for breaching the Constitution — s. 52(1) is the basis for judicial review.
  • Conflating "supreme law" with "perfect law" — the Constitution has been amended (1985, 1993) and could be again.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "describe the division of powers between federal and provincial governments as set out in sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867."

⚖️ 1.5 Common Law, Civil Law, and Statute Law
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada is a bijural country: it operates simultaneously under the British common-law tradition (used in all provinces and territories except Quebec for private matters, and across Canada for federal matters) and the French civil-law tradition (used in Quebec for private matters through the Civil Code of Quebec). Sorting out which tradition supplies which rule is part of doing law in Canada, and it matters because the two traditions produce law in different ways.

In the common-law tradition, much of private law (contracts, torts, property) develops case by case through judges' written reasons for decision. When a higher court resolves a novel question, lower courts in the same hierarchy must follow that resolution in similar cases — the doctrine of stare decisis ("let the decision stand"). A judge confronted with a new dispute looks for a precedent that fits and reasons by analogy. The reasoning is inductive: build from particular cases to general principles. The vast body of judge-made rules is called case law.

In the civil-law tradition, by contrast, the starting point is a comprehensive code. The Civil Code of Quebec (in force since 1994, replacing the older 1866 Code) sets out general rules in numbered articles, and judges apply those articles to particular cases. The reasoning is deductive: build from the code's general principle to the particular outcome. Prior cases are persuasive but not strictly binding in the way they are in common law. The civil-law tradition descends from Roman law through the Napoleonic Code (1804).

Both traditions in Canada coexist with statute law: legislation passed by Parliament or by a provincial legislature, then signed into force. Statute law is the dominant modern source of new legal rules; whenever Parliament passes the Criminal Code or Ontario passes the Employment Standards Act, they create law that displaces inconsistent common-law rules in that area. Where statute and common law conflict, statute wins (assuming the statute is itself constitutional). Where statute is silent, common law fills in. A modern lawyer therefore reads four sources together: the constitutional text, the relevant statute, the regulations made under the statute, and the case law interpreting both. Statutory interpretation — figuring out what an ambiguous section of a statute actually means — has become a major skill, governed by the modern principle articulated in Rizzo & Rizzo Shoes Ltd. (Re) [1998] 1 SCR 27: "the words of an Act are to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, the object of the Act, and the intention of Parliament."

  • Common law — judge-made law developed through case-by-case decisions; binding on lower courts via stare decisis.
  • Civil law (Quebec, private matters) — codified law in the Civil Code of Quebec.
  • Statute law — Acts passed by Parliament or a provincial legislature.
  • Regulation — subordinate legislation made by Cabinet or a minister under authority of a parent statute.
  • Stare decisis — "let the decision stand"; lower courts bound by higher-court precedent.
  • Binding precedent — same jurisdiction, same or higher court, on substantially the same point of law.
  • Persuasive precedent — from another jurisdiction, or from a lower court; can be cited but is not binding.
  • Ratio decidendi — the binding legal principle of a case; obiter dicta — non-binding remarks.
  • Codification — gathering scattered rules into a single statute or code.

Worked Example — How Stare Decisis Constrains a Trial Judge

A 19-year-old in Ontario buys a chocolate-covered ice-cream bar. Inside, she finds part of a decomposing snail. She sues the bar's manufacturer in tort for damages. There is no contract — she bought it from a corner store, not the manufacturer.

The trial judge looks for a precedent. The leading case is Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 (House of Lords), in which Mrs. Donoghue's friend bought her a ginger beer in Paisley, Scotland, and she discovered the remains of a decomposed snail. Lord Atkin held that manufacturers owe a duty of care to ultimate consumers, even in the absence of a contractual relationship — the famous "neighbour principle": "you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."

The trial judge in Ontario in 2024 is bound — not by the House of Lords as such, but by the long line of Canadian appellate decisions (including from the Supreme Court of Canada) that adopt the neighbour principle as Canadian law. He applies it to a fact pattern with a different dessert and a different geography, and the manufacturer is liable. This is stare decisis in action: a 1932 Scottish snail constrains a 2024 Ontario judge because the principle has been adopted into Canadian common law.

  • Mixing up "common law" with "civil law" — in Canada, "civil" can mean either (a) non-criminal private law generally, or (b) the Quebec civil-code tradition specifically. Context decides.
  • Believing courts can ignore precedent if they "disagree." Lower courts generally cannot; the Supreme Court can overrule its own prior decisions, but rarely does.
  • Treating regulations as inferior in force to statutes — properly made regulations have the force of law.
  • Forgetting that an unconstitutional statute is invalid — supremacy of the Constitution outranks supremacy of Parliament.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "explain the relationship between common law and statute law and how each is created and applied."

🏛️ 1.6 Court System & Legal Actors
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Canadian court system is a pyramid. At its base are the provincial courts that hear the bulk of everyday matters; at its apex is the Supreme Court of Canada (SCC). A decision moves up the pyramid only through appeal, and only if leave (permission) is granted or an appeal lies as of right. Understanding the structure lets you read a case citation and know immediately what weight the case carries.

In Ontario, the courts in ascending order are: the Ontario Court of Justice (OCJ) hears most criminal matters (90% of criminal trials), provincial-offence matters, family-law matters under the OCJ's jurisdiction, and youth-justice matters; judges are appointed provincially. The Superior Court of Justice (SCJ) is a section-96 court (federally appointed judges), hearing serious criminal indictable offences (e.g., murder), all civil claims over $35,000, family-law matters including divorce, estate matters, and judicial review of administrative decisions. The Small Claims Court is a branch of the SCJ handling claims up to $35,000. The Divisional Court handles statutory appeals and judicial review. The Court of Appeal for Ontario hears appeals from the SCJ and certain appeals from the OCJ. Above all sits the Supreme Court of Canada, which hears appeals from all provinces by leave (usually) — about 70 cases per year out of 600 leave applications.

Federal courts run a parallel system: the Federal Court, the Federal Court of Appeal, and various specialised federal courts (Tax Court, Court Martial). These hear matters within federal jurisdiction — immigration, intellectual-property, federal-employee disputes, certain Indigenous matters, tax. Appeals from the Federal Court of Appeal also go to the Supreme Court of Canada.

Legal actors fall into several roles. A judge presides over a court, manages the trial, and renders the verdict or judgment (the Crown attorney prosecutes criminal matters on behalf of the state); a defence counsel represents the accused; a plaintiff's lawyer and a defendant's lawyer represent the parties in civil cases. A justice of the peace handles bail hearings, search-warrant applications, and certain provincial offences. A jury (12 members in criminal cases, 6 in some civil) is the fact-finder where a jury trial is held. A paralegal is licensed by the Law Society of Ontario to provide certain legal services (small claims, traffic, residential-tenancy disputes). Judicial independence — the principle that judges decide free of governmental, political, or personal influence — is constitutionally protected and is one of the foundations on which the rule of law rests.

  • Ontario Court of Justice (OCJ) — provincial appointments; most criminal trials, youth justice, family.
  • Superior Court of Justice (SCJ) — federal appointments under s. 96; serious criminal and major civil.
  • Court of Appeal for Ontario — provincial appellate court; cited as e.g. R v X, 2024 ONCA 100.
  • Supreme Court of Canada — final court of appeal; 9 justices including 3 from Quebec.
  • Federal Court / Federal Court of Appeal — federal-matter jurisdiction.
  • Crown attorney — prosecutes criminal matters; represents society, not the victim.
  • Defence counsel — represents the accused.
  • Justice of the peace — bail hearings, search warrants, provincial offences.
  • Judicial independence — judges decide free of external influence; security of tenure, financial security, administrative independence.
  • Citation — the standard way to refer to a case (court, year, parties, reporter), e.g., R v Oakes, [1986] 1 SCR 103.

Worked Example — Reading a Case Citation

Citation: R v Brown, 2022 SCC 18, [2022] 1 SCR 374.

Decoded:

  • R = Regina (the Crown). The case is a criminal matter — Crown vs accused.
  • Brown = the accused (or appellant on appeal).
  • 2022 = the year of the decision.
  • SCC = the court — Supreme Court of Canada.
  • 18 = the 18th decision the Supreme Court released that year (the "neutral citation" number).
  • [2022] 1 SCR 374 = the parallel citation to the Supreme Court Reports, volume 1, page 374.

From this citation alone you know: it is binding on every court in Canada below the SCC; it is recent; and you can find it on CanLII or the SCC's website. If a citation says "2024 ONCA 100" you know it is from the Ontario Court of Appeal in 2024 — binding on Ontario trial courts but not (technically) outside Ontario. "2023 ONSC 4500" is the Ontario Superior Court of Justice. Citation literacy is a foundational skill of Strand A.

  • Confusing the Ontario Court of Justice (provincial) with the Superior Court of Justice (federal-appointment) — different judges, different jurisdictions.
  • Believing the Supreme Court hears every appeal — it grants leave in roughly 12% of leave applications.
  • Treating the Crown attorney as the victim's lawyer — the Crown represents the state and society; the victim has separate rights (Victims' Bill of Rights) but does not direct the prosecution.
  • Confusing "judicial independence" with "no oversight" — judges are independent in their decision-making but subject to the Canadian Judicial Council on conduct.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "explain the structure of the Canadian court system (provincial courts, superior courts, courts of appeal, Supreme Court of Canada)."
