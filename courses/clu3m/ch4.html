🤝 4.1 Tort Law & Negligence
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A tort is a civil wrong — a breach of a duty owed by one person to another that causes loss and is actionable in the courts. Tort law is private law: the plaintiff (the injured party) sues the defendant for damages, and the action is independent of any criminal prosecution that might arise from the same conduct. Torts are divided into intentional torts (where the defendant intended the contact or consequence) and negligence (where the defendant failed to take reasonable care). A third category, strict liability, covers a narrow set of "abnormally dangerous" activities (e.g., keeping wild animals) and dispenses with the need to prove fault.

The intentional torts include assault (intentional threat creating a reasonable apprehension of imminent contact), battery (intentional offensive or harmful contact), false imprisonment (intentional confinement without lawful authority), trespass to land (intentional unauthorised entry), conversion (intentional dealing with another's chattels), and defamation (a published statement that lowers the plaintiff's reputation). Each has its own elements but they share an "intent" requirement — the defendant must have intended the act, even if not its precise consequence. Defences include consent (sometimes implied), legal authority (police arrest), self-defence, and qualified privilege (defamation in certain contexts).

The leading case in negligence remains Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 — Mrs. Donoghue's friend bought her a ginger beer, she drank some, and the remains of a decomposed snail emerged when she poured the rest. Lord Atkin held that the manufacturer owed a duty of care to the ultimate consumer, articulating the "neighbour principle": "you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour." The modern Canadian formulation of the negligence claim has four elements: (1) the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care; (2) the defendant breached the standard of care; (3) the breach caused (factually and legally) the plaintiff's damage; (4) the damage is not too remote and is one the law compensates.

The duty of care is determined under the Anns / Cooper framework (Cooper v Hobart [2001] 3 SCR 537). First, was there a recognised category of duty? If yes, duty is established. If not, ask whether the harm was reasonably foreseeable and whether there was sufficient "proximity" between the parties — then ask whether any policy considerations negate the duty. The standard of care is what a reasonable person in the defendant's circumstances would have done. Causation is generally tested by the "but for" test: but for the defendant's negligence, would the plaintiff have suffered the harm (Clements v Clements [2012] 2 SCR 181)? Damages compensate the plaintiff for actual loss — physical injury, property damage, lost income, pain and suffering — and aim to put the plaintiff in the position they would have been in absent the wrong (restitutio in integrum).

  • Tort — civil wrong actionable by the injured party.
  • Intentional torts — assault, battery, false imprisonment, trespass, defamation, conversion.
  • Negligence — failure to take reasonable care (the dominant modern tort).
  • Duty of care — the relationship that obliges reasonable care (Donoghue, Anns/Cooper).
  • Standard of care — what a reasonable person would do.
  • Causation — "but for" the breach, the harm would not have occurred (Clements).
  • Remoteness — only foreseeable harms are compensable (Wagon Mound).
  • Damages — pecuniary (income, costs) + non-pecuniary (pain, suffering); capped for non-pecuniary in personal injury since the 1978 "trilogy."
  • Contributory negligence — plaintiff's own fault reduces recovery proportionally (Ontario Negligence Act).
  • Strict liability — no fault required; narrow categories.

Worked Example — Slip and Fall Negligence Action

On a snowy December afternoon, Priya enters a grocery store. The mat at the entrance has not been replaced since morning, and water has pooled. She slips, falls, and fractures her wrist. She sues the store for negligence in the Superior Court of Justice (claim of $80,000, above small-claims limit).

Applying the four-element framework:

  1. Duty of care: Occupiers' Liability Act, RSO 1990, c. O.2, s. 3(1) imposes a duty on occupiers to take such care as is reasonable in the circumstances to ensure persons entering are reasonably safe. Established category; duty exists.
  2. Standard of care: what would a reasonable grocery store occupier do on a snowy December day? Likely: place mats, monitor pooling, post warning signs, replace mats as they saturate. Did this store do those things? On the facts, no.
  3. Causation: but for the saturated mat and pooled water, would Priya have fallen? Plaintiff bears the burden on a balance of probabilities. The facts (water present, sudden slip, no other cause) make this provable.
  4. Damages and remoteness: a wrist fracture from a slip on a wet floor is squarely foreseeable. Quantum (the amount): special damages — medical costs, lost wages; general damages — pain and suffering, possible permanent loss of function. Subject to non-pecuniary cap.
  5. Defences: contributory negligence — was Priya running, distracted, wearing inappropriate shoes? If yes, the court reduces her recovery proportionally under the Negligence Act.

A plausible outcome: judgment for Priya in the range $35,000–$60,000, possibly reduced by 10–20% for contributory negligence if she was distracted by her phone.

  • Confusing battery (contact) with assault (apprehension of contact) — both can be present, but they are distinct torts.
  • Treating any accident as negligence — fault is the controlling concept; bad luck without breach is not actionable.
  • Forgetting causation requires "but for" — proving breach is not the same as proving the breach caused the harm.
  • Believing the criminal and civil tracks are interchangeable — different burdens (beyond reasonable doubt vs balance of probabilities), different remedies (punishment vs compensation), different rules.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E1.2 — "analyse the elements of negligence (duty of care, standard of care, causation, damages) as set out in Donoghue v Stevenson."

📝 4.2 Contract Law
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties. Not every promise is a contract, and not every signed document is enforceable. The common-law rules for contract formation require five elements: offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, and intention to create legal relations. Some contracts also require a particular form (writing under the Statute of Frauds — land contracts, guarantees, agreements that cannot be performed in one year), and contracts for illegal objects are unenforceable on public-policy grounds.

An offer is a clear proposal capable of acceptance — a definite indication of willingness to be bound on stated terms. An offer is distinct from an invitation to treat, which is a preliminary indication that the party will consider offers. A storefront price tag is an invitation to treat (the customer offers to buy at that price; the store accepts at the cashier). Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. [1893] 1 QB 256 famously held that an advertisement promising £100 to anyone who used the product and still caught influenza was a unilateral offer (because the deposit of £1,000 in the bank made the promise specific and intended); generally, however, advertisements are invitations to treat.

Acceptance is unconditional agreement to the terms of the offer, communicated to the offeror. Acceptance can be express (signature, "I agree") or implied (performing the act called for in a unilateral offer). The acceptance must match the offer exactly — a "yes, but with this change" is a counter-offer, which destroys the original offer (Hyde v Wrench [1840] 49 ER 132). The postal acceptance rule, useful but quaint, treats acceptance as complete when posted; electronic acceptance is generally complete when received by the offeror.

Consideration is what each party gives — the price paid for the promise. A gratuitous promise is generally not enforceable absent consideration ("a bare promise"). The consideration must move from the promisee to the promisor and must be of some value, but courts do not generally assess the adequacy of consideration ("peppercorn rule"). Capacity requires that the parties have legal capacity to contract — minors generally have limited capacity (contracts for necessaries are enforceable; others are voidable at the minor's option); the mentally incapable lack capacity. Intention to create legal relations is presumed in commercial dealings and presumed absent in domestic dealings (Balfour v Balfour [1919] 2 KB 571). Contracts can be voidable for vitiating factors: misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence, or unconscionability. Where one party has materially deceived or coerced the other, the agreement can be set aside even though all formal elements are present.

  • Offer — clear proposal capable of acceptance (vs invitation to treat).
  • Acceptance — unconditional agreement; communicated to offeror.
  • Consideration — value exchanged; the price of the promise.
  • Capacity — legal ability to contract (age, mental capacity).
  • Intention — to create legal relations (presumed in commercial, presumed absent in social/domestic).
  • Form / Statute of Frauds — certain contracts must be in writing (land, guarantees, > 1 year).
  • Vitiating factors — misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence, unconscionability.
  • Breach — failure to perform a contractual obligation.
  • Remedies — damages (compensatory, mitigation required), specific performance (equitable, rare for chattels), rescission (undo the contract), injunction.

Worked Example — Was There a Contract?

Maya owns a used bike. On Facebook Marketplace she posts: "Trek hybrid, $400 OBO." Sam messages: "I'll take it for $400, can pick up tomorrow." Maya replies "Great, see you at 2." Sam arrives, but Maya has sold the bike to Jordan for $450 in the interim. Sam sues Maya in small-claims court for breach of contract.

Analysis:

  1. Offer: Maya's post is arguably an invitation to treat (advertisements typically are). Sam's "I'll take it for $400" is the offer. Maya's "Great, see you at 2" is the acceptance — clear, communicated, on the offered terms.
  2. Consideration: Sam will pay $400; Maya will transfer the bike. Both sides give value.
  3. Capacity: both adults; no incapacity issue.
  4. Intention: commercial-like context; presumption of intention applies.
  5. Form: no writing requirement for a chattel sale of this value; messages constitute written record in any event.
  6. Conclusion: a contract was formed. Maya breached by selling to Jordan. Sam's remedy is damages for the difference between contract price and substitute purchase price (if he had to buy a comparable bike at a higher price). Specific performance is rare for non-unique chattels — Sam will not get this particular bike; he will get money damages.

The lesson: contracts can form quickly, often without a signature, often in chat messages. The five elements check whether enforceability has crystallised.

  • Treating signature as the only proof of contract — an exchange of messages can be enough.
  • Confusing offer with invitation to treat — a price tag is not an offer; the customer offers.
  • Forgetting consideration — gratuitous promises generally fail (with exceptions for promissory estoppel).
  • Assuming "all contracts must be in writing" — only specific categories require writing under the Statute of Frauds.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.1 — "identify the essential elements of a valid contract (offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, legality, intention to create legal relations)."

🛒 4.3 Consumer Protection
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The common law of contract assumes parties of equal bargaining power. In modern markets, that assumption is often false — a consumer buying a phone, a car, or a payday loan does not negotiate terms; she accepts the standard-form contract the seller offers. Consumer-protection legislation corrects for this imbalance by imposing mandatory rules that override contractual freedom. In Ontario the primary statute is the Consumer Protection Act, 2002 (CPA), supplemented by sector-specific legislation (Sale of Goods Act, Payday Loans Act, Motor Vehicle Dealers Act).

The CPA applies to "consumer agreements" — those between a "supplier" (in the business of doing the activity) and a "consumer" (an individual acting for personal, family, or household purposes; not a business buyer). Several protections sit at the core:

  • Cooling-off period (s. 43): consumers can cancel certain contracts without reason within 10 days of receiving the written copy (direct agreements, time-share agreements). Other categories (gym memberships under s. 31) have longer cooling-off rights.
  • Disclosure requirements: certain contracts (credit, leasing, time-shares, gym memberships) must contain specific written disclosures; failure to disclose entitles the consumer to cancel.
  • Unfair practices (Part III): false, misleading, or deceptive representations are prohibited; unconscionable acts (e.g., taking advantage of inability to understand, exorbitant price) are remediable.
  • Warranty (s. 9): services must be supplied to a reasonably acceptable quality; goods sold are subject to the Sale of Goods Act's implied conditions and warranties (fitness for purpose, merchantable quality, sale by description).

The Sale of Goods Act, RSO 1990, c. S.1, contains implied terms that operate regardless of what the contract says (subject to limited disclaimers for non-consumer sales). Sections 14–16 imply conditions about title, quality, and fitness for purpose. A consumer who buys a phone that doesn't work as described has an SGA claim independent of any specific warranty the merchant offered. The CPA further provides that any waiver or release of consumer rights is generally void (s. 7).

Beyond statute, the common law doctrines of misrepresentation and unconscionability remain available. A misrepresentation can be fraudulent (knowingly false), negligent (carelessly false), or innocent (false but without fault); the available remedy varies. An unconscionable contract — one in which one party took advantage of the other's vulnerability and the terms are substantively unfair — can be set aside in equity. The Supreme Court in Uber Technologies Inc v Heller [2020] 2 SCR 118 used the doctrine to invalidate Uber's arbitration clause that would have required a Toronto driver to arbitrate in the Netherlands at a cost of $14,500 — the court held the clause was unconscionable. Consumer protection therefore has both statutory and common-law sources.

  • Consumer Protection Act, 2002 (Ontario) — main statutory framework.
  • Sale of Goods Act (Ontario) — implied conditions and warranties.
  • Cooling-off period — 10 days for direct agreements (s. 43 CPA).
  • Unfair practices — false / misleading / deceptive / unconscionable conduct (Part III CPA).
  • Implied warranty — fitness for purpose, merchantable quality.
  • Misrepresentation — fraudulent / negligent / innocent.
  • Unconscionability — one party took advantage; terms substantively unfair (Uber v Heller).
  • Standard-form contracts (contracts of adhesion) — interpreted strictly against the drafter (contra proferentem).
  • Class action — collective consumer remedy under Class Proceedings Act, 1992.

Worked Example — Cooling-Off Right

A door-to-door salesperson signs Tomas, age 22, to a 36-month rental contract on a water-heater system in his apartment. The monthly payment is $89; cancellation costs are listed as "the balance of payments owing." Three days later, Tomas reads the contract and realises he can buy a comparable unit outright for $300.

Analysis under CPA:

  1. Is this a "direct agreement"? Yes — it was entered into between supplier and consumer at a place other than the supplier's place of business (s. 20). The CPA classifies door-to-door agreements as direct agreements.
  2. Cooling-off period (s. 43): a consumer can cancel a direct agreement within 10 days of receiving the written copy. Tomas is at day 3 — well within. He can cancel for any reason.
  3. How to cancel: s. 92 requires written notice; the consumer can deliver it any reasonable way. Tomas emails the supplier formally cancelling under s. 43.
  4. Effect: the contract is terminated. Section 96 directs that any deposit or payment made must be refunded within 15 days. Tomas owes nothing further.
  5. If the cooling-off period had passed (e.g., day 30), Tomas would not have an automatic right to cancel but could still challenge under unfair practices or unconscionability if applicable — those have different burdens.

The cooling-off period is a structural recognition that direct-sales pressure tactics produce regret; consumer law gives a no-fault unwind.

  • Confusing the cooling-off period with a "satisfaction guarantee" — the cooling-off right is statutory, no questions asked, time-limited.
  • Believing all contracts have cooling-off rights — only specified categories under the CPA.
  • Treating arbitration clauses as iron-clad — Uber v Heller shows they can be unconscionable.
  • Forgetting that the Sale of Goods Act overrides "as-is" disclaimers in consumer sales.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E2.2 — "analyse consumer-protection legislation (Ontario Consumer Protection Act, 2002) and unconscionable contracts."

💼 4.4 Employment Law
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Employment law in Ontario draws from three layers: the common law of employment (contract + duty of good faith), the Employment Standards Act, 2000 (ESA — minimum standards), and other statutes that protect workers (Occupational Health and Safety Act, Workplace Safety and Insurance Act, Ontario Human Rights Code, Labour Relations Act for unionised settings). The ESA sets the floor — minimum wage, hours of work, vacation, public holidays, leaves of absence, notice and termination pay — that the parties can contract to exceed but not below.

The first analytical question in employment disputes is the characterisation of the working relationship: employee, dependent contractor, or independent contractor? An employee is hired under a contract of service, works under direction, uses the employer's tools, and is paid through payroll. An independent contractor is hired under a contract for services, controls how the work is done, supplies their own tools, and bears the risk of profit and loss. The dependent-contractor category is intermediate — economically dependent on a single principal but contractually structured as a contractor. The distinction matters because the ESA, OHSA, WSIA, and the common-law right to reasonable notice on dismissal apply primarily to employees and dependent contractors, not to true independent contractors. The Supreme Court in 671122 Ontario Ltd v Sagaz Industries [2001] 2 SCR 983 set out the leading test: courts consider the level of control, ownership of equipment, opportunity for profit, risk of loss, and whether the person is integrated into the principal's business.

The Employment Standards Act sets the minimum standards. Selected provisions: minimum wage (set by regulation and updated periodically — $17.20/hour general minimum as of October 2024); maximum hours of work (s. 17 — 8 hours per day, 48 per week, with employee-consent rules above those limits); overtime pay (s. 22 — 1.5× regular wage after 44 hours/week); statutory holidays (9 public holidays per year with public-holiday pay rules); vacation (s. 33 — 2 weeks per year of vacation, 3 weeks after 5 years); various leaves (pregnancy, parental, family-medical, domestic-violence, COVID-19-related, sick).

The most-litigated provisions are termination and severance. Under the ESA, an employee terminated without cause is entitled to notice or pay in lieu based on length of service (s. 57: 1 week per year up to 8 weeks). Larger employers (payroll > $2.5M) also owe severance pay under s. 64 (1 week per year up to 26 weeks). The ESA minimums are floors — the common law of wrongful dismissal provides for "reasonable notice" that is usually significantly longer than the ESA minimum. Reasonable notice under the common law was framed in Bardal v Globe & Mail Ltd. [1960] OWN 253: the factors are age, length of service, character of employment, and availability of similar employment. A senior manager with 20 years of service might be entitled to 18–24 months of reasonable notice; an entry-level worker with 2 years might be entitled to 6 weeks. Termination clauses in employment contracts can limit common-law entitlement but only if they meet stringent drafting requirements (Waksdale v Swegon North America, 2020 ONCA 391 — invalid "for cause" clause invalidates the entire termination clause).

  • Employment Standards Act, 2000 (Ontario) — minimum statutory standards.
  • Employee vs independent contractor — Sagaz control/integration/risk test.
  • Dependent contractor — intermediate category; ESA notice rights apply.
  • Minimum wage — set by regulation; updated periodically.
  • Overtime — 1.5× after 44 hours/week (s. 22).
  • Termination without cause — ESA notice + common-law reasonable notice (Bardal factors).
  • Termination for cause — high standard; the conduct must be incompatible with the employment relationship (McKinley v BC Tel).
  • Severance pay (s. 64) — large employers, >5 years' service.
  • Bardal factors — age, length of service, character of employment, availability of similar work.
  • Duty to mitigate — wrongfully dismissed employee must seek comparable employment.

Worked Example — Wrongful Dismissal

Helena, 47, has been a senior marketing manager at a mid-sized Toronto firm for 9 years. Her contract has no enforceable termination clause (the drafted clause is invalid post-Waksdale). One Monday she is told the position is eliminated; the employer offers 8 weeks' pay in lieu (the ESA minimum).

Calculation under common law:

  1. ESA minimum (s. 57): 9 years' service = 8 weeks' notice or pay in lieu. The employer's offer matches the minimum.
  2. Severance pay (s. 64): if the employer's payroll exceeds $2.5M and the firm employs Helena in Ontario, she is also entitled to 9 weeks of severance pay (1 week per year).
  3. Common-law reasonable notice (Bardal): age 47 (mid-career; more difficult to re-enter than 25-year-old); 9 years' service; senior managerial role; specialised function. A reasonable-notice award in this profile is typically 10–14 months.
  4. Duty to mitigate: Helena must look for comparable work and document the search. Income earned during the notice period reduces her damages.
  5. Likely settlement: 10–12 months' notice (~$110,000–$140,000 in pay-equivalent), reduced by any mitigation earnings, plus the s. 64 severance.

The 8-week offer is the floor, not the ceiling. Most wrongful-dismissal disputes settle privately for amounts substantially above ESA minimums; employees who do not negotiate often forfeit this gap.

  • Treating the ESA minimum as the complete entitlement — common-law reasonable notice is usually much larger.
  • Confusing "without cause" termination (lawful with notice) and "for cause" (high standard — gross misconduct).
  • Forgetting the duty to mitigate — wrongfully dismissed employees must make reasonable efforts to find replacement work.
  • Calling an "independent contractor" arrangement protective for the worker — losing employee status forfeits ESA, EI, CPP-employer-contribution, and notice rights.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.2 — "analyse employment standards under the Ontario Employment Standards Act, 2000 and the law of wrongful dismissal."

👨‍👩‍👧 4.5 Family Law
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Family law in Canada operates on the federal-provincial division: marriage and divorce are federal (Constitution Act, 1867 s. 91(26) — federal Parliament has authority over marriage and divorce); property division, custody and access for unmarried parents, and many child-protection matters are provincial. In Ontario the key statutes are the federal Divorce Act (RSC 1985, c. 3 (2nd Supp.)) and the provincial Family Law Act (RSO 1990, c. F.3) and Children's Law Reform Act (RSO 1990, c. C.12).

Marriage in Canada is the lawful union of two persons to the exclusion of all others. Same-sex marriage was legalised across Canada by the Civil Marriage Act (SC 2005, c. 33) following a sequence of provincial court decisions (Ontario's Halpern v Canada (AG) [2003] 65 OR (3d) 161 led the way). Capacity to marry requires age (16 with parental or court consent; the Federal Civil Marriage Act set a federal minimum of 18), single status (no existing marriage), no prohibited consanguinity, and consent. Common-law relationships in Ontario are recognised in some respects (spousal support after 3 years of cohabitation or where the parties have a child together; s. 29 Family Law Act) but the property regime differs significantly from marriage.

Divorce. The Divorce Act provides one ground for divorce: breakdown of marriage. Section 8(2) defines breakdown as proved by (a) separation of one year, (b) adultery, or (c) cruelty. The "no-fault" 1-year separation ground is now standard; adultery and cruelty are legally available but practically rare because they require additional proof and produce no advantage in modern equal-property regimes.

Property division. Married spouses in Ontario are governed by the net family property regime under Part I of the Family Law Act. Each spouse calculates the value of all assets owned on the valuation date (usually the date of separation), subtracts debts and pre-marriage and excluded property, and the result is the spouse's "net family property." The spouse with the larger NFP pays an "equalisation payment" so both end up equal in increase during the marriage. The matrimonial home is treated specially — it cannot be excluded even if owned before marriage. Common-law partners do not have automatic property rights and must rely on common-law remedies (constructive trust, unjust enrichment — Kerr v Baranow [2011] 1 SCR 269). Spousal support is governed by the Spousal Support Advisory Guidelines and varies with length of cohabitation, age, income disparity, and roles during the relationship. Child support is governed by the Federal Child Support Guidelines, which set the payor's amount as a function of income and the number of children — formula-driven, with limited room for variation. Decision-making responsibility and parenting time (the terms used since 2021 reforms, replacing "custody" and "access") are governed by the best-interests-of-the-child principle, with detailed factors in s. 16 Divorce Act and the Children's Law Reform Act.

  • Federal Divorce Act — marriage and divorce; equal-marriage law nationally.
  • Ontario Family Law Act — property division for married spouses (net family property + equalisation).
  • Ontario Children's Law Reform Act — decision-making and parenting time for unmarried parents.
  • Breakdown of marriage — 1-year separation, adultery, or cruelty (s. 8 Divorce Act).
  • Net family property — assets minus debts and excluded property; valuation at separation.
  • Equalisation payment — spouse with larger NFP pays half the difference.
  • Matrimonial home — special status; cannot be excluded.
  • Common-law spouses — limited automatic property rights; constructive trust available.
  • Spousal support — Spousal Support Advisory Guidelines (SSAG, advisory).
  • Child support — Federal Child Support Guidelines (mandatory tables).
  • Best interests of the child — controlling principle in parenting decisions (s. 16 Divorce Act, 2021 reforms).

Worked Example — Equalisation Calculation

Aleksey and Petra were married for 10 years and are now separating. Date of separation: June 1, 2024.

Aleksey's net family property on June 1:

  • Assets: $480,000 (half-interest in matrimonial home $300,000 + RRSP $150,000 + car $30,000)
  • Debts: $50,000 (line of credit)
  • Pre-marriage property: $20,000 (savings he had before marriage; excluded if traceable)
  • NFP = 480,000 − 50,000 − 20,000 = $410,000

Petra's net family property:

  • Assets: $370,000 (half-interest in matrimonial home $300,000 + RRSP $60,000 + jewellery $10,000)
  • Debts: $20,000 (credit card)
  • Pre-marriage property: $0
  • NFP = 370,000 − 20,000 = $350,000

Equalisation: Aleksey's NFP ($410k) is $60k larger than Petra's ($350k). He pays her half the difference — $30,000. After the payment, each ends up with $380,000 of marital wealth, reflecting the FLA's principle that the increase in wealth during marriage is a shared product.

Note: the matrimonial home is uniquely treated — even if Aleksey bought it before the marriage, its full value at separation enters his NFP (not just the increase). Pre-marriage and excluded property (gifts and inheritances received during marriage, certain insurance proceeds) can be excluded if properly traced.

  • Conflating "common-law" with "married" — common-law partners do not automatically get equalisation.
  • Forgetting that the matrimonial home loses pre-marriage protection — even if owned before, its full value enters NFP.
  • Treating "custody" as the controlling term post-2021 — the Divorce Act now uses "decision-making responsibility" and "parenting time."
  • Believing child support is negotiable — the Federal Guidelines impose a mandatory amount tied to payor income.

Curriculum expectation: Strand E, Expectation E3.1 — "describe the legal framework for marriage, divorce, and the division of property under the Family Law Act and the federal Divorce Act."

🤝 4.6 Alternative Dispute Resolution & Tribunals
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Most civil disputes in Ontario do not end with a trial verdict. They settle. They settle because trials are slow, expensive, public, and final — and the parties usually prefer faster, cheaper, private, and modifiable outcomes. Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) is the umbrella term for the family of processes that resolve disputes outside the formal courts. The three main forms — negotiation, mediation, and arbitration — sit on a continuum from least to most formal.

Negotiation is direct or lawyer-assisted bargaining. The parties retain full control over both process and outcome; no third party is involved. Negotiation underlies all the other forms — it never stops, even during a trial. Most civil cases resolve at this stage, often shortly before trial when costs and risks become concrete.

Mediation introduces a neutral third party — the mediator — who facilitates the discussion but does not decide. The mediator's job is to identify interests beneath positions, generate options, test assumptions, and shepherd the parties toward an agreement they own. Mediated settlements are voluntary; the mediator cannot impose. In Ontario, mediation is mandatory in some civil cases (e.g., simplified procedure under Rule 76 in Toronto, Ottawa, and Windsor for non-family matters; certain family cases under the Family Law Rules). Mediation has emerged as a major instrument in family law, where preserving the parties' future relationship (parenting, support payments) is itself a goal.

Arbitration is the most formal ADR option: an arbitrator hears evidence and arguments and issues a binding decision. Arbitration looks like a private court — the parties' agreement (the arbitration clause) sets the rules, the arbitrator's authority, the location, and the language. Arbitration awards are enforceable through the courts and are reviewable only on narrow grounds (procedural fairness; jurisdiction; rarely the merits). Commercial contracts increasingly contain arbitration clauses; the Supreme Court in Uber v Heller [2020] held some such clauses unconscionable when they make access to justice illusory.

Beyond ADR, Ontario operates an extensive system of administrative tribunals — specialised adjudicative bodies that decide disputes in regulatory contexts. The Landlord and Tenant Board hears residential-tenancy disputes (eviction applications, rent disputes, repair orders) under the Residential Tenancies Act, 2006. The Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario hears discrimination complaints under the Human Rights Code. The Workplace Safety and Insurance Appeals Tribunal hears worker-compensation appeals. The Small Claims Court is a branch of the Superior Court of Justice and handles civil claims up to $35,000 with simplified procedure (no formal pleadings, no examination for discovery). Tribunals offer specialised expertise, lower cost, and faster timelines; their decisions can be judicially reviewed by the Divisional Court on grounds of legal error or procedural unfairness (Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v Vavilov [2019] 4 SCR 653 — the leading standard of review case).

  • ADR — alternatives to litigation: negotiation, mediation, arbitration.
  • Negotiation — direct or lawyer-assisted; full party control.
  • Mediation — neutral facilitator; non-binding (until agreement).
  • Arbitration — neutral decider; binding award; private and confidential.
  • Mandatory mediation — civil cases in Toronto/Ottawa/Windsor under Rule 76.
  • Small Claims Court — branch of SCJ; up to $35,000; no lawyer required.
  • Landlord and Tenant Board — Residential Tenancies Act disputes.
  • Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario — Ontario Human Rights Code complaints.
  • Judicial review — court oversight of tribunal decisions on standard of review (Vavilov).
  • Settlement privilege — communications during settlement discussions are protected from disclosure.

Worked Example — Choosing the Right Forum

Lia rents an apartment from Marco. The fridge breaks and Marco refuses to fix it. Lia stops paying $300 of the rent in protest. Marco serves an N4 (notice for non-payment of rent) and applies to the Landlord and Tenant Board for eviction. Lia counter-claims for a rent reduction for loss of the fridge.

Forum analysis:

  1. Could Lia sue in Small Claims Court? She could try, but residential-tenancy disputes are within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Landlord and Tenant Board (Residential Tenancies Act, s. 168). Small Claims will refuse to hear it.
  2. The LTB hearing. The LTB hears both Marco's eviction application and Lia's counter-application together. The board is empowered to grant an eviction, deny it, conditionally suspend it, order rent abatement, order repair, and award costs.
  3. Mediation at the LTB. The LTB routinely offers same-day mediation by trained mediators. If Lia and Marco can reach an agreement (Marco fixes the fridge within 7 days; Lia pays the back rent over 60 days), it can be incorporated into a board order — enforceable like any other order.
  4. If they cannot agree: a member of the LTB hears evidence under the Statutory Powers Procedure Act and issues a written decision.
  5. Appeal: LTB decisions can be appealed to the Divisional Court only on questions of law (s. 210 RTA), reviewed on Vavilov standards.

Two points: ADR (mediation) is embedded within the tribunal process, not separate from it; and the choice of forum follows the subject matter, not the parties' preference.

  • Treating ADR as a single thing — it is a continuum from informal to highly structured.
  • Believing arbitration is always "better than court" — it is private but the appeal grounds are narrower.
  • Forgetting jurisdictional limits — residential tenancy and human-rights claims belong in their tribunals.
  • Calling settlement weakness — most civil cases settle; settlement is the system working as intended.
