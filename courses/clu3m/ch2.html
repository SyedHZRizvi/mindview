📜 2.1 Origins and Architecture of the Charter
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms came into force on April 17, 1982, as Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982. Before the Charter, Canadians had two main sources of rights protection: the rights inherited from English common law and statutes (habeas corpus, the common-law presumption of innocence, the Canadian Bill of Rights of 1960), and the implicit rights flowing from the Constitution Act, 1867 (e.g., the protection of language and denominational schools). The pre-Charter regime had a structural weakness: Parliament could amend, narrow, or repeal these rights by ordinary statute. The Charter changed that by entrenching rights at the constitutional level, where they could be limited only by constitutional amendment or by the procedures the Charter itself prescribes.

Architecturally the Charter has thirty-four sections, organised into seven categories of rights plus three operative provisions. Sections 2 (fundamental), 3–5 (democratic), 6 (mobility), 7–14 (legal), 15 (equality), and 16–23 (official-language and minority-language education) are the rights themselves. Section 1 is the "reasonable limits" clause. Section 24 is the remedies provision. Section 33 is the notwithstanding clause. Section 35 — outside the Charter but in the same 1982 Act — recognises and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights.

Two questions structure most Charter analysis. First, has the government action (statute, regulation, police conduct, school-board rule) infringed a right or freedom protected by sections 2–23? This is the rights stage. Second, if so, can the government justify that infringement as a "reasonable limit prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society" under section 1? This is the justification stage. The bulk of Charter litigation lives at one of these two stages. A claimant who proves an infringement at stage one shifts the burden to the government at stage two, and a government that fails at stage two will see the statute struck down (or the police conduct excluded) unless section 33 has been invoked.

The Charter applies only to government action. Section 32(1) provides that the Charter binds Parliament, the federal government, the legislatures, and the provincial governments. It does not directly bind private parties. A private employer is not "violating section 15" by discriminating against an employee; the employee's remedy is under the Human Rights Code, not the Charter. The Supreme Court in RWDSU v Dolphin Delivery [1986] 2 SCR 573 confirmed this scope, and subsequent cases have refined it (entities like universities and hospitals are sometimes "government" for Charter purposes when carrying out a specifically governmental function). The distinction matters because it tells you which legal instrument applies to a given dispute.

  • April 17, 1982 — Charter comes into force as Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982.
  • Entrenched — protected at the constitutional level, not by ordinary statute.
  • Section 32 — Charter applies to government action, not private parties.
  • Two-step analysis — rights infringement (stage one) + s. 1 justification (stage two).
  • Section 24 — court-ordered remedies for infringement; s. 24(2) excludes evidence obtained in a manner that brings the administration of justice into disrepute.
  • Section 52(1) (Constitution Act, 1982) — unconstitutional laws are "to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect."
  • Reading down / reading in — judicial techniques to remedy partial unconstitutionality.
  • Suspended declaration of invalidity — court delays the effect of striking a law to give Parliament time to fix it.

Worked Example — Charter Applies vs Does Not Apply

Compare two scenarios:

  1. Scenario A: A municipal by-law in a small Ontario town bans wearing religious head coverings at council meetings. Charter applies — a municipality is a creature of provincial statute and is "government" for Charter purposes. The by-law infringes section 2(a) (freedom of religion) and section 15 (equality). Government must justify under section 1; likely fails.
  2. Scenario B: A private gym in the same town bans members from wearing religious head coverings during workouts, citing safety. Charter does not apply — a private gym is not "government." The member's remedy is under the Ontario Human Rights Code, which prohibits discrimination in goods, services, and facilities on the ground of creed. The legal test (prima facie discrimination + bona fide requirement defence) is similar in spirit but the instrument is the Code, not the Charter.

Same conduct, different defendant, different law. The Charter's reach stops at the boundary of state action; the Human Rights Code's reach extends into private life.

  • Calling the Charter "rights for everyone everywhere" — it is rights against government, not against private actors.
  • Believing the Charter is older than 1982 — pre-1982 Canada had the Bill of Rights (1960), a federal statute with much weaker effect.
  • Saying "the Charter is supreme" — the Constitution is supreme (s. 52); the Charter is one part of the Constitution.
  • Confusing s. 24 (remedies — exclude evidence, damages) with s. 52 (invalidate the law itself).

🕊️ 2.2 Fundamental Freedoms (Section 2)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Section 2 of the Charter guarantees four "fundamental freedoms" — freedom of conscience and religion (s. 2(a)); freedom of thought, belief, opinion, and expression, including freedom of the press (s. 2(b)); freedom of peaceful assembly (s. 2(c)); and freedom of association (s. 2(d)). These freedoms are individual, but several (assembly, association, sometimes religion) are also collective in their exercise. They have been the most-litigated provisions of the Charter outside section 7.

Section 2(a) — freedom of religion. The leading case is R v Big M Drug Mart [1985] 1 SCR 295, in which the Supreme Court struck down the federal Lord's Day Act for compelling Sunday closing on a Christian Sabbath rationale. Chief Justice Dickson defined freedom of religion as "the absence of coercion or constraint" in respect of religious belief and practice. The test for a section 2(a) infringement has two parts: the claimant must show (a) a sincere religious belief, and (b) state action that interferes with that belief in a non-trivial or non-insubstantial way. The court does not police the truth of the belief or its conformity with religious doctrine.

Section 2(b) — freedom of expression. The leading case is Irwin Toy Ltd. v Quebec (AG) [1989] 1 SCR 927: any activity that conveys or attempts to convey meaning falls within section 2(b), with the exception of violent expression. The breadth is intentional — even commercial advertising, hate speech, and pornography are presumptively protected at stage one; the limits come at the section 1 justification stage. R v Keegstra [1990] 3 SCR 697 upheld the Criminal Code prohibition on wilful promotion of hatred (s. 319(2)) under section 1, holding the harm of hate propaganda justified the limit. The Charter does not protect a "right to be uncivil"; it protects expression while permitting carefully-tailored limits.

Section 2(c) — peaceful assembly and section 2(d) — freedom of association. Peaceful assembly is mostly subsumed into expression; freedom of association has its own line of cases protecting union activity. Mounted Police Association of Ontario v Canada (AG) [2015] 1 SCR 3 confirmed that section 2(d) protects the right of employees to a meaningful collective-bargaining process — a major development in Canadian labour law. The basic move in all four Section 2 freedoms is the same: define the freedom broadly, then let the section 1 reasonable-limits test do the work of accommodating competing public interests.

  • s. 2(a) — freedom of conscience and religion.
  • s. 2(b) — freedom of thought, belief, opinion, expression, including the press.
  • s. 2(c) — freedom of peaceful assembly.
  • s. 2(d) — freedom of association (including meaningful collective bargaining).
  • Sincere belief — the standard for religious-freedom claims; courts do not test doctrinal correctness.
  • Hate-speech limit — s. 319(2) Criminal Code; upheld in Keegstra as a reasonable s. 1 limit.
  • Symbolic speech — non-verbal expression (flag-burning, picketing) is protected expression.
  • Commercial expression — advertising is protected (Ford v Quebec [1988] sign-language case).

Worked Example — Step-by-Step Section 2(b) Analysis

A 17-year-old student wears a T-shirt to school reading "Climate Strike — Walk Out Now." The school board has a dress code that bans "political messaging" on clothing. The student is told to remove the shirt or go home; she sues alleging breach of section 2(b).

Step 1 — Does the activity convey meaning? Yes, plainly: a slogan is expression by definition. The shirt is also not violent. So it falls within s. 2(b)'s scope.

Step 2 — Does state action infringe? Yes — the school board is a "government" actor under s. 32 and the dress code suppresses the expression.

Step 3 — Section 1 justification. The board would have to prove (i) the dress code is "prescribed by law" (yes, it is a board policy); (ii) the objective is pressing and substantial (e.g., a neutral learning environment); (iii) the means are rationally connected (a blanket ban on political messaging arguably is); (iv) minimally impairing (a blanket ban on all political content is probably broader than necessary — schools could target disruptive or hateful content); (v) overall benefit outweighs the cost to expression. The likely outcome: the ban fails minimal impairment.

Step 4 — Remedy. The court can grant a personal remedy under s. 24 (allow the student to wear the shirt) or strike down the policy under s. 52.

  • Calling all expression unlimited — even s. 2(b) is subject to s. 1 limits.
  • Believing s. 2(b) protects violent expression — explicitly excluded in Irwin Toy.
  • Treating school rules as private — public school boards are subject to the Charter.
  • Confusing offensive speech with hate speech — only the latter meets s. 319's threshold.

⚖️ 2.3 Legal Rights (Sections 7–14)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Sections 7 through 14 of the Charter protect the legal rights of people interacting with the criminal-justice and detention systems. They include section 7's open-ended right to "life, liberty, and security of the person and the right not to be deprived thereof except in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice"; section 8's protection against "unreasonable search or seizure"; section 9's protection against arbitrary detention; section 10's right on arrest to be informed of the reasons, to retain and instruct counsel without delay (10(b)), and to challenge the validity of the detention by way of habeas corpus (10(c)); section 11's procedural protections at trial (including the presumption of innocence and trial within a reasonable time); section 12's protection from cruel and unusual treatment or punishment; and section 13's protection against self-incrimination in subsequent proceedings.

Section 7 is perhaps the most consequential single sentence in the Charter. It works in tandem with the "principles of fundamental justice" — a category that courts have populated with substantive principles like overbreadth, arbitrariness, gross disproportionality, and vagueness. Section 7 has been the basis for striking down the abortion provisions of the Criminal Code (R v Morgentaler [1988] 1 SCR 30), the prohibition on physician-assisted dying (Carter v Canada (AG) [2015] 1 SCR 331), and the laws criminalising aspects of sex work (Canada (AG) v Bedford [2013] 3 SCR 1101). Section 7 is not a free-roaming licence — claimants must show both a deprivation of life, liberty, or security of the person, and a violation of fundamental justice.

Section 8 — unreasonable search or seizure — is governed by R v Collins [1987] 1 SCR 265 and Hunter v Southam [1984] 2 SCR 145. A search is reasonable if (a) it is authorised by law, (b) the authorising law is reasonable, and (c) the search is conducted reasonably. Where police search without a warrant in circumstances where a warrant could reasonably have been obtained, the search is presumptively unreasonable. Section 10(b) entrenches the right to counsel: when arrested or detained, you must be told you can phone a lawyer and given a reasonable opportunity to do so before further questioning (R v Manninen [1987]; R v Bartle [1994]). Section 11(b) requires trial within a reasonable time; R v Jordan [2016] 1 SCR 631 set presumptive ceilings — 18 months for provincial-court trials, 30 months for Superior Court trials.

Section 11(d) entrenches the presumption of innocence: the Crown must prove every element of the offence beyond a reasonable doubt. The presumption is not just a procedural nicety — it is the central structural commitment of Canadian criminal law. Reverse-onus clauses (provisions that put a burden on the accused) are presumptively invalid and need to be justified under section 1. Together, sections 7–14 build a system in which the awesome powers of the state — to search, arrest, detain, prosecute, and imprison — must be exercised within carefully defined limits. When those limits are breached, section 24(2) directs courts to exclude evidence whose admission would bring the administration of justice into disrepute, applying the framework in R v Grant [2009] 2 SCR 353.

  • s. 7 — life, liberty, security of the person; principles of fundamental justice.
  • s. 8 — unreasonable search or seizure.
  • s. 9 — arbitrary detention or imprisonment.
  • s. 10(a)(b)(c) — reasons for arrest; right to counsel; habeas corpus.
  • s. 11(b) — trial within reasonable time (Jordan ceilings: 18/30 months).
  • s. 11(d) — presumption of innocence.
  • s. 12 — cruel and unusual punishment.
  • s. 24(2) — exclusion of evidence obtained by Charter breach; Grant framework.

Worked Example — Section 10(b) Right to Counsel

Officer Singh pulls over Marcus for a routine traffic check and detects alcohol on his breath. She arrests Marcus for impaired driving, places him in the cruiser, and immediately begins asking questions about how much he had to drink. She has not yet told him about his right to a lawyer. Marcus answers and inculpates himself.

At trial, defence counsel argues a section 10(b) breach. Analysis:

  1. R v Manninen [1987] establishes that police must (i) inform the detainee of the right to counsel without delay, (ii) provide a reasonable opportunity to contact counsel, and (iii) cease questioning until the detainee has had that opportunity (subject to limited exceptions).
  2. Here, Officer Singh has skipped step (i) and step (iii) — she questioned Marcus without informing him of his right to a lawyer first.
  3. The breach having been established, the question moves to s. 24(2): should the resulting statement be excluded? Under R v Grant [2009], the court weighs the seriousness of the Charter-infringing conduct, the impact on the accused, and society's interest in adjudication on the merits.
  4. A failure to inform of the right to counsel is a serious breach (squarely on the officer); the impact on Marcus is significant (self-incriminating statements); the offence is serious but not violent. The likely outcome is exclusion of the statements.

The result is not that Marcus walks free — the Crown still has the breath samples and Officer Singh's observations. But the inculpatory statements come out. This is what section 24(2) is for: a structural cost on Charter-breaching investigation.

  • Confusing the right to counsel (s. 10(b)) with the right to remain silent (s. 7) — both apply but are distinct.
  • Believing a Charter breach automatically excludes evidence — exclusion is governed by the Grant balancing test.
  • Treating "reasonable time" under s. 11(b) as elastic — Jordan set presumptive numeric ceilings.
  • Forgetting that s. 7 covers more than the criminal process — it has been used in immigration, abortion, end-of-life, and child-welfare contexts.

⚖️ 2.4 Equality Rights (Section 15)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Section 15(1) of the Charter reads: "Every individual is equal before and under the law and has the right to the equal protection and equal benefit of the law without discrimination and, in particular, without discrimination based on race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age or mental or physical disability." Section 15(2) preserves affirmative-action programs — laws that benefit historically disadvantaged groups do not breach section 15(1) merely by being asymmetric.

Section 15(1) does not prohibit all distinctions — laws inevitably distinguish (between adults and minors, drivers and non-drivers, taxpayers in different brackets). It prohibits discrimination, defined narrowly. The current test, articulated in R v Kapp [2008] 2 SCR 483 and refined in Withler v Canada (AG) [2011] 1 SCR 396, has two parts. First, does the law create a distinction based on an enumerated ground (the listed grounds in section 15(1)) or an analogous ground (a personal characteristic, immutable or constructively immutable, central to dignity)? Second, does the distinction create a disadvantage by perpetuating prejudice or stereotyping?

Sexual orientation is the leading example of an analogous ground. The Supreme Court read it in to the open-ended list in Egan v Canada [1995] 2 SCR 513 and Vriend v Alberta [1998] 1 SCR 493. In Vriend, Alberta's human-rights statute did not list sexual orientation as a protected ground; the Court remedied this not by striking down the statute but by "reading in" sexual orientation. This is a notable judicial technique: where Parliament or a legislature has under-protected a group, courts can sometimes add to the statute rather than remove it. Other analogous grounds include marital status, citizenship, and Aboriginal residence off-reserve.

The complement to section 15 is provincial and federal human-rights legislation: the Ontario Human Rights Code (OHRC) and the Canadian Human Rights Act. These statutes prohibit discrimination in five social areas (services, accommodation, employment, contracts, vocational associations) on enumerated grounds (race, ancestry, place of origin, colour, ethnic origin, citizenship, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, age, marital status, family status, disability, and others). Unlike the Charter, the OHRC binds private parties — landlords, employers, businesses. The OHRC also imposes a duty to accommodate to the point of undue hardship: an employer who has a workplace policy that disadvantages a protected group must accommodate that group (modified equipment, schedule changes, alternative duties) unless doing so would cause undue hardship in terms of cost, health, or safety (British Columbia (Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v BCGSEU [1999] 3 SCR 3 — the "Meiorin" test).

  • s. 15(1) — equality "before and under the law" + "equal protection and equal benefit of the law without discrimination."
  • Enumerated grounds — race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, mental or physical disability.
  • Analogous grounds — sexual orientation, marital status, citizenship, Aboriginal off-reserve residence.
  • s. 15(2) — affirmative action / ameliorative programs.
  • Substantive equality — same treatment can produce different outcomes; equality requires recognising disadvantage, not blindness to difference.
  • Reading in — judicial remedy that adds a protection to a statute.
  • Ontario Human Rights Code — provincial statute binding private actors; grounds wider than the Charter's.
  • Duty to accommodate / Meiorin test — accommodation to the point of undue hardship.
  • Bona fide occupational requirement — defence in employment-discrimination cases.

Worked Example — Applying the Section 15 Test (Vriend)

Delwin Vriend was fired from his job at a Christian college in Alberta for being gay. He tried to file a complaint under Alberta's Individual's Rights Protection Act (IRPA). Problem: the IRPA did not list sexual orientation as a protected ground.

At the Supreme Court:

  1. Stage one — does s. 15 apply? The Court held that the omission of sexual orientation from the IRPA, by itself, created a distinction. Lesbians and gay men were left without legal protection that other groups had.
  2. Stage two — analogous ground? Sexual orientation had already been recognised in Egan v Canada as analogous to the listed grounds.
  3. Stage three — discrimination? Yes — the under-inclusion perpetuated the historic disadvantage of LGBTQ+ Albertans.
  4. Stage four — section 1 justification? Alberta argued that the omission reflected a legislative choice, but the Court held the omission could not be demonstrably justified.
  5. Remedy. Rather than strike down the entire IRPA (which would have left everyone unprotected), the Court read in "sexual orientation" as a protected ground. Vriend won.

Vriend is a teaching case for three reasons: it shows the under-inclusion can be unconstitutional, it shows analogous grounds are real and operative, and it shows the creativity of Charter remedies.

  • Treating "equality" as identical treatment — Canadian law has moved to substantive equality, recognising that same treatment can entrench disadvantage.
  • Believing s. 15 covers only the listed grounds — the Court can recognise analogous grounds (sexual orientation, marital status, citizenship).
  • Conflating Charter equality (s. 15, government action) with Human Rights Code protection (private actors).
  • Forgetting the duty to accommodate is a positive obligation on employers and service providers, not just a prohibition on overt discrimination.

📏 2.5 Reasonable Limits: Section 1 and the Oakes Test
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Section 1 of the Charter says: "The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees the rights and freedoms set out in it subject only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society." This single sentence is the structural genius of the Charter. It admits openly that rights can be limited; it sets a high but specific test for when limits are constitutionally tolerable; and it shifts the analytical burden — once a claimant proves an infringement of a right, the government must justify the limit.

The leading authority on section 1 is R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103. David Oakes was charged with possession of narcotics for the purpose of trafficking. Section 8 of the Narcotic Control Act provided that once the Crown proved possession, the accused had to prove he was not in possession for the purpose of trafficking. The reverse-onus violated section 11(d) (presumption of innocence). The Crown argued the reverse-onus was a reasonable limit under section 1. Chief Justice Dickson laid out a structured test that has governed every section 1 case since.

The Oakes test has two preliminary requirements and a four-part proportionality analysis:

  1. Prescribed by law — the limit must be set out in legislation, regulation, or a clear common-law rule. A police officer's ad-hoc decision is not "prescribed by law."
  2. Pressing and substantial objective — the government's purpose in limiting the right must be sufficiently important. Trivial purposes fail at the threshold.
  3. Rational connection — the means chosen must be rationally connected to the objective. A measure that does not actually further the goal fails.
  4. Minimal impairment — the means must impair the right as little as reasonably possible. Overbroad measures fail.
  5. Proportionality of effects — the salutary effects of the limit must outweigh the deleterious effects on the right. The means cannot be disproportionate to the end.

The Crown bears the burden at every step. Oakes itself failed at rational connection — there was no rational link between possessing a small amount of narcotics and the inference of trafficking — so the reverse-onus was struck down. Most modern section 1 litigation now turns on minimal impairment: the Crown can usually clear the first two hurdles and rational connection, but minimal impairment forces the government to consider whether a less rights-restrictive alternative would achieve the same objective.

The Oakes framework is exported across the common-law world; the South African Constitutional Court and the UK Supreme Court use structurally similar tests. Inside Canada, the test produces a culture of constitutional drafting in which legislators have to ask, while drafting a new statute, whether their proposed limit could withstand Oakes. That ex-ante effect — laws drafted with Charter-compliance in mind — may matter more than any individual judicial decision.

  • s. 1 — "reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society."
  • Two-step Charter analysis — infringement at stage one; s. 1 justification at stage two.
  • Burden — claimant proves infringement; government justifies the limit.
  • Prescribed by law — legislation, regulation, or clear common-law rule.
  • Pressing and substantial — objective sufficiently important.
  • Rational connection — means actually further the goal.
  • Minimal impairment — least rights-restrictive alternative.
  • Proportionality of effects — benefits outweigh costs to the right.

Worked Example — Applying Oakes to a Hate-Speech Statute

Section 319(2) of the Criminal Code prohibits "wilful promotion of hatred" against an "identifiable group." James Keegstra, a high-school teacher in Alberta, was convicted for teaching Holocaust denial. He challenged s. 319(2) as an infringement of his s. 2(b) freedom of expression.

The Supreme Court in R v Keegstra [1990] 3 SCR 697 conceded the infringement (hate speech is "expression" under Irwin Toy) and then applied Oakes:

  1. Prescribed by law: Yes — s. 319(2) is a clearly drafted Criminal Code provision.
  2. Pressing and substantial: Yes — protection of target groups from psychological and social harm, and protection of multicultural Canadian society, are both sufficient.
  3. Rational connection: Yes — criminalising wilful promotion of hatred plausibly reduces both the speech and its harm.
  4. Minimal impairment: Yes (in this case) — the section has multiple safeguards: it requires wilful promotion (high mens rea), it has defences in s. 319(3) (truth, public-interest opinion, religious teaching), and it requires the Attorney General's consent to prosecute.
  5. Proportionality of effects: Yes — the benefit of suppressing hateful speech that targets vulnerable groups outweighs the cost to the speaker, particularly given hate speech contributes only marginally to s. 2(b)'s core values of truth-seeking, democratic participation, and self-fulfilment.

Section 319(2) survives Oakes. The case illustrates how s. 1 lets the Charter sit comfortably with a society that draws lines against the most harmful kinds of speech.

  • Skipping the prescribed-by-law step — ad-hoc executive action does not get section 1 protection.
  • Treating minimal impairment as "the least possible impairment" — the test is the least rights-restrictive reasonable alternative, not the absolute minimum.
  • Forgetting the burden is on the Crown, not the claimant, once an infringement is shown.
  • Calling section 1 a "loophole" — it is a structural acknowledgment that rights are not absolute, common to most modern bills of rights.

🛑 2.6 The Notwithstanding Clause (Section 33)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Section 33 of the Charter — commonly called the "notwithstanding clause" or "override clause" — lets Parliament or a provincial legislature pass legislation that operates "notwithstanding" sections 2 and 7–15 of the Charter. The override must be expressly declared in the statute, lasts at most five years, and can be re-enacted. Section 33 is the most contested feature of the Charter, the one that critics of judicial review point to as a sign of parliamentary supremacy and that defenders of rights point to as the loophole that lets governments suspend rights.

Section 33 was the price of patriation. When Pierre Trudeau, the federal government, and nine of the ten provinces met in November 1981 to negotiate the Charter, several premiers (Saskatchewan's Allan Blakeney, Manitoba's Sterling Lyon, and others) objected to handing rights determination to courts. The compromise reached at the "kitchen accord" was that legislatures would retain the ability to override certain Charter sections, but only by explicit declaration, only for five years at a time, and only for specified provisions. Sections 3–6 (democratic, mobility), 16–23 (language), and 28 (gender-equality guarantee) are not subject to section 33 — the override only reaches sections 2 and 7–15.

Section 33 was rarely invoked in its first three decades. Quebec famously used a blanket s. 33 declaration in every statute passed from 1982 to 1985 as a protest against not having signed the patriation; the override was only used substantively in Ford v Quebec (AG) [1988] 2 SCR 712 (the sign-language case), where the Supreme Court struck down a Quebec law requiring French-only commercial signs as a s. 2(b) breach, and Quebec responded by re-enacting the law with a s. 33 override. Saskatchewan invoked it in 1986 to override a labour-relations decision; Alberta has used it in family law; recent years have seen substantially more invocations. Ontario invoked it pre-emptively in 2021 (Bill 254, election finance) and again in 2022 (Bill 28, education-sector collective bargaining), and Quebec invoked it pre-emptively in 2019 (Bill 21, secularism / religious symbols) and 2022 (Bill 96, French language).

The legal scholarly debate about s. 33 has two camps. The "dialogue" view sees section 33 as a healthy mechanism by which legislatures can respond when courts misread public opinion or constitutional balance — Parliament and provinces retain final say on hard cases. The "abdication" view sees section 33 as a way to enact unconstitutional policy without judicial check, undermining the Charter's protective function. Both views are defensible; the constitutional architecture supports the dialogue reading on its face (section 33 is explicit, time-limited, and renewable only by re-enactment), but the empirical use of section 33 in recent years has shifted toward pre-emptive deployment — invoking it before any court has ruled — which sits less comfortably with a "dialogue" justification.

  • Section 33(1) — explicit declaration; statute operates notwithstanding ss. 2 or 7–15.
  • Section 33(3) — declaration expires after five years, renewable.
  • Outside s. 33 — democratic rights (ss. 3–6), language rights (ss. 16–23), gender-equality guarantee (s. 28).
  • Pre-emptive use — invoking s. 33 before any court has struck down the law.
  • Dialogue theory — courts and legislatures interact; s. 33 lets legislatures respond.
  • Abdication critique — s. 33 lets governments enact rights-violating policy without judicial check.
  • Ford v Quebec (AG) [1988] — leading section 33 case; Quebec's sign-language re-enactment.
  • Five-year sunset — forces re-enactment if government wants to maintain the override.

Worked Example — Section 33 in Action: Quebec Bill 21

Quebec's 2019 An Act respecting the laicity of the State (Bill 21) prohibits a range of public-sector workers in positions of authority (teachers, judges, police, prosecutors) from wearing religious symbols at work. Predictably the law infringes section 2(a) (freedom of religion) and likely section 15 (equality — disproportionate impact on Sikh, Jewish, and Muslim public servants).

Anticipating the Charter challenge, the Quebec legislature invoked section 33 in the statute itself. The Quebec Court of Appeal in Hak c Procureur général du Québec [2024 QCCA 154] held that the s. 33 invocation succeeded in shielding Bill 21 from challenge under sections 2 and 15, but did not shield it from challenges under sections that are not covered by s. 33 (notably s. 28, the gender-equality guarantee). The case is on appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada.

Analysis points:

  1. The s. 33 declaration was express and meets the formal requirements.
  2. It covers ss. 2 and 15 (the rights infringed).
  3. It does not cover s. 28 (which is not in the list of overridable sections).
  4. Even if upheld, the override expires after five years — Quebec must re-enact in 2024 / 2029 / 2034 to maintain it.
  5. The political and reputational cost of invoking s. 33 is a real, if unmeasured, constraint on its use.

Bill 21 illustrates the live tension in Canadian constitutionalism: a democratically enacted law that explicitly suspends fundamental rights is, on the face of the Charter, constitutional. Whether it should be is a different question.

  • Saying s. 33 makes a law "constitutional" — it makes the law operative; whether it complies with the Charter's substance is a different question.
  • Believing s. 33 can override any Charter section — it cannot override ss. 3–6, 16–23, or 28.
  • Treating the five-year sunset as a small constraint — it forces re-enactment, which invites political accountability.
  • Confusing s. 33 with s. 1 — s. 1 is judicial weighing of justified limits; s. 33 is legislative override that bypasses s. 1.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "explain the role of section 33 of the Charter (the notwithstanding clause) and analyse situations in which it has been invoked."
