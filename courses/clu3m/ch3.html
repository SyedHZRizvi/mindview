🧠 3.1 Elements of a Crime: Actus Reus & Mens Rea
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canadian criminal law begins from a deceptively simple principle: nobody can be convicted of a true crime without proof beyond a reasonable doubt of two distinct elements — the guilty act (actus reus) and the guilty mind (mens rea). This combination is older than the Criminal Code; it descends from the medieval English maxim "actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea" — "the act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty." The Supreme Court of Canada has constitutionalised the requirement of proof of mens rea for true crimes under section 7 of the Charter (Re BC Motor Vehicle Act [1985] 2 SCR 486).

The actus reus is the physical, external component of the offence. It typically has three sub-components: the conduct, the circumstances, and the consequence. The Crown must prove each one beyond a reasonable doubt. For murder under section 229(a) of the Criminal Code, the actus reus is causing the death of a human being. For theft under section 322, the actus reus is taking another's property without consent. The act must also be voluntary — the accused must have control over the physical movement. An epileptic seizure that causes a person to swing their arm into another is not a voluntary act and therefore not an actus reus (R v Stone [1999] 2 SCR 290; R v Parks [1992] 2 SCR 871, sleepwalking).

The mens rea is the mental state with which the act was performed. Mens rea varies across offences — Parliament can require intention (the accused meant the outcome), knowledge (the accused was aware of a fact), recklessness (the accused was aware of a risk and proceeded anyway), wilful blindness (the accused deliberately avoided knowing), or criminal negligence (a marked departure from the standard of a reasonable person). Each Criminal Code offence prescribes its own mens rea, sometimes expressly ("with intent to") and sometimes implicitly. Some offences are strict liability or absolute liability — regulatory offences where mens rea is not required — but these are not true crimes and cannot result in imprisonment without proof of fault (Re BC Motor Vehicle).

Mens rea has both subjective and objective forms. Subjective mens rea asks what the accused actually knew, intended, or foresaw. Objective mens rea asks what a reasonable person in the accused's circumstances would have known or foreseen. Most true crimes require subjective mens rea (murder, robbery, theft); some require objective fault (criminal negligence causing death; dangerous driving). The Charter has shaped this allocation: R v Vaillancourt [1987] 2 SCR 636 held that the most serious crimes (murder) require subjective mens rea as a matter of fundamental justice, because the stigma and punishment are so severe.

  • Actus reus — the guilty act; physical conduct, circumstances, consequences.
  • Mens rea — the guilty mind; intention, knowledge, recklessness, wilful blindness, criminal negligence.
  • Voluntariness — the act must be willed; involuntary movements are not actus reus.
  • Subjective mens rea — what the accused actually knew or intended.
  • Objective mens rea — what a reasonable person would have known (marked departure).
  • Intention — purpose or desire to bring about a consequence.
  • Recklessness — awareness of a risk + proceeding anyway.
  • Wilful blindness — deliberate avoidance of knowledge.
  • Strict liability — regulatory offence; defence of due diligence.
  • Absolute liability — no fault required; unconstitutional if combined with imprisonment.

Worked Example — Section 229(a) Murder Charge

Section 229(a) provides: "Culpable homicide is murder where the person who causes the death of a human being (i) means to cause his death, or (ii) means to cause him bodily harm that he knows is likely to cause his death, and is reckless whether death ensues or not."

Hassan strikes Lin with a baseball bat in a fight; Lin dies of head trauma three days later. The Crown charges Hassan with murder. What must the Crown prove?

  1. Actus reus:
    • Conduct: Hassan struck Lin.
    • Circumstances: Lin was a human being.
    • Consequence: Lin died.
    • Causation: Hassan's act caused Lin's death (factual cause + legal cause beyond a de minimis contribution).
    • Voluntariness: Hassan controlled the movement.
  2. Mens rea (s. 229(a)(i)): Did Hassan mean to cause Lin's death? (Subjective intent to kill.) If not, move to:
  3. Mens rea (s. 229(a)(ii)): Did Hassan mean to cause bodily harm that he knew was likely to cause Lin's death, and was he reckless whether death ensued? (Lower threshold but still subjective.)

If the Crown cannot prove subjective intent for either branch — perhaps Hassan only intended to injure — the charge will likely be reduced to manslaughter under s. 234 (culpable homicide that is not murder). The mental-element finding can swing the conviction across the line between manslaughter (maximum penalty: life imprisonment) and second-degree murder (mandatory life imprisonment, parole ineligibility 10 years).

  • Calling motive the same as mens rea — motive (the reason for the act) is generally not required; mens rea is the mental state at the moment of the act.
  • Confusing intention with knowledge — intention is about purpose; knowledge is about awareness of facts.
  • Believing all criminal liability requires subjective fault — some offences are properly objective (dangerous driving, criminal negligence).
  • Treating "I didn't mean to" as a complete defence — recklessness still attaches if the risk was foreseen and ignored.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "explain the two essential elements of a crime — actus reus (the guilty act) and mens rea (the guilty mind) — and their components."

📂 3.2 Categories of Offences
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Criminal Code of Canada divides offences into three main procedural categories: summary-conviction offences, indictable offences, and hybrid offences (also called "dual-procedure" or "Crown-elect"). The category determines almost everything about how the case will be prosecuted — the maximum sentence, the right to trial by jury, the court level, the limitation period for laying the information, and the bail process.

Summary-conviction offences are the least serious. They are heard in the Ontario Court of Justice before a judge alone (no jury option). The general maximum penalty is set out in section 787(1) of the Criminal Code: $5,000 fine, two years less a day in prison, or both — unless a specific section provides otherwise. There is a 12-month limitation period for laying the information (s. 786(2)) running from when the offence was committed. Examples include causing a disturbance (s. 175), trespassing at night (s. 177), and many minor offences.

Indictable offences are the most serious. They have no general limitation period — an indictable charge can be laid years after the conduct (the famous limit-less property of indictable offences was used in historical sexual-assault prosecutions). Indictable offences trigger the accused's section 11(f) Charter right to a jury trial where the maximum penalty is five years or more. Penalties range from a few years to life imprisonment (murder, first-degree being mandatory life with parole ineligibility 25 years). Indictable offences are split by section 469 into Superior-Court-only offences (most serious: murder, treason), section 553 OCJ-only offences (less serious indictable: theft under $5,000, fraud under $5,000), and the broad middle category where the accused elects the mode of trial under s. 536(2) (judge in OCJ, judge alone in SCJ, or judge and jury in SCJ).

Hybrid offences are the most common. The Criminal Code defines an offence as "punishable by way of indictment or summary conviction" and lets the Crown choose how to proceed. Until the Crown elects, the offence is treated as indictable for procedural purposes (e.g., fingerprinting under the Identification of Criminals Act). After election, the procedural consequences follow accordingly. The vast majority of charges under the Criminal Code — assault (s. 266), theft under $5,000 (s. 334(b)), uttering threats (s. 264.1), impaired driving (s. 320.14) — are hybrid. The Crown's choice is influenced by the seriousness of the conduct, the accused's record, the available sentence, and resource considerations.

  • Summary-conviction — least serious; OCJ judge alone; 12-month limitation; max $5,000 / 2 years less a day (default).
  • Indictable — most serious; no limitation period; possible jury trial if 5+ years available.
  • Hybrid (dual-procedure) — Crown elects summary or indictable; ~85% of Criminal Code charges.
  • Section 469 — exclusive SCJ jurisdiction (murder, treason).
  • Section 553 — exclusive OCJ jurisdiction (less-serious indictable).
  • Section 536(2) — accused's election among modes of trial.
  • Section 11(f) Charter — right to jury trial where maximum sentence is 5 years or more.
  • Preliminary inquiry — pre-trial hearing on indictable matters (now restricted to serious offences post-2019 amendments).

Worked Example — Crown Election in an Assault Charge

Maya allegedly punches her co-worker during an argument. The co-worker has a bloody nose but no lasting injury. The Crown can charge Maya with assault under section 266 of the Criminal Code, which is hybrid:

  • If proceeding summarily: max 2 years less a day in OCJ; no jury; faster trial; lighter sentencing range; 12-month limitation.
  • If proceeding by indictment: max 5 years; if Maya elects, possible jury trial in SCJ; longer pre-trial process; broader sentencing range available; no limitation period.

The Crown's decision depends on:

  1. Seriousness: minor injury, single punch — leans summary.
  2. Accused's record: if Maya has prior assaults, the Crown may proceed by indictment to access a stiffer sentence.
  3. Aggravating factors: any vulnerability of the victim, breach of trust, or workplace harassment context can push toward indictment.
  4. Time since the offence: if more than 12 months has passed and the Crown wants to charge, only indictment is available.
  5. Resources and policy: the Crown attorney's office has manuals on how to exercise discretion consistently.

In most everyday assaults like Maya's, the Crown will proceed summarily. The accused's exposure is materially different depending on this single procedural decision.

  • Treating "hybrid" as a separate category once charged — once the Crown elects, the case proceeds as either summary or indictable.
  • Forgetting that pre-election, hybrid offences are deemed indictable for some procedural purposes (e.g., fingerprinting).
  • Believing all indictable offences allow a jury — only those with 5+ years maximum sentence trigger s. 11(f) right.
  • Confusing the maximum penalty with the typical sentence — most assault sentences are well below the 5-year ceiling.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "distinguish between summary-conviction offences, indictable offences, and hybrid offences."

👮 3.3 Criminal Trial Process: Arrest to Verdict
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A criminal case moves through a procedural pipeline that begins long before the trial and continues after the verdict. The steps in order are: investigation, arrest or appearance notice, charge (laying the information), first appearance, judicial interim release (bail), disclosure, election and plea, preliminary inquiry (some cases), trial, verdict, sentencing, and appeal. Each step is governed by specific Criminal Code provisions and constitutional rules.

Arrest and detention. Police can arrest with a warrant or, in defined circumstances, without one. Section 495 of the Criminal Code permits warrantless arrest where the officer (a) finds a person committing an offence, (b) believes on reasonable grounds the person has committed an indictable offence, or (c) reasonably believes a warrant has been issued. Charter sections 9 (no arbitrary detention) and 10 (right to be informed of reasons, to retain counsel, and to challenge detention) shape how arrest must be conducted. Section 10(b) requires the police to inform the detainee of the right to counsel "without delay" — practically, immediately on arrest — and to hold off questioning until the detainee has had a reasonable opportunity to consult counsel.

Bail. Section 11(e) of the Charter guarantees the right not to be denied "reasonable bail without just cause." Section 515 of the Criminal Code governs the bail process. The default is release on undertaking (a written promise to attend court); the Crown can seek detention or release on conditions if there are concerns about (a) attendance — the "primary ground"; (b) protection of the public, including the likelihood of reoffending — the "secondary ground"; or (c) maintenance of confidence in the administration of justice — the "tertiary ground." Section 515(10) was upheld and refined in R v Antic [2017] 1 SCR 509, which reaffirmed the "ladder principle" — release on the least restrictive form of conditions is the default, and detention requires real justification.

Disclosure. The Crown must disclose all relevant information to the defence — both inculpatory and exculpatory — before the accused must plead. The leading case is R v Stinchcombe [1991] 3 SCR 326, which transformed Canadian criminal procedure by treating disclosure as a constitutional obligation derived from section 7. Failure to disclose can produce a stay of proceedings or other remedies.

Trial and verdict. At trial, the Crown presents its case first; defence can call evidence but is not obligated to. The Crown must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt; the defence must merely raise a reasonable doubt. The trier of fact (judge alone or jury) renders a verdict of guilty or not guilty. In a jury trial, the jury's verdict must be unanimous and the jury cannot be told how to weigh evidence — only what the legal elements are. The trial-within-a-reasonable-time guarantee in section 11(b) is now governed by R v Jordan [2016] 1 SCR 631, which set presumptive ceilings of 18 months from charge to end of trial in provincial court and 30 months in superior court (subject to defence delay and exceptional circumstances). Cases exceeding the ceilings without justification will be stayed.

  • Investigation — police gather evidence; Charter limits apply to searches (s. 8) and detentions (s. 9).
  • Arrest — s. 495; with or without warrant; triggers ss. 9, 10 Charter.
  • First appearance — accused brought before justice within 24 hours (s. 503).
  • Bail (judicial interim release) — s. 515; ladder principle (Antic); s. 11(e) Charter.
  • Disclosure — Crown must disclose relevant material (Stinchcombe).
  • Election / plea — accused chooses mode of trial (indictable); pleads guilty or not guilty.
  • Preliminary inquiry — pre-trial test of evidence in serious indictable cases.
  • Verdict — guilty / not guilty (jury must be unanimous).
  • s. 11(b) Charter / Jordan ceilings — 18 months OCJ, 30 months SCJ.
  • Appeal — to provincial Court of Appeal; further appeal to SCC by leave (mostly).

Worked Example — A Bail Hearing

Trent is arrested at 9:00 pm Friday for armed robbery of a convenience store. Sometime before 9:00 pm Saturday he must be brought before a justice of the peace (s. 503).

At the bail hearing the following Monday, the Crown seeks detention. The defence asks for release on conditions. Applying section 515:

  1. Reverse onus? Armed robbery is not one of the s. 515(6) reverse-onus offences (those include murder, certain firearms offences while on existing bail, etc.). So the burden is on the Crown to show why detention is justified.
  2. Primary ground (attendance): Trent has roots in the community — a job, no record of failure to appear. Crown loses this ground.
  3. Secondary ground (public protection): The robbery was violent, with a firearm. Trent has no prior firearms charges. Crown might gain partial ground if circumstances suggest risk.
  4. Tertiary ground (confidence in administration of justice): The seriousness of armed robbery, especially with a firearm, may meet the bar in R v St-Cloud [2015] for tertiary-ground detention.
  5. Ladder principle (Antic): If release is granted, the justice must consider the least restrictive option — undertaking, then surety, then deposit, with conditions like curfew and no-contact orders.

A likely outcome: release on a surety with conditions, given Trent's roots and the Antic-mandated default of least restrictive release.

  • Believing arrest equals guilt — arrest is a step in investigation; guilt is determined at trial.
  • Treating bail as a "favour" to the accused — it is a Charter right under s. 11(e).
  • Forgetting Stinchcombe disclosure is constitutionally required, not a matter of Crown courtesy.
  • Calling the jury verdict the same as the judge's sentence — the jury decides guilt; the judge imposes sentence.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "describe the criminal trial process, from bail hearing through plea, trial, verdict, and sentencing."

🛡️ 3.4 Defences
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Defences in criminal law fall into two broad categories. Justifications argue that the conduct was actually right under the circumstances (self-defence under s. 34, defence of property under ss. 35, necessity in extreme situations). Excuses concede that the conduct was wrong but argue the accused should not be blamed because of an internal incapacity or external compulsion (mental disorder under s. 16, automatism, duress, intoxication where it negates intent). Some defences operate as negation of an element — they prevent the Crown from proving mens rea — while others operate as affirmative defences that the accused must put in issue with an evidentiary burden.

Self-defence (s. 34, as reformed by Bill C-26 in 2012). Three elements: (a) the accused reasonably believed that force was being used or threatened against them or another person; (b) the act was committed for the purpose of defending or protecting; (c) the act was reasonable in the circumstances. Section 34(2) lists nine factors for assessing reasonableness (nature of the force, imminence, presence of other means, role in the incident, weapons, size differential, history of the parties, nature of any response, proportionality). The leading case applying the reformed defence is R v Khill [2021] 2 SCR 269, which clarified the "role in the incident" factor.

Mental disorder (s. 16). Section 16(1) provides that no person is criminally responsible if at the time of the offence the person was suffering from a mental disorder that rendered them incapable of (a) appreciating the nature and quality of the act, or (b) knowing it was wrong. The disorder must be a "disease of the mind" — a legal concept, not a medical one (R v Stone [1999]). The verdict for a successful s. 16 defence is "not criminally responsible on account of mental disorder" (NCR), and the accused is then dealt with under Part XX.1 of the Code, which provides for assessment and disposition by a Review Board rather than criminal punishment. NCR is not an acquittal in the ordinary sense; it leads to a separate civil-style regime aimed at treatment and public safety.

Intoxication, duress, necessity, automatism. Voluntary intoxication is generally not a defence to general-intent offences (assault, sexual assault) but can negate the specific intent of certain offences (murder reduced to manslaughter). Extreme self-induced intoxication akin to automatism was for a time barred by s. 33.1 of the Code; the Supreme Court in R v Brown [2022] 1 SCR 374 struck down the old s. 33.1, and Parliament enacted a replacement provision later that year. Duress (s. 17 and common-law duress for parties) requires a threat of death or bodily harm and no safe avenue of escape (R v Ryan [2013]). Necessity (common-law) requires imminent peril, no reasonable legal alternative, and proportionality between harm avoided and harm caused (R v Latimer [2001]). Automatism is a state of impaired consciousness in which the accused has no voluntary control over actions; non-mental-disorder automatism (e.g., sleepwalking) leads to outright acquittal, mental-disorder automatism leads to NCR.

  • Justifications — the conduct was right (self-defence, defence of property, necessity).
  • Excuses — the conduct was wrong but the accused was not blameworthy (mental disorder, duress, automatism).
  • Self-defence (s. 34) — reasonable belief in force; defensive purpose; reasonable in the circumstances (9-factor list).
  • Mental disorder (s. 16) — disease of the mind; incapable of appreciating nature/quality OR knowing wrong; NCR verdict.
  • NCR — Not Criminally Responsible on account of Mental Disorder; Review Board disposition.
  • Intoxication — generally not a defence for general-intent; can negate specific intent.
  • Duress (s. 17) — threat of death/bodily harm + no safe avenue of escape.
  • Necessity — imminent peril + no reasonable legal alternative + proportionality (Perka, Latimer).
  • Automatism — impaired consciousness; non-mental-disorder leads to acquittal, mental-disorder leads to NCR.

Worked Example — Section 34 Self-Defence Analysis (Khill-Style Framework)

Late one night, Sahar hears noises at her car parked outside. She walks out with a baseball bat. A man, Kane, is leaning into her open driver-side door. He turns and steps toward her with a knife in hand. She swings the bat and strikes him on the head. He is hospitalised with a serious injury. She is charged with aggravated assault.

Section 34 analysis:

  1. s. 34(1)(a) — reasonable belief in force? Yes — Kane was advancing with a knife. Sahar's belief that force was being threatened is reasonable.
  2. s. 34(1)(b) — defensive purpose? Yes — she swung to stop him, not to punish or pursue.
  3. s. 34(1)(c) and (2) — reasonable in the circumstances? Apply the nine factors:
    • Nature of force / threat: knife;
    • Imminence: yes — he stepped toward her;
    • Other means: she had limited options at her own home at night;
    • Role in the incident (the Khill factor): she initiated the encounter by approaching, but only on her own property and only after hearing the intrusion;
    • Weapons: knife (his) vs bat (hers);
    • Size / capability: roughly equivalent;
    • History: no prior relationship;
    • Proportionality: a single bat strike versus a knife advance — arguably proportionate;
    • Nature of any response: not pursued after he fell.

Section 34 likely succeeds on these facts. The "role in the incident" factor introduced by Khill matters — the courts now look more carefully at whether the accused's earlier conduct (approaching, with a weapon) was reasonable in itself.

  • Treating self-defence as "anything goes if you're scared" — reasonableness is the controlling standard.
  • Confusing NCR with acquittal — NCR triggers Review Board disposition, often resulting in restrictions on liberty.
  • Believing intoxication is a complete defence — generally limited; especially restricted post-Brown for extreme intoxication akin to automatism.
  • Forgetting duress and necessity require no safe avenue of escape — if a reasonable alternative existed, the defence fails.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "analyse the role and types of defences (justifications, excuses, mental disorder, automatism, self-defence, intoxication)."

⚖️ 3.5 Sentencing
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Sentencing in Canada is governed by Part XXIII of the Criminal Code (sections 716 onward). Section 718 sets out the fundamental purpose: "to contribute, along with crime prevention initiatives, to respect for the law and the maintenance of a just, peaceful and safe society by imposing just sanctions that have one or more of the following objectives." Six purposes are then listed: denunciation, deterrence (general and specific), rehabilitation, separation from society, reparation, and promotion of a sense of responsibility. No single objective dominates; the sentencing judge weighs them in light of the offence, the offender, and the victim.

Section 718.1 is the fundamental principle of sentencing: "A sentence must be proportionate to the gravity of the offence and the degree of responsibility of the offender." Proportionality has both a retributive and a restraining function — it ensures sentences are severe enough to denounce serious wrongs and limited enough not to over-punish for moral fault that does not warrant it. Section 718.2 lists secondary principles: account for aggravating and mitigating circumstances, parity (similar sentences for similar offenders), totality (cumulative sentences must be just), and the special directive in s. 718.2(e) to consider all available sanctions other than imprisonment, with particular attention to Indigenous offenders.

The toolkit of sentencing dispositions runs from least to most severe: discharge (absolute, or conditional under s. 730), suspended sentence with probation, fine (s. 734), restitution, community service, conditional sentence served in the community (s. 742.1 — the "house arrest" alternative for non-violent, non-serious-personal-injury offences), intermittent sentence (s. 732 — weekends), and finally imprisonment in a provincial institution (under two years) or federal penitentiary (two years or more). Mandatory minimums for some firearms and certain other offences constrain judicial discretion; many mandatory minimums have been struck down on Charter section 12 grounds (cruel and unusual punishment) — see R v Nur [2015] 1 SCR 773 and R v Bissonnette [2022] 1 SCR 5.

Section 718.2(e) directs sentencing judges to consider "all available sanctions, other than imprisonment, that are reasonable in the circumstances and consistent with the harm done to victims or to the community… with particular attention to the circumstances of aboriginal offenders." This is the textual basis for R v Gladue [1999] 1 SCR 688 and R v Ipeelee [2012] 1 SCR 433: sentencing judges must consider the unique systemic factors that brought an Indigenous accused before the court (residential schools, intergenerational trauma, displacement, poverty) and the sentencing approaches available within the offender's Indigenous community. This is not a discount for Indigenous offenders; it is a substantive requirement that arises from the over-incarceration of Indigenous people (currently ~32% of federal inmates against ~5% of the general adult population — Correctional Investigator's annual report).

  • Section 718 — purposes of sentencing (denunciation, deterrence, rehabilitation, separation, reparation, responsibility).
  • Section 718.1 — fundamental principle: proportionality.
  • Section 718.2 — aggravating / mitigating, parity, totality, restraint, Indigenous offenders.
  • Discharge — absolute or conditional; no criminal record after conditions met.
  • Probation — community supervision; conditions (report, no-contact, treatment).
  • Conditional sentence (s. 742.1) — served in community with strict conditions ("house arrest").
  • Imprisonment — provincial (under 2 years), federal (2 years +).
  • Mandatory minimum — minimum sentence set by statute; some struck down under s. 12 Charter (Nur, Bissonnette).
  • Gladue / Ipeelee principles — Indigenous-offender sentencing considerations.
  • Victim impact statement — read at sentencing under s. 722.

Worked Example — Sentencing Reasoning

Devon, 24, pleads guilty to break and enter into a dwelling house (s. 348 — indictable, max life). He took $1,200 worth of electronics. He has no prior record but has been struggling with opioid addiction. He has begun treatment voluntarily before sentencing and the victim suffered no physical harm but reports continuing anxiety.

The sentencing judge applies s. 718 and 718.2:

  1. Section 718 purposes: denunciation (yes — break-and-enter into a home is serious); deterrence (general — yes; specific — addressed by treatment); rehabilitation (strongly weighted given Devon's age and treatment engagement); reparation (restitution order possible).
  2. Section 718.1 proportionality: the gravity of break-and-enter into a dwelling is high (the dwelling is symbolically and emotionally sacrosanct in Canadian sentencing law), but Devon's moral blameworthiness is moderated by addiction and treatment efforts.
  3. Section 718.2(a) aggravating / mitigating: aggravating — dwelling-house entry, victim's continuing anxiety; mitigating — no record, guilty plea, voluntary treatment, age.
  4. Section 718.2(d)(e) restraint: the judge considers whether something less than imprisonment is consistent with the harm done. Given the mitigating factors, a conditional sentence (s. 742.1, where statutory eligibility exists) or significant probation may be possible. Note that s. 742.1 has had restrictions removed and reinstated over the years — its current scope is shaped by 2022 amendments.
  5. Likely sentence: a conditional sentence of 18 months with strict conditions (curfew, treatment continuation, no-contact with victim, community service), followed by 2 years' probation; restitution of $1,200. The judge writes reasons explaining the weighing.

A different fact pattern — prior break-and-enter, weapon used, victim home at the time, no treatment — would push to a custodial sentence of 2–4 years in a federal penitentiary.

  • Treating sentencing as "what the prosecutor asks for" — judges have wide discretion within statutory limits.
  • Confusing parole eligibility with sentence length — parole is a separate decision by the Parole Board of Canada.
  • Believing Gladue principles are a "discount" — they are a substantive sentencing consideration tied to systemic over-incarceration.
  • Forgetting that victims have a voice (Victim Impact Statement, s. 722) but do not control the sentencing decision.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.4 — "analyse the principles and purposes of sentencing under section 718 of the Criminal Code (denunciation, deterrence, rehabilitation, reparation)."

🧑‍⚖️ 3.6 Youth Criminal Justice Act (YCJA)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Youth Criminal Justice Act (SC 2002, c. 1), in force April 1, 2003, governs the prosecution of persons aged 12 to 17 who are alleged to have committed offences under the Criminal Code or other federal statutes. It replaced the Young Offenders Act (1984), which had itself replaced the Juvenile Delinquents Act (1908). The YCJA sits at the intersection of two principles: young people should be held accountable for criminal conduct in a manner proportionate to their age and reduced moral blameworthiness, and the youth-justice system should focus on rehabilitation, reintegration, and the long-term protection of the public.

The YCJA's declaration of principle (section 3) is explicit. The youth-justice system is intended to (a) prevent crime by addressing underlying circumstances, (b) rehabilitate and reintegrate young persons, (c) ensure meaningful consequences for offending, and (d) protect the public. Section 3 also lists guiding principles: reduced moral culpability of young persons, enhanced procedural protections, timely intervention, and meaningful involvement of parents, victims, and community. The Supreme Court in R v DB [2008] 2 SCR 3 entrenched reduced moral blameworthiness as a principle of fundamental justice under section 7 of the Charter — meaning Parliament cannot impose adult sentences on youth without a substantial procedural safeguard.

The YCJA establishes a graduated approach. Police can warn, caution, or refer a young person to a community program for minor offences without laying a charge (sections 6–10). For more serious offences, prosecutors can use extrajudicial sanctions (s. 10) — formal alternatives like community service, restitution, or counselling that resolve the matter without a finding of guilt. If the case proceeds to court, youth-court judges have a range of sentencing options, including reprimand, fine (max $1,000), community service, intensive support and supervision, deferred custody and supervision, and youth custody (open or secure). A custodial sentence is presumptively reserved for violent offences and repeat or serious-violent offenders (s. 39). Youth records are tightly protected (s. 119) and access closes after a "period of access" that varies with the offence severity.

The most consequential modern issue under the YCJA is the procedure for an adult sentence. The Crown can apply to have a young person aged 14 or older sentenced as an adult for a presumptive offence (murder, attempted murder, manslaughter, aggravated sexual assault) or for a serious violent offence where the youth sentence would be inadequate (s. 64). Following R v DB, the onus to displace the presumption of a youth sentence rests on the Crown, not the youth — an important procedural safeguard. If an adult sentence is imposed, the young person can still serve it initially in a youth facility under section 76 conditions.

  • YCJA scope — ages 12–17 inclusive; under 12 not criminally responsible under federal law.
  • Section 3 principles — rehabilitation, reintegration, reduced moral blameworthiness, procedural protections.
  • Extrajudicial measures — warning, caution, referral; first-line response for non-violent offences.
  • Extrajudicial sanctions — formal program; admission of responsibility; no finding of guilt.
  • Youth sentence options — reprimand, fine (max $1,000), community service, probation, custody (last resort).
  • Section 39 custody — restricted; presumptively for violent offences only.
  • Adult sentence (s. 64) — Crown application; presumption of youth sentence; R v DB safeguards.
  • Privacy (s. 110, 119) — identity not published; records sealed after period of access.
  • Conferences (s. 19) — multidisciplinary discussions with youth, family, professionals.

Worked Example — YCJA Decision Tree

Aalia, age 15, takes a $400 video-game console from a department store without paying. Store security stops her. The police are called.

  1. Police initial response (s. 6). Section 6(1) requires the officer to consider whether it would be sufficient to (a) take no further action, (b) warn the young person, (c) administer a caution, or (d) refer to a community program. For a first-time, non-violent theft, sections 4–8 favour an extrajudicial measure. The officer warns Aalia and contacts her parents.
  2. Crown consideration (s. 10). If the police decide to recommend a charge, the Crown screens whether extrajudicial sanctions are appropriate. Aalia is offered an extrajudicial sanctions program: she must complete 20 hours of community service, write a letter of apology, and attend a one-day shoplifting-prevention workshop. She accepts (acceptance includes acknowledging responsibility but is not a guilty plea).
  3. If she completes the sanctions: the matter closes without a finding of guilt; no court process.
  4. If she does not complete: the matter can be referred back to court. Even there, custody is presumptively unavailable for a first-time non-violent property offence under s. 39.
  5. Privacy. Throughout, Aalia's identity may not be published (s. 110). Her records will be accessible only for a defined period (s. 119 — three years from the completion of the sanction for a summary-conviction-track matter).

The same conduct by an adult would proceed straight to a Criminal Code theft-under charge, the Crown would consider an absolute or conditional discharge, and the public record would attach. The YCJA's intermediate steps — warning, caution, sanctions, restricted custody — are designed to keep young people out of the formal criminal system unless and until that intervention is necessary.

  • Treating "youth" as identical to "adult, just younger" — the YCJA is a separate statute with separate principles.
  • Calling youth records "permanent" — most close after a defined period of access (s. 119); publication of identity is prohibited (s. 110).
  • Believing youth sentences are always custodial — section 39 restricts custody to violent offences and limited other circumstances.
  • Forgetting that R v DB entrenches reduced moral blameworthiness as a constitutional principle for youth sentencing.
