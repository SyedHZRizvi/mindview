SBI3U — Biology, Grade 11 University Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Science (2008, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: SBI3U  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: SNC2D (Grade 10 Academic Science)

1. Course Description

This course furthers students' understanding of the processes that occur in biological systems. Students will study theory and conduct investigations in the areas of biodiversity; evolution; genetic processes; the structure and function of animals; and the anatomy, growth, and function of plants. The course focuses on the theoretical aspects of the topics under study, and helps students refine skills related to scientific investigation. SBI3U prepares students for SBI4U (Grade 12 Biology) and post-secondary programs in the life sciences, health sciences, and environmental sciences.

3. Fundamental Concepts

ConceptApplication in SBI3U
MatterLiving things are composed of matter that cycles through ecosystems (water, carbon, nitrogen) and through individual organisms (digestion, respiration, transpiration).
Systems & InteractionsOrgan systems work together to maintain life; populations and species interact within communities and ecosystems; phylogenetic relationships group organisms based on shared ancestry.
Change & ContinuityEvolution drives change in populations over time; meiosis and fertilization ensure continuity of species; the fossil record documents change over geological time.
Structure & FunctionBody plans (vertebrate vs invertebrate), tissues, organs, and entire organ systems show that structure dictates function in animals and plants alike.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Diversity of Living Things (B): Biodiversity comprises the range of organisms within a species, ecosystem, or biosphere; classification reflects evolutionary relationships.
  • Evolution (C): Populations of living organisms evolve over time through natural selection; lines of evidence (fossils, biogeography, anatomy, molecular biology) support evolutionary theory.
  • Genetic Processes (D): DNA, chromosomes, and inheritance link the cellular level to whole-organism traits and underlie modern biotechnology.
  • Animals: Structure and Function (E): Animals depend on the coordinated functioning of specialized organ systems to obtain nutrients, exchange gases, transport materials, eliminate wastes, and respond to the environment.
  • Plants: Anatomy, Growth, and Function (F): Plants have specialized structures that enable them to grow, reproduce, and respond to environmental cues; plant productivity sustains agricultural and natural ecosystems.

5. Strand A — Scientific Investigation Skills & Career Exploration (embedded across all units)

A1. Initiating & Planning

  • A1.1 formulate scientific questions and testable hypotheses about biological phenomena
  • A1.2 plan controlled experiments — identify independent, dependent, controlled variables; design experimental procedures

A2. Performing & Recording

  • A2.1–A2.6 use appropriate laboratory equipment (compound microscope, dissection tools, dichotomous keys, spirometer); follow WHMIS and safety protocols; record observations qualitatively and quantitatively

A3. Analysing & Interpreting

  • A3.1–A3.5 analyse data using charts, graphs, and simple statistics (mean, ratios, Punnett-square probability, Simpson's biodiversity index); evaluate sources of error; draw justified conclusions

A4. Communicating

  • A4.1–A4.5 use proper biological terminology and conventions (binomial nomenclature, anatomical terms); communicate using lab reports, posters, models, oral presentations, multimedia

Career Connections

Biologist, zoologist, botanist, geneticist, microbiologist, marine biologist, taxonomist, agricultural scientist, conservation officer, environmental consultant, veterinarian, nurse, physician, dentist, pharmacist, forensic scientist, science teacher, science journalist.

6. Strand B — Diversity of Living Things (Chapter 1, ~30 hours)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. analyse the effects of various human activities on the diversity of living things;
  • B2. investigate, through laboratory and/or field activities or through simulations, the principles of scientific classification, using appropriate sampling and classification techniques;
  • B3. demonstrate an understanding of the diversity of living organisms in terms of the principles of taxonomy and phylogeny.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B2.2 use a dichotomous key to identify and classify representative organisms;
  • B2.3 conduct a biodiversity inventory or comparison using sampling techniques and an appropriate biodiversity index (e.g., Simpson's D = 1 − Σ(pi)2);
  • B3.1 describe the anatomical and behavioural characteristics used to group organisms into the three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) and the major eukaryotic kingdoms (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia);
  • B3.2 describe the binomial nomenclature system and the Linnaean taxonomic hierarchy (domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species);
  • B3.3 explain the principle of phylogeny and how molecular data (DNA, rRNA) refine classification;
  • B3.5 explain the major characteristics of representative vertebrate and invertebrate phyla (Porifera, Cnidaria, Mollusca, Arthropoda, Echinodermata, Chordata).
STSE focus: human impacts on biodiversity (habitat destruction, invasive species, overharvesting); the role of natural and provincial parks; the Convention on Biological Diversity.

7. Strand C — Evolution (Chapter 2, ~25 hours)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. analyse the economic and environmental advantages and disadvantages of an artificial selection technology, and evaluate the impact of environmental changes on natural selection and endangered species;
  • C2. investigate evolutionary processes, using laboratory or computer-simulated activities and field-based research;
  • C3. demonstrate an understanding of the theory of evolution, the evidence that supports it, and some of the mechanisms by which it occurs.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C3.1 explain the fundamental theory of biological evolution as developed by Darwin and Wallace and refined by the modern synthesis (population genetics);
  • C3.2 explain the four principles of natural selection: variation, heritability, differential survival/reproduction, and accumulation over generations;
  • C3.3 distinguish among the major sources of variation: mutation, sexual reproduction (independent assortment, crossing over, random fertilization);
  • C3.4 describe lines of evidence for evolution: fossil record, biogeography (e.g., Galápagos finches), comparative anatomy (homologous, analogous, vestigial structures), embryology, molecular biology (DNA/protein similarity);
  • C3.5 distinguish between micro- and macro-evolution; describe allopatric and sympatric speciation;
  • C3.6 describe the Hardy–Weinberg principle as a null model (p² + 2pq + q² = 1).
STSE focus: artificial selection in agriculture (Bt corn, dairy cows); antibiotic resistance in bacteria; conservation of endangered species (panda, polar bear, Atlantic cod).

8. Strand D — Genetic Processes (Chapter 3, ~25 hours)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. evaluate the importance of some recent contributions to our knowledge of genetic processes, and analyse social and ethical implications of genetic and genomic research;
  • D2. investigate genetic processes, including those that occur during meiosis, and analyse data to solve basic genetics problems involving monohybrid and dihybrid crosses;
  • D3. demonstrate an understanding of concepts, principles, and theories related to genetic processes.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D2.2 conduct a laboratory or simulated investigation to demonstrate Mendelian inheritance patterns; calculate expected ratios using Punnett squares;
  • D2.3 analyse data from monohybrid and dihybrid crosses; analyse pedigrees for autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, and X-linked traits;
  • D3.1 describe the cell cycle and the stages of meiosis I and II, distinguishing meiosis from mitosis;
  • D3.2 describe Mendel's laws of segregation and independent assortment;
  • D3.3 explain non-Mendelian inheritance patterns: incomplete dominance, codominance (ABO blood groups, MN), multiple alleles, polygenic inheritance, sex-linkage, sex-influenced traits;
  • D3.4 describe chromosomal abnormalities (non-disjunction → trisomy 21 Down syndrome; Turner XO, Klinefelter XXY; structural mutations: deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation);
  • D3.5 explain the relationship between meiosis and genetic variation (independent assortment, crossing over, random fertilization).
STSE focus: genetic counselling and pre-natal screening; GMO crops and labelling; pharmacogenomics; Human Genome Project; privacy and direct-to-consumer DNA tests.

9. Strand E — Animals: Structure and Function (Chapter 4, ~30 hours)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. analyse the relationships between changing societal needs, technological advances, and our understanding of internal systems of humans;
  • E2. investigate, through laboratory inquiry or computer simulation, the functional responses of the respiratory and circulatory systems of animals, and the relationships between their respiratory, circulatory, and digestive systems;
  • E3. demonstrate an understanding of the anatomy and physiology of human body systems, and explain how systems work together to maintain homeostasis.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E2.2 conduct a laboratory investigation of vital capacity, tidal volume, and reserve volumes using a spirometer; or a heart-rate response to exercise;
  • E3.1 describe the structure and function of the human digestive system (mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine villi/microvilli, large intestine, accessory organs: liver, pancreas, gall bladder);
  • E3.2 describe the structure and function of the circulatory system (heart, four chambers, valves, systemic vs pulmonary circuit, arteries vs veins vs capillaries, blood components, lymphatic system);
  • E3.3 describe the structure and function of the respiratory system (nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli, diaphragm, pleura) and gas exchange;
  • E3.4 describe the structure and function of the excretory system (kidney → nephron: glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, collecting duct);
  • E3.5 describe the structure and function of the nervous system (CNS vs PNS; somatic vs autonomic; sympathetic vs parasympathetic; neuron structure: dendrites, axon, myelin, synapse; reflex arc).
STSE focus: organ transplantation; vaccines and the immune response; smoking, vaping, and lung disease; obesity and metabolic syndrome; dialysis; CPR and AEDs.

10. Strand F — Plants: Anatomy, Growth, and Function (Chapter 5, ~20 hours)

Overall Expectations

  • F1. evaluate the importance of sustainable use of plants to Canadian society and other cultures;
  • F2. investigate the structures and functions of plant tissues, and factors affecting plant growth;
  • F3. demonstrate an understanding of the diversity, structure, and function of vascular plants.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • F2.2 conduct a laboratory investigation of plant cells and tissues (dermal, ground, vascular) using prepared slides or fresh specimens;
  • F2.3 investigate factors that affect plant growth (light intensity, light wavelength, gravity, water, minerals, plant hormones);
  • F3.1 describe the structure and function of the three plant tissue systems: dermal (epidermis, cuticle, stomata, guard cells, trichomes), vascular (xylem with vessels and tracheids; phloem with sieve tubes and companion cells), ground (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma);
  • F3.2 describe the structure and function of roots (tap vs fibrous, root hairs, Casparian strip), stems (monocot vs dicot, primary vs secondary growth, annual rings) and leaves (mesophyll: palisade vs spongy; veins; stomata);
  • F3.3 explain transport in plants: water and mineral uptake; the cohesion–tension theory of xylem transport (transpiration pull); pressure-flow model of phloem translocation;
  • F3.4 describe plant reproduction: alternation of generations, flower anatomy, pollination, fertilization, seed and fruit formation; vegetative propagation;
  • F3.5 describe agricultural and economic importance of plants (food, fibre, medicine, ecological services).
STSE focus: sustainable forestry; genetically modified crops; food security; native vs cultivated plants; traditional Indigenous uses of plants; horticulture.

13. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, labs, performance tasks, projects, assignments. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a culminating performance task and/or a final examination administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

14. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, reflection, peer review).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction and student goal-setting.
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, performance tasks, final exam).

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation.
