CHC2D — Canadian History since World War I, Grade 10 Academic

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9 and 10: Canadian and World Studies (2018, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CHC2D  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: None (Grade 9 Geography CGC1D or CGC1P is recommended)

1. Course Description

This course explores social, economic, and political developments and events and their impact on the lives of different individuals, groups, and communities, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit individuals and communities, in Canada since 1914. Students will examine the role of conflict and cooperation in Canadian society, Canada's evolving role within the global community, and the impact of various individuals, organizations, and events on Canadian identity, citizenship, and heritage. They will develop their ability to apply the concepts of historical thinking and the historical inquiry process, including the interpretation and analysis of evidence, when investigating key issues and events in Canadian history since 1914.

3. The Historical Thinking Concepts (Seixas & Morton, "Big Six")

ConceptDefinition (CHC2D Application)
Historical SignificanceWhat makes an event, person, or development significant? How does significance change with time and perspective? (e.g., is Vimy Ridge a turning point in Canadian nationhood, or a myth made retroactively significant?)
Cause and ConsequenceWhat causes (long, short, intended, unintended) led to an event? What were its consequences for different groups? (e.g., causes and consequences of the conscription crisis of 1917, the 1995 Quebec referendum)
Continuity and ChangeWhat has changed, what has stayed the same, and at what pace? (e.g., from Indian Act 1876 to TRC 2015 — continuity of colonial structures even amid policy change)
Historical PerspectiveReconstructing how people in the past understood their world, without imposing present values. (e.g., understanding why thousands of Canadians enlisted in 1914 without retrospective condescension)
EvidenceUsing primary and secondary sources to construct, support, and evaluate historical accounts (war diaries, photographs, RCMP files, oral testimony at TRC)
Ethical DimensionsMaking fair judgments about past conduct and considering responsibilities those judgments create today (residential schools, Japanese-Canadian internment, MS St. Louis)

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Strand A — Historical Inquiry & Skills (embedded): Historical understanding requires deliberate inquiry, source evaluation, and the disciplined use of the six historical thinking concepts.
  • Strand B — Canada 1914–1929: Canada's participation in the First World War transformed its society, economy, and place in the world; the social fractures that emerged (conscription, labour unrest, the role of women, Indigenous service) reshape national identity.
  • Strand C — Canada 1929–1945: The Great Depression and the Second World War tested the limits of Canadian institutions and reshaped the federal state, while also exposing failures of inclusion (Japanese-Canadian internment, MS St. Louis, Indigenous enlistment without citizenship rights).
  • Strand D — Canada 1945–1982: Postwar prosperity, Cold War alignment, and large-scale immigration reshaped Canadian society; movements for Indigenous rights, Québécois self-determination, women's equality, and multiculturalism converged toward patriation and a Charter of Rights.
  • Strand E — Canada 1982–Present: The Charter era has reshaped Canadian citizenship, federalism, foreign policy, and reconciliation, even as questions of national unity, Indigenous sovereignty, and global responsibility remain unresolved.

5. Strand A — Historical Inquiry & Skills (embedded across all units)

Overall Expectations

  • A1. Historical Inquiry: use the historical inquiry process and the concepts of historical thinking when investigating aspects of Canadian history since 1914;
  • A2. Developing Transferable Skills: apply in everyday contexts skills developed through historical investigation, and identify careers in which a background in history might be an asset.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • A1.1 formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in Canadian history since 1914;
  • A1.2 select and organize relevant evidence and information from a variety of primary and secondary sources;
  • A1.3 assess the credibility of sources and information relevant to their investigations;
  • A1.4 interpret and analyse evidence and information relevant to their investigations, using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for historical inquiry;
  • A1.5 use the concepts of historical thinking when analysing, evaluating evidence about, and formulating conclusions and/or judgements regarding historical issues, events, and/or developments in Canada since 1914;
  • A1.7 communicate their ideas, arguments, and conclusions using various formats and styles, as appropriate for the audience and purpose;
  • A2.1 describe several ways in which historical investigation can help them develop skills, including the essential skills in the Ontario Skills Passport.
Cross-curricular focus: students apply the six historical thinking concepts to every unit; one full unit (Unit 5) treats methodology explicitly.

6. Strand B — Canada 1914–1929 (Unit 1)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe some key social, economic, and political events, trends, and developments between 1914 and 1929, and assess their significance for different groups in Canada;
  • B2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse some key interactions within and between communities in Canada, and between Canada and the international community, from 1914 to 1929, and how these interactions affected Canadian society and politics;
  • B3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: explain how various individuals, groups, and events, including some major international events, contributed to the development of identity, citizenship, and heritage in Canada between 1914 and 1929.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 describe some key social, economic, and political events of Canada's participation in World War I (e.g., the Battle of Vimy Ridge, the introduction of income tax, the Wartime Elections Act, the Halifax Explosion);
  • B1.2 describe the social and economic situation in Canada in the 1920s (e.g., urbanisation, the rise of consumerism, regional disparities, the role of women in the workplace);
  • B2.2 analyse the conscription crisis of 1917 and its impact on Canada (English-French relations, the 1917 election, the Military Service Act);
  • B2.4 describe interactions between First Nations and the Canadian government during this period (residential schools, the Indian Act 1920 amendment, Indigenous enlistment without citizenship);
  • B3.1 explain how some events from World War I and the postwar period (Vimy, Passchendaele, the Statute of Westminster's antecedents, the Persons Case 1929) contributed to a developing sense of Canadian identity.
Anchor topics: Vimy Ridge; the conscription crisis; the Winnipeg General Strike 1919; women's suffrage; the Persons Case.

7. Strand C — Canada 1929–1945 (Unit 2)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe some key social, economic, and political events, trends, and developments in Canada between 1929 and 1945, and assess their significance for different groups in Canada;
  • C2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse some key interactions within and between communities in Canada, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities, and between Canada and the international community, from 1929 to 1945;
  • C3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse how significant events and developments between 1929 and 1945 contributed to the development of identity, citizenship, and heritage in Canada.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 describe some key social and economic conditions in Canada during the Depression (unemployment 30%, "On-to-Ottawa Trek," relief camps, Prairie drought);
  • C1.3 describe Canada's role in World War II (Dieppe 1942, Battle of the Atlantic, Italian Campaign, D-Day and Normandy, liberation of the Netherlands);
  • C2.3 analyse the internment of Japanese Canadians (1942) and other restrictions on civil liberties during the war;
  • C2.4 explain Canada's response to the refugee crisis of the late 1930s (MS St. Louis 1939, the antisemitic "none is too many" policy);
  • C3.2 analyse how the war affected the role of women in Canadian society (factory employment, CWAC/RCAF-WD, return to domestic role 1945).
STSE focus: scientific and technological developments (radar, RDX, penicillin production, atomic research); civil liberties and the War Measures Act.

8. Strand D — Canada 1945–1982 (Unit 3)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe some key social, economic, and political events, trends, and developments in Canada between 1945 and 1982, and assess their significance for different groups in Canada;
  • D2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse some key experiences of and interactions between different communities in Canada, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities, as well as between Canada and the international community, from 1945 to 1982;
  • D3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse how significant events, individuals, and groups, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit individuals and communities, contributed to the development of identity, citizenship, and heritage in Canada between 1945 and 1982.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.2 describe the postwar economic boom and the rise of the welfare state (universal health care 1966 — Medical Care Act, Canada Pension Plan 1965);
  • D1.4 analyse the Quiet Revolution in Quebec (1960s) and its consequences (secularisation, "maîtres chez nous," nationalisation of hydroelectricity);
  • D2.3 analyse Cold War involvement: NATO 1949, NORAD 1958, the Avro Arrow 1959, the Cuban Missile Crisis 1962, peacekeeping origins (Pearson 1956 Suez);
  • D2.5 examine the FLQ October Crisis 1970 and the invocation of the War Measures Act;
  • D3.3 analyse the residential schools system in the postwar period, the 1969 White Paper, and the rise of Indigenous political organisation (NIB, Manitoulin Treaty Council).
Anchor topics: Baby boom; Avro Arrow; immigration policy shift (1967 points system); Quiet Revolution; FLQ; Multiculturalism Act 1971.

9. Strand E — Canada 1982–Present (Unit 4)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. Social, Economic, and Political Context: describe some key social, economic, and political events, trends, and developments in Canada from 1982 to the present, and assess their significance for different groups in Canada;
  • E2. Communities, Conflict, and Cooperation: analyse some key experiences of and interactions between different communities in Canada, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities, as well as between Canada and the international community, from 1982 to the present;
  • E3. Identity, Citizenship, and Heritage: analyse how various significant individuals, groups, and events have contributed to the development of identity, citizenship, and heritage in Canada from 1982 to the present.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.1 analyse the patriation of the Constitution (1982) and the entrenchment of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms;
  • E1.3 describe Canada's role in the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA 1994 / USMCA 2020) and its effects on different sectors;
  • E2.2 analyse the failed Meech Lake Accord (1987–1990) and Charlottetown Accord (1992), and the 1995 Quebec referendum (50.58% No);
  • E2.4 describe Indigenous events including Oka Crisis 1990, Nisga'a Treaty 2000, Ipperwash Inquiry, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission 2009–2015 and its 94 Calls to Action;
  • E3.2 analyse Canada's role in post-9/11 conflicts including Afghanistan 2001–2014 and the decision not to join the Iraq invasion 2003.
STSE focus: the digital revolution (BlackBerry, internet adoption); climate change as a Canadian policy issue; pandemic response.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, source analyses, in-class essays, inquiry projects, oral presentations, and seminars. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a culminating historical-inquiry task (15%) and the Final Examination (15%) administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, source-analysis worksheets, vocabulary checks, timeline-construction exercises).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction (e.g., pre-unit diagnostic, mid-inquiry conferencing, draft thesis feedback).
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (unit tests, formal essays, inquiry projects, oral presentations, final exam).

14. Differentiated Instruction & Universal Design for Learning

Multiple means of representation (primary documents in transcript and audio; captioned archival film; photo essays); multiple means of engagement (choice of inquiry topic, choice of community/individual to study); multiple means of expression (essay, podcast, infographic, video documentary). The course centres First Nations, Métis, and Inuit perspectives throughout, not as an addendum, in alignment with the TRC's Calls to Action 62 and 63.

15. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation. Particularly important in CHC2D for sustaining multi-week inquiry projects and for ethical engagement with difficult content (residential schools, internment, the Holocaust).
