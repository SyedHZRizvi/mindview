📖 1.1 What is Law? Functions, Categories, and the Rule of Law
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Law is the body of binding rules a society uses to order conduct, resolve disputes, and allocate the legitimate use of force. Each element of that definition does analytical work. Law is binding — non-compliance attracts consequences enforced by the state. Law is public — it issues from recognised institutions (Parliament, legislatures, courts), not private will. Law is general — it applies to categories of persons and situations, not to named individuals. And law is institutionalised — there are agencies authorised to make it, interpret it, and apply it. A grocery store's no-shoplifting sign is a private rule; section 322 of the Criminal Code, which defines theft, is law.

Canadian law performs four classical functions: it establishes rules of conduct (don't steal, drive on the right, file a tax return); it resolves disputes (courts and tribunals adjudicate competing claims); it protects rights and freedoms (the Charter, the Human Rights Code, the various international human-rights treaties Canada has ratified); and it provides for orderly change (legislation amends prior law; courts overrule prior cases through the principle of stare decisis with departure). Grade 12 students should be able to give a specific example of each function and explain how the four interact — for instance, how Parliament's enactment of the Cannabis Act (2018) simultaneously changed rules of conduct, created new dispute-resolution avenues (regulatory tribunals), and provided for orderly change to the prior criminal regime.

Law is divided most usefully into public versus private. Public law governs relationships between the individual and the state: constitutional law (the supreme law), criminal law (offences against society), administrative law (rules of government agencies), tax law. Private law governs relationships between individuals: contract, tort, property, family, employment. A speeding ticket is public (the Crown vs the driver); a slip-and-fall lawsuit is private (the injured person vs the store). The same physical event can spawn both — a drunk-driving collision can produce a criminal charge (public) and a civil suit for damages (private), with independent procedures and outcomes.

The rule of law is the constitutional principle that everyone — including the government — is subject to the law, that laws are applied predictably and equally, and that no one can be punished except for a breach of an established law. The Supreme Court of Canada reaffirmed this in Reference re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 217, identifying the rule of law as one of four unwritten constitutional principles (alongside federalism, democracy, and the protection of minorities). Students should distinguish the rule of law from rule by law: an authoritarian regime might still use laws to govern, but if the rulers themselves are not bound, and if laws can be retroactively rewritten to punish enemies, the rule of law is absent.

  • Rule of law — government is bound by law; law is applied predictably and equally.
  • Public law — state vs individual: constitutional, criminal, administrative.
  • Private law — individual vs individual: contract, tort, property, family, employment.
  • Substantive law — defines rights and duties.
  • Procedural law — defines how rights are enforced (arrest, trial, appeal).
  • Domestic law — law within one state.
  • International law — law governing relations between states.
  • Jurisprudence — the philosophy and theory of law.
  • Codified vs uncodified — written in a single statute book vs scattered across statutes and cases.

Worked Example — One Event, Four Bodies of Law

Scenario: Anna is driving and runs a red light, striking Ben's car. Ben suffers a broken arm. Anna had two beers earlier and blows over 80 mg per 100 mL on a roadside breath test.

This single incident triggers four distinct bodies of law:

  1. Criminal law (public, substantive): the Crown may charge Anna with impaired driving under section 320.14 of the Criminal Code.
  2. Quasi-criminal / provincial regulatory (public): Anna also faces Highway Traffic Act charges (running a red, careless driving).
  3. Tort law (private, substantive): Ben can sue Anna in negligence for damages — broken arm, vehicle repair, lost income.
  4. Procedural law (public + private): the criminal trial follows the Criminal Code's procedural rules; the civil suit follows the Rules of Civil Procedure.

The same conduct produces independent proceedings. A criminal conviction does not automatically award Ben damages, and a successful civil suit does not put Anna in jail. Each system answers a different question.

  • Confusing the criminal and civil tracks ("she was acquitted, so he can't sue").
  • Treating "law" as identical to "police" or "court" — law is the rule; police enforce, courts adjudicate.
  • Forgetting that constitutional law sits above ordinary statute — Parliament cannot legislate around the Charter without invoking section 33.
  • Calling the rule of law the same as "the law is always right." The rule of law means law applies equally; it does not guarantee a law is just.

🏛️ 1.2 Natural Law vs Legal Positivism
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The oldest and deepest debate in jurisprudence is between natural law and legal positivism. The question they answer is the same: when we ask "what is law?", do we answer in terms of what it ought to be (its morality) or what it actually is (its sources)? The answer chosen has enormous consequences for how a lawyer reads a statute, how a judge writes a decision, and how a citizen responds to an unjust law.

Natural law theory holds that law and morality are necessarily connected. An unjust law, on a strong natural-law view, is not a law at all — lex iniusta non est lex. Thomas Aquinas, drawing on Aristotle, argued in the Summa Theologiae that human law derives its authority from natural law, which in turn participates in divine eternal law. A statute that violates the natural law is not binding on the conscience. Modern natural lawyers (John Finnis, Lon Fuller) usually defend a weaker version: morality is internal to the very idea of law, so that a system of rules so monstrous that no one could be expected to follow them does not deserve the name "law." Fuller's inner morality of law (1964) requires that rules be public, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, capable of performance, and stable — failures of which (he argued through the example of Nazi rule) are failures of legality, not merely failures of justice.

Legal positivism rejects the conceptual link between law and morality. For Jeremy Bentham (writing against Blackstone) and John Austin (1832), law is the command of a sovereign, backed by sanction; an unjust command is still a command and still a law. The 20th-century positivist H.L.A. Hart refined this in The Concept of Law (1961): a legal system is a union of primary rules (rules of conduct) and secondary rules (rules about rules — recognition, change, adjudication), and the rule of recognition is what identifies what counts as law in a given society. Morality may guide what we want law to be, but it is not part of what law is. Hart famously debated Fuller about the Nazi-informer cases after World War II: were grudge informers, who used Nazi statutes to send rivals to the camps, breaking the law or following it? Hart said they followed the law — wicked law, but law — and post-war courts should have used new, retrospective legislation to prosecute them rather than pretending the Nazi statutes had been invalid all along.

The debate is not academic. When a Canadian judge interprets section 7 of the Charter (life, liberty, and security of the person), she must decide whether the "principles of fundamental justice" mentioned there are positively determined by the text and prior cases, or whether they reach beyond positive law into a moral substratum that includes the dignity of the person. The Supreme Court's gestures in cases like Reference re BC Motor Vehicle Act [1985] 2 SCR 486 — holding that a statute imposing absolute liability for imprisonable offences violates fundamental justice — show that Canadian constitutional law contains both positivist and natural-law strands. Civil disobedience, conscientious objection, and Nuremberg's Justice trials (1947) all turn on whether there is a moral order that limits what positive law can validly demand.

  • Natural law — law derives validity from a higher moral order; unjust laws lack full authority.
  • Lex iniusta non est lex — "an unjust law is not a law" (Aquinas, paraphrased).
  • Legal positivism — law's validity depends on its sources, not its content.
  • Command theory — Austin's view: law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanction.
  • Rule of recognition — Hart's idea: a master rule identifying what counts as law in a system.
  • Inner morality of law — Fuller's eight principles a system must roughly satisfy to be legal.
  • Hart–Fuller debate — the post-war exchange about Nazi law in the Harvard Law Review (1958).
  • Conscientious objection — refusal to obey a law on moral grounds; the natural-law tradition's classical fact-pattern.

Worked Example — Applying Both Theories to a Single Statute

Imagine Parliament enacts a law: "Any person who criticises a government minister commits an offence punishable by two years' imprisonment."

Positivist reading: The statute was enacted by Parliament following normal legislative procedure. It is recognised by courts and police. Therefore it is law. Its merits are a separate question — moral, political, possibly unconstitutional under the Charter — but it remains law until repealed or struck down.

Natural-law reading: The statute attempts to criminalise the exercise of a fundamental human capacity (political speech). It violates the natural law's protection of practical reason and political participation. On a strong reading, citizens have no moral obligation to obey it; on Fuller's weaker reading, the statute violates the inner morality of law because it makes ordinary political conduct unpredictably criminal. Either way, the natural lawyer can reject the statute's legal authority on grounds the positivist treats as external.

In the Canadian system, the same statute would almost certainly be struck down under section 2(b) of the Charter (freedom of expression), which is — interestingly — a positive enactment that incorporates substantive moral content. Constitutional review thus partly bridges the natural-law and positivist positions, by giving positive legal effect to what natural lawyers would call pre-positive moral limits.

  • Treating natural law as religious doctrine — modern natural-law theorists (Finnis, Fuller) defend secular versions.
  • Treating positivism as moral indifference — Hart and Bentham were both fierce moral reformers; they just thought reform required identifying law clearly first.
  • Assuming Canadian constitutional law is purely positivist because it is written — section 7 and section 1 import substantive moral concepts.
  • Confusing positivism with "the law is whatever the dictator says" — Hart's rule of recognition is a complex social rule, not a tyrant's whim.

⚖️ 1.3 Legal Realism, Critical Legal Studies, Feminist Jurisprudence
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

If natural law and positivism are the two classical poles of legal philosophy, the 20th and 21st centuries added three further movements that asked harder questions about what actually happens when judges decide cases: legal realism, critical legal studies, and feminist jurisprudence. Each shifts the focus from the abstract concept of law to the social practice of legal decision-making.

Legal realism began with the American jurists Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Karl Llewellyn, and Jerome Frank, writing in the early 20th century. Their core claim is that legal doctrine, taken on its own, under-determines judicial decisions. The same statute can be read multiple ways; precedents pull in different directions; "the law" alone cannot tell us how a real case will turn out. What does the predictive work, the realists said, is the judge's psychology, the social context, and the political background — Holmes's famous line that "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law" (The Path of the Law, 1897). Canadian legal realism is most visible in Supreme Court decisions that openly weigh policy consequences — for example, the policy stage of the duty-of-care analysis in Cooper v Hobart [2001] 3 SCR 537 (the second stage of the Anns/Cooper test) explicitly asks whether recognising a new duty would produce undesirable social consequences. Realism is not cynicism; it is an honesty about how the sausage gets made.

Critical legal studies (CLS), emerging in U.S. and Canadian law schools in the late 1970s, radicalised the realist insight. CLS scholars (Duncan Kennedy, Roberto Unger, in Canada Allan Hutchinson) argued that legal doctrine is not just indeterminate but is systematically structured to entrench existing relations of power. A doctrine like "freedom of contract" looks neutral but in practice empowers the party with greater bargaining power. The standard story that law is impartial, the CLS scholars said, masks the political work that law does. Indigenous and Black critical scholars later extended this to argue that Canadian legal categories themselves were built around the experience of certain groups and continue to disadvantage others (see R v Ipeelee [2012] 1 SCR 433 on the persistent over-incarceration of Indigenous offenders despite Gladue principles).

Feminist jurisprudence (Catharine MacKinnon in the U.S., Patricia Hughes and Beverley Baines in Canada) asks how legal categories, doctrines, and practices have historically excluded women's experience. It examines, for example, why "reasonable person" so often meant "reasonable man" (a barrier the Supreme Court addressed in R v Lavallee [1990] 1 SCR 852 on battered-woman syndrome and self-defence); why sexual-assault evidentiary rules historically penalised the complainant (reformed in R v Seaboyer [1991] 2 SCR 577 and successor cases); why categories like "public" and "private" have shielded domestic violence from legal scrutiny. Different feminist schools — liberal, radical, intersectional — offer different prescriptions, but all share the conviction that taking gender seriously is part of analysing law honestly.

  • Legal realism — legal doctrine under-determines decisions; psychology, policy, and context fill the gap.
  • The Path of the Law — Holmes's 1897 article launching American realism.
  • Critical legal studies (CLS) — law as systematically political; "neutral" doctrine masks power.
  • Indeterminacy thesis — the realist/CLS claim that the same rules can yield opposite results.
  • Feminist jurisprudence — analysing law's gendered assumptions and effects.
  • Intersectionality — Kimberlé Crenshaw's framework; women experience law through multiple, overlapping identities.
  • Battered-woman syndrome — expert evidence admitted in R v Lavallee; reshaped self-defence.
  • Gladue principles — sentencing consideration of Indigenous offenders' circumstances.

Worked Example — Three Theories Reading One Case

Consider R v Lavallee [1990] 1 SCR 852. Angelique Lavallee shot her abusive partner Kevin Rust in the back of the head as he was leaving the room, after years of escalating violence. She was charged with murder; her defence was self-defence under the old s. 34(2) of the Criminal Code, which required reasonable apprehension of imminent death or grievous bodily harm.

Positivist reading: The court must apply the statutory language. "Imminent" should be given its ordinary meaning. The shooting was not in response to an imminent attack, so the statute appears not to apply. The Supreme Court's allowing self-defence is either a creative interpretation or a quiet rewriting.

Realist reading: The court was responding to a social problem — battered women whose accumulated knowledge of their abuser told them when violence was about to escalate. Strict literal reading of "imminent" would have produced unjust results in a recurring pattern of cases. Justice Wilson's reasoning, allowing expert evidence on battered-woman syndrome, was policy-driven.

Feminist reading: The pre-Lavallee doctrine of self-defence assumed a particular fact-pattern — a single confrontation between strangers of roughly equal strength. That model excluded the typical experience of battered women, whose situations involve power asymmetries, recurring violence, and credible threats that may not be "imminent" in the model's narrow sense. Lavallee incorporated women's experience into the substantive law of self-defence.

All three readings are partial truths about the same decision; the realist and feminist readings, together, explain why the positivist reading was unstable.

  • Treating realism as the claim that "law doesn't matter" — realists think doctrine matters but is incomplete.
  • Treating CLS as a partisan movement rather than an analytic stance — CLS scholars can be politically diverse.
  • Reducing feminist jurisprudence to a single school — liberal, radical, cultural, and intersectional feminism diverge.
  • Forgetting that Canadian appellate courts cite all three traditions, sometimes in the same paragraph.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "analyse the main philosophical schools of legal thought (natural law, legal positivism, legal realism, critical legal studies, feminist jurisprudence, Indigenous legal traditions)."

🪶 1.4 Sources of Canadian Law & Indigenous Legal Traditions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canadian law is not produced by any single institution. It is the product of overlapping sources, each with its own authority and method. A lawyer's first analytical move on receiving a problem is to ask which sources govern. The five primary sources are: the Constitution, statutes, regulations, common-law cases, and (in Quebec for private matters) the Civil Code. To these, Canadian law increasingly recognises Indigenous legal traditions as a sixth, independent source — not merely a subject of law, but a source of law in its own right.

The Constitution is the supreme law of Canada. Section 52(1) of the Constitution Act, 1982 states: "The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law of Canada, and any law that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect." This includes the Constitution Act, 1867 (which created the federal structure and divided powers between Parliament and the provinces in sections 91 and 92), the Constitution Act, 1982 (which patriated the Constitution, added the Charter, and entrenched Aboriginal and treaty rights in section 35), and various other constitutional documents listed in the schedule to the 1982 Act. Constitutional law sits above everything else: a statute that conflicts with the Constitution is invalid to the extent of the conflict.

Statutes (Acts of Parliament or a provincial legislature) are the most common source of new law. Bill C-46 (the impaired-driving reforms of 2018), the Cannabis Act, the Youth Criminal Justice Act are all statutes. A statute is created by a bill that passes three readings in each chamber and receives Royal Assent. Regulations are delegated legislation — rules made by a minister, a Cabinet, or an administrative body under authority delegated by a statute. Regulations have the force of law but only insofar as they stay within the enabling statute's authority (the doctrine of ultra vires).

Common-law cases are decisions of courts that develop and apply law where Parliament has not legislated or where statutes require interpretation. The doctrine of stare decisis ("to stand by what is decided") binds courts to follow earlier decisions of the same or higher courts in the same jurisdiction. The Supreme Court of Canada can, but rarely does, depart from its own past decisions (most recently in R v Henry [2005] and Carter v Canada [2015]). Stare decisis produces stability; cautious departure produces evolution. Indigenous legal traditions — distinct legal orders developed by Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee, Cree, Inuit, Mi'kmaq, Coast Salish, Métis, and many other nations over thousands of years — are increasingly recognised as a source of Canadian law, not merely a subject for it. The Supreme Court's decisions in R v Sparrow [1990] 1 SCR 1075, R v Van der Peet [1996] 2 SCR 507, and Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia [2014] 2 SCR 257, alongside the federal United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (2021), commit Canadian law to ongoing reception of Indigenous legal orders. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 50 specifically calls for the establishment of Indigenous law institutes. Indigenous law is not a museum piece; it is an active source of legal authority that courts must give effect to.

  • Constitution — supreme law; everything else must conform (s. 52(1) Constitution Act, 1982).
  • Statute — Act of Parliament or a legislature.
  • Regulation — delegated legislation under statutory authority.
  • Common law — judge-made law derived from cases.
  • Civil Code of Quebec — codified private law in Quebec.
  • Indigenous legal traditions — distinct legal orders of Indigenous nations; an independent source of law.
  • Stare decisis — lower courts bound by higher courts' precedents.
  • Ultra vires — "beyond the powers"; an act exceeding granted authority is invalid.
  • Royal prerogative — residual Crown powers not displaced by statute.

Worked Example — Tracing Multiple Sources in One Problem

Problem: A First Nations community sets a community-protocol rule prohibiting commercial fishing on a traditional ground during the spawning season. A non-Indigenous commercial fisher ignores the rule and is charged under the federal Fisheries Act; the community simultaneously brings a court application asserting an Aboriginal right.

Sources engaged:

  1. Constitution Act, 1982, s. 35: recognises and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. Sparrow's three-part test (existing right, prima facie infringement, justification) applies.
  2. Federal statute: the Fisheries Act and its regulations purport to govern; their application is subject to s. 35.
  3. Common-law cases: R v Sparrow, R v Marshall, Mikisew Cree — these decisions structure the analysis.
  4. Indigenous legal tradition: the community-protocol rule itself is a source of law; courts are increasingly asked to give it weight as evidence of how the right has historically been exercised and is currently understood.
  5. UNDRIP Act (2021): federal statutory commitment to align Canadian law with UNDRIP, including Article 26 (rights to lands and resources).

A complete legal analysis works through each source and explains how they interact — which dominates, which informs interpretation, which is a treaty obligation Canada must implement.

  • Treating "common law" as the law of all of Canada — Quebec uses civil law for private matters.
  • Treating Indigenous legal traditions as historical artefact rather than a living source.
  • Forgetting that statutes can be invalid if unconstitutional or ultra vires.
  • Confusing the Constitution Act, 1867 with the Constitution Act, 1982 — they coexist and both remain in force.
  • Ignoring regulations — most actual rules people deal with (driving, building, food safety) are regulations, not statutes.

🌐 1.5 Comparative Legal Systems
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The world's legal systems can be grouped, very roughly, into five families: common law, civil law, religious-law systems, customary-law systems, and mixed systems. Each family has distinctive sources, methods, and concepts. Studying them comparatively does two useful things: it clarifies what is distinctive about Canadian law (which is itself mixed — common law in nine provinces and the territories, civil law in Quebec for private matters, with Indigenous legal traditions throughout), and it equips students to navigate the international and transnational legal questions that dominate Strands C, D, and E of this course.

The common-law tradition, descending from medieval English royal courts, is judge-led. Cases produce binding precedents, statutes are interpreted narrowly against the background of the common law, and procedure is typically adversarial. The lawyer's craft is case analysis — identifying the holding (ratio decidendi) of past decisions and distinguishing inapplicable ones. Common-law countries include the UK (except Scotland), the United States, Canada (outside Quebec for private law), Australia, New Zealand, India, Pakistan, Nigeria, and Ireland. The Canadian Supreme Court has repeatedly described Canada's common law as having developed "with sensitivity to Charter values" (R v Salituro [1991] 3 SCR 654), meaning judges shape common-law doctrine to align with constitutional rights.

The civil-law tradition, descending from Roman law via the medieval glossators and the Napoleonic codification of 1804, is code-led. Comprehensive codes are the primary source; cases interpret but do not bind; procedure is typically inquisitorial, with the judge actively investigating. Civil-law countries include France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Japan, much of Latin America, and Quebec (for private matters, under the Civil Code of Quebec, 1994). The conceptual organisation of a civil code — book of persons, book of family, book of property, book of obligations — gives the system its characteristic systematicity. Canadian law students studying Quebec private law work primarily from the Code.

Religious-law systems derive primary authority from religious sources. Sharia (Islamic law) is followed for personal-status matters in many Muslim-majority countries; halakha (Jewish law) governs religious-tribunal questions; canon law governs the internal life of the Catholic Church. Customary-law systems draw primary authority from long-standing practices of a community recognised as binding. Many African and Pacific states operate mixed systems where customary law governs alongside common or civil law for matters such as land tenure and family disputes. Mixed systems (South Africa, Scotland, Louisiana, Quebec, the Philippines) combine traditions in different proportions. Indigenous legal traditions in Canada are sometimes treated, comparatively, as customary law — though Indigenous scholars (Borrows, Napoleon) emphasise that they are full legal orders in their own right, not "custom" in the limited European sense.

  • Common law — judge-led; binding precedent; adversarial procedure.
  • Civil law — code-led; persuasive precedent; inquisitorial procedure.
  • Ratio decidendi — the legal reasoning that decided the case; binding part of a precedent.
  • Obiter dicta — judicial remarks beyond what was needed to decide; persuasive only.
  • Religious-law systems — primary authority from religious sources (sharia, halakha, canon law).
  • Customary-law systems — primary authority from long-standing community practice.
  • Mixed systems — combining two or more traditions (Quebec, South Africa, Scotland).
  • Codification — comprehensive statutory restatement of a body of law.

Worked Example — Same Problem, Two Systems

Scenario: A consumer in Ontario discovers a manufacturing defect in a refrigerator bought from a retailer six months ago. The same scenario plays out in Lyon, France.

Ontario (common law + statute): Begin with the contract (warranty terms); consult statute (Consumer Protection Act, 2002; Sale of Goods Act); then case law on implied conditions of merchantability and fitness for purpose; then the common law of negligence (manufacturer liability under Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, still binding common-law authority). Multiple sources, hierarchically arranged, working together.

Lyon (civil law): Open the Code civil at the relevant section on hidden defects (vices cachés, Article 1641–1649); apply the Code's framework directly. The lawyer cites the article number, not a case, as the primary authority. Lower-court decisions may illustrate the article's application but do not bind.

Same problem, same broad answer (consumer gets a remedy), strikingly different legal method. A graduate of CLN4U should be able to explain why the methods differ and how each remains internally rational.

  • Treating "civil law" as the same as "civil litigation" — civil-law tradition (a system) versus civil law as opposed to criminal law (a category).
  • Assuming common-law judges "make up" the law freely — stare decisis constrains them heavily.
  • Calling Indigenous legal traditions "customary law" in a way that demotes them — they are full legal orders.
  • Forgetting Quebec is bijural — common law for public matters, civil law for private.
  • Assuming religious-law systems are uniform — Sunni and Shia jurisprudence diverge, as do Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali, and Ja'fari schools within Sunni Islam.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "analyse the influence of different legal systems and traditions on Canadian law (common law, civil law, religious-law traditions, Indigenous legal traditions)."

📝 1.6 Legal Reasoning & the IRAC Method
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The single most important academic skill in this course is legal reasoning — applying a legal rule to a specific set of facts and producing a defensible conclusion. The standard method for organising legal reasoning is known by its acronym IRAC: Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. Every case brief, every essay paragraph, every moot submission can usefully follow this four-part move. The same architecture organises judicial decisions themselves, which is why reading appellate judgments is excellent practice for writing your own.

Issue is the legal question. A good issue statement is narrow and specific — not "did the accused commit the offence?" but "did the accused have the requisite mens rea for second-degree murder when he discharged the firearm?". Issue statements should usually take the form "whether X" and identify the specific element or doctrine in dispute. A common student error is to confuse the issue (legal) with the question of fact (whether the accused fired the gun). Facts may be hotly disputed, but at the trial appellate level the issue is the legal characterisation of facts found below.

Rule is the law that governs the issue. State the rule clearly and accurately, with the source (statute section, case citation, treaty article). For example: "Under section 229(a) of the Criminal Code, a person commits second-degree murder when he or she means to cause death, or means to cause bodily harm that the person knows is likely to cause death and is reckless whether death ensues or not (R v Cooper [1993] 1 SCR 146)." The rule statement is your authority; quote or paraphrase carefully, and cite. Where two rules conflict — statute vs common law, federal vs provincial, domestic vs international — name the conflict and explain how it is resolved.

Application is the analytical heart of the brief. Apply the rule to the facts, step by step, in the order the rule itself prescribes. If the rule has three elements, address each element in turn. Quote facts that satisfy or fail to satisfy each element. Anticipate counter-arguments — what would the other side say about a weak point? — and rebut them. This is where law students earn their marks: not in stating the rule, which most students can do, but in linking specific facts to specific elements with explicit reasoning. Vague application sentences ("the accused appears to have had the necessary mens rea") earn nothing; specific ones ("the accused's statement to his roommate 'I am going to kill him tonight,' uttered hours before the shooting, supports the inference that he meant to cause death") earn full marks. Conclusion directly answers the issue: "Therefore, the accused had the requisite mens rea for second-degree murder." Conclusions follow from the application; they are not a place to introduce new analysis.

  • IRAC — Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion.
  • Case brief — concise summary of a judicial decision: facts, issue, holding, reasoning, disposition.
  • Holding (ratio decidendi) — the legal rule the case stands for.
  • Disposition — what the court did (allowed appeal, dismissed claim).
  • Distinguishing — showing that a precedent does not govern because facts differ in legally significant ways.
  • Counter-argument — anticipated objection; strong writing rebuts before opponents raise it.
  • Burden of proof — which party must prove what, to what standard.
  • Standard of proof — beyond reasonable doubt (criminal); balance of probabilities (civil).

Worked Example — IRAC Applied to a Short Problem

Facts: Mira, a 17-year-old high-school student, takes a Polaroid photo of a classmate's wallet sitting unattended on a desk during gym class. She does not take the wallet. Police charge her with theft under section 322 of the Criminal Code.

Issue: Whether Mira committed the offence of theft under s. 322 by photographing, but not removing, the wallet.

Rule: Section 322(1) of the Criminal Code defines theft: "Everyone commits theft who fraudulently and without colour of right takes, or fraudulently and without colour of right converts to his use or to the use of another person, anything, whether animate or inanimate, with intent (a) to deprive, temporarily or absolutely, the owner of it…" The actus reus requires (i) taking or conversion (ii) of a thing (iii) without colour of right; the mens rea requires fraudulent intent and intent to deprive (R v Milne [1992] 1 SCR 697).

Application: Mira did not "take" the wallet — she did not move it or assume possession of it. She did not "convert" it to her use; photography of an object does not, in ordinary statutory meaning or in the case law, amount to conversion. The actus reus of taking is therefore not made out. Without the actus reus, the mens rea question does not arise. Even if a prosecutor argued metaphorically that Mira "took an image," s. 322 governs takings of "anything … whether animate or inanimate" — meaning tangible or intangible property; an image of property is not the property itself.

Conclusion: Mira did not commit theft under s. 322. The charge cannot be sustained on these facts. A more appropriate charge, if any, might lie under provincial offence legislation (mischief), but theft is incorrect.

  • Writing the conclusion before doing the application.
  • Quoting the rule but not parsing it into elements.
  • Application that just re-narrates the facts without connecting them to rule elements.
  • Ignoring counter-arguments — strong analysis names them and answers them.
  • Confusing burden of proof (who must prove) with standard of proof (how strongly).
