📜 2.1 The Constitution Act, 1867 — Federalism & the Division of Powers
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canadian constitutional law begins on 1 July 1867, when the British North America Act (since 1982 renamed the Constitution Act, 1867) created the Dominion of Canada by uniting the United Province of Canada (which became Ontario and Quebec), New Brunswick, and Nova Scotia into a federation. The framers — Sir John A. Macdonald, George-Étienne Cartier, George Brown, and the other Fathers of Confederation — designed a structure that combined British parliamentary supremacy with a strong central government and an explicit federal division of powers. Understanding this 1867 architecture is the necessary first step for understanding everything that came after, including the Charter.

The defining feature of Canadian federalism is the division of powers. Sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867 enumerate the heads of power assigned to Parliament (section 91) and the provincial legislatures (section 92). Section 91 gives Parliament authority over national matters: trade and commerce, criminal law, banking, bankruptcy, marriage and divorce, naturalisation, the postal service, defence, navigation, and "the regulation of trade and commerce" and "the peace, order, and good government of Canada" (POGG). Section 92 gives the provinces authority over local matters: property and civil rights in the province, the administration of justice (including the constitution of provincial courts), municipal institutions, education (section 93), hospitals, and "generally all matters of a merely local or private nature in the province." Section 92A (added in 1982) gives provinces authority over natural resources within their boundaries.

Constitutional disputes about division of powers are resolved by the courts. The leading method, traceable through more than a century of Privy Council and Supreme Court decisions, is the pith and substance doctrine: a law is characterised by its dominant purpose and effect, then matched to the appropriate head of power. A law about cigarette advertising might in pith and substance be about public health (federal POGG / criminal law power) or about advertising in the province (provincial property and civil rights). Because federal and provincial authorities sometimes overlap, courts also use the double-aspect doctrine (some subjects can be regulated by both levels), paramountcy (federal law prevails where there is operational conflict), and interjurisdictional immunity (one level cannot impair the core of the other's exclusive competence).

Two further architectural features deserve attention. First, Canada is a constitutional monarchy: the Crown remains the formal head of state, represented federally by the Governor General and in each province by a Lieutenant Governor. Second, Canada has responsible government: the executive (Cabinet) must hold the confidence of the elected lower house (House of Commons or provincial assembly). The 1867 Act establishes these institutions but, in classic British style, leaves much to convention — unwritten rules that, while not enforceable in court, are treated as binding by political actors. The Supreme Court's Reference re Resolution to Amend the Constitution [1981] 1 SCR 753 (the "Patriation Reference") explicitly recognised conventions as part of Canada's constitutional order, while distinguishing them from legal rules.

  • Constitution Act, 1867 — formerly the British North America Act; created the Dominion and the federal structure.
  • Federalism — division of powers between national and provincial governments.
  • Section 91 — Parliament's heads of power (trade and commerce, criminal law, POGG, etc.).
  • Section 92 — provincial heads of power (property and civil rights, administration of justice, local matters).
  • Pith and substance — characterise a law by its dominant purpose and effect.
  • Double-aspect — some subjects validly regulated at both levels.
  • Paramountcy — federal law prevails on operational conflict.
  • POGG — peace, order, and good government clause in s. 91 preamble.
  • Convention — unwritten constitutional rule, binding politically but not legally enforceable.
  • Responsible government — executive must hold confidence of the elected legislature.

Worked Example — Classifying a Hypothetical Law

Suppose Parliament passes a federal "Healthy Eating Act" prohibiting the sale of food products with trans-fat content above 2%, anywhere in Canada.

Step 1 — Pith and substance: What is the law really about? Reading the preamble and the operative provisions, its dominant purpose is public health (reducing cardiovascular disease). Its dominant effect is on food manufacturers and retailers.

Step 2 — Match to a head of power: Public health legislation could potentially fall under several heads. The federal criminal law power (s. 91(27)) is broad and includes prohibitions backed by penalties aimed at a public-health, safety, or moral purpose (RJR-MacDonald v Canada [1995] 3 SCR 199 on tobacco advertising). The federal trade and commerce power (s. 91(2)) supports laws regulating inter-provincial trade in goods. Provincial authority over public health within the province (s. 92(13) property and civil rights) could also apply if the legislation were provincial.

Step 3 — Conclusion: The federal Act is likely valid as a criminal-law-power exercise, on the RJR-MacDonald analogy. If a province enacted a similar statute, it would also likely be valid under property and civil rights, subject to paramountcy if the federal statute applied with operational conflict.

  • Treating ss. 91 and 92 as exhaustive — POGG and the residual clause have absorbed new subjects (aeronautics, telecommunications, environmental law).
  • Confusing federalism with separation of powers — Canada has both, but they answer different questions.
  • Asserting that the federal government "outranks" provinces — for matters within s. 92, provinces are sovereign within their sphere.
  • Forgetting that convention can be as binding politically as a written rule, even if unenforceable in court.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "explain the division of powers between federal and provincial governments (Constitution Act, 1867, ss. 91–92)."

⚖️ 2.2 The Constitution Act, 1982 — Patriation & the Amending Formula
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

From 1867 until 1982, the Canadian Constitution was technically a statute of the United Kingdom Parliament. The 1867 Act had been enacted at Westminster, and amendments to it required a UK statute (or, after the Statute of Westminster 1931, a joint Canada–UK process for fundamental amendments). Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau's government negotiated patriation in 1980–81: bringing the Constitution home to Canada by enacting a new constitutional document and a domestic amending formula. The result was the Constitution Act, 1982, enacted by the UK Parliament's Canada Act 1982 and proclaimed by Queen Elizabeth II in Ottawa on 17 April 1982. After that date, the UK Parliament has no further legislative authority over Canadian constitutional matters.

The 1982 Act added three monumental pieces to the constitutional architecture. First, the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (Part I, ss. 1–34), which constitutionalised a bill of rights binding on both federal and provincial governments. Second, the recognition of existing Aboriginal and treaty rights in section 35 (Part II). Third, an amending formula (Part V, ss. 38–49), allowing Canadians to amend their own Constitution domestically through a structured process involving Parliament and the provinces.

The amending formula is not a single procedure but a graduated set. Section 38 (general formula): most amendments require resolutions of the Senate, the House of Commons, and legislative assemblies of at least two-thirds of the provinces (that is, seven) containing at least 50% of the population — the "7/50" formula. Section 41 (unanimity): amendments to the Office of the Queen, the Governor General, Lieutenant Governors, the right to use English or French as enumerated, the composition of the Supreme Court, and the amending formula itself require resolutions of Parliament and every provincial legislature. Section 42 specifies items covered by the general formula but not by the opt-out provision. Section 43 allows bilateral amendments involving Parliament plus the affected province (used for the 1997 amendment to s. 93 to abolish denominational school rights in Quebec). Section 44 permits Parliament alone to amend matters within federal authority; section 45 permits a province alone to amend its provincial constitution.

The patriation process was politically and legally fraught. Quebec did not consent to the 1982 Act, and the patriation has been a source of continuing constitutional grievance there. The Supreme Court's Reference re Resolution to Amend the Constitution [1981] 1 SCR 753 ("Patriation Reference") and Reference re Objection by Quebec to a Resolution to Amend the Constitution [1982] 2 SCR 793 held that the federal government had legal authority to proceed to Westminster without unanimous provincial consent, but that doing so without substantial provincial consent would violate constitutional convention. Two later attempts at amendment — the Meech Lake Accord (1987–90), which would have recognised Quebec as a "distinct society," and the Charlottetown Accord (1992), a broader constitutional reform package — both failed. Constitutional amendment in Canada has therefore been extraordinarily difficult: only a handful of minor amendments have been adopted since 1982, none of them touching the contested questions of Quebec's status or Senate reform.

  • Patriation — bringing constitutional amendment authority from the UK to Canada (1982).
  • Constitution Act, 1982 — the statute that did the patriating; added the Charter and s. 35.
  • Canada Act 1982 (UK) — the parallel UK statute that gave the 1982 Act effect.
  • Section 38 (7/50 formula) — general amending procedure: 7 provinces with 50% of population.
  • Section 41 (unanimity) — Parliament + every province for the most fundamental items.
  • Section 33 (notwithstanding clause) — permits temporary legislative override of certain Charter sections.
  • Section 35 — recognises and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights.
  • Patriation Reference (1981) — court held federal authority existed but convention required substantial provincial consent.
  • Meech Lake (1987–90), Charlottetown (1992) — failed post-patriation constitutional packages.

Worked Example — Choosing the Right Amending Procedure

Suppose Parliament wishes to add a new clause to the Charter recognising a right to a healthy environment, binding on all governments.

Step 1 — Which formula applies? The amendment changes the Charter and therefore alters the powers of the legislature and the executive vis-à-vis individuals. This is not one of the s. 41 unanimity items. It is not a Parliament-alone matter under s. 44, because it binds the provinces. It is not bilateral under s. 43.

Step 2 — The 7/50 general formula (s. 38). Requires resolutions of the Senate, the House of Commons, and legislative assemblies of at least two-thirds of the provinces containing at least 50% of the population.

Step 3 — Procedure. The amendment can be initiated in either federal House or in any provincial legislature. After authorisation by Parliament and the required provinces, the Governor General issues a proclamation. Section 38(3) opt-out: if the amendment derogates from a provincial power or privilege, dissenting provinces may opt out and be entitled to compensation (s. 40) in matters of education or other cultural areas. Practically, such an amendment requires very broad political agreement and is unlikely without significant pre-amendment negotiation.

  • Saying "the Queen patriated the Constitution" — patriation was Canadian initiative; the Queen proclaimed.
  • Treating s. 33 as part of the amending formula — it is a Charter override, not an amendment.
  • Confusing constitutional amendment with statutory enactment — amendments must satisfy Part V; statutes only require ordinary legislation.
  • Forgetting Quebec's non-consent in 1982 — legally valid, politically and constitutionally contested.

🗽 2.3 The Charter of Rights and Freedoms — Structure & Content
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, enacted as Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982 (sections 1–34), is the constitutional charter of rights binding on every level of government in Canada. It applies, by section 32, to "the Parliament and government of Canada" and "the legislature and government of each province" — but not, in itself, to private actors. The Charter has reshaped Canadian society more than any other single legal document since Confederation, restructuring criminal procedure, equality law, language rights, freedom of expression, and the relationship between courts and legislatures.

The Charter is organised into a sequence of rights categories. Section 1 is the limitations clause (treated in detail in topic 2.4). Section 2 protects fundamental freedoms: (a) conscience and religion, (b) thought, belief, opinion, and expression (including freedom of the press), (c) peaceful assembly, and (d) association. Sections 3–5 protect democratic rights: every citizen has the right to vote and stand for office (s. 3); the maximum legislative term is five years (s. 4); annual sittings of legislatures are required (s. 5). Section 6 protects mobility rights: the right to enter, remain in, and leave Canada, and to pursue a livelihood in any province (subject to provincial residency-affirmative-action programs).

Sections 7–14 protect legal rights, the most extensively litigated part of the Charter: s. 7 (life, liberty, security of the person; principles of fundamental justice — the source of most criminal-law constitutional litigation, including Reference re BC Motor Vehicle Act, Suresh v Canada, Bedford, and Carter v Canada); s. 8 (unreasonable search and seizure — Hunter v Southam [1984]); s. 9 (arbitrary detention); s. 10 (rights on arrest or detention, including counsel); s. 11 (rights in criminal and penal proceedings, including presumption of innocence, trial within a reasonable time — R v Jordan [2016]); s. 12 (cruel and unusual treatment or punishment); s. 13 (self-incrimination); s. 14 (interpreter).

Section 15 protects equality rights: every individual is equal before and under the law, with equal protection and benefit, without discrimination based on enumerated grounds (race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, mental or physical disability) or analogous grounds the courts have recognised (sexual orientation in Egan v Canada [1995], marital status, citizenship, off-reserve Aboriginal status). The Andrews v Law Society of BC [1989] test, refined in R v Kapp [2008] and Withler v Canada [2011], asks: (1) does the law create a distinction based on an enumerated or analogous ground? (2) does the distinction create a disadvantage by perpetuating prejudice or stereotyping? Section 15(2) explicitly protects affirmative-action programs. Sections 16–22 protect language rights (official-language status for English and French). Sections 23–24 protect minority-language education rights and provide remedies for Charter violations (s. 24(1) discretionary remedies, s. 24(2) exclusion of evidence).

  • Section 1 — reasonable limits, prescribed by law, demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society.
  • Section 2 — fundamental freedoms (religion, expression, assembly, association).
  • Section 7 — life, liberty, security of the person; principles of fundamental justice.
  • Section 8 — protection against unreasonable search and seizure.
  • Section 11 — rights in criminal proceedings (presumption of innocence, trial within reasonable time).
  • Section 15 — equality without discrimination on enumerated or analogous grounds.
  • Section 24 — remedies for Charter breaches (s. 24(1) appropriate and just; s. 24(2) exclude evidence if admission would bring administration of justice into disrepute).
  • Section 32 — Charter binds federal and provincial governments, not private actors.
  • Section 52(1) Constitution Act, 1982 — supremacy clause; courts may strike down inconsistent laws.

Worked Example — Reading a Charter Provision Carefully

Section 11(b): "Any person charged with an offence has the right … to be tried within a reasonable time."

Five questions a Grade 12 student should ask of this provision:

  1. Who? "Any person charged with an offence" — not just citizens, includes non-citizens; "charged" begins on laying of an information.
  2. What right? "Tried within a reasonable time" — both pretrial and the trial itself.
  3. Test? Set in R v Jordan [2016] 1 SCR 631: 18 months from charge to end of trial in provincial court; 30 months in superior court, subject to defence-caused delay being subtracted and exceptional circumstances.
  4. Remedy? Section 24(1) — appropriate and just; for s. 11(b) breaches, the standard remedy is a stay of proceedings (the charge is permanently halted).
  5. Limits? Section 1 — could the Crown justify delay as a "reasonable limit"? In practice, Jordan ceilings absorb the s. 1 analysis; only the most exceptional circumstances apply.

This is the structure of every Charter analysis: identify the right, identify the test, identify the breach, identify the remedy, consider section 1.

  • Saying "the Charter protects me from my employer" — generally false; s. 32 limits the Charter to government action (though human-rights statutes fill the gap for private discrimination).
  • Calling section 1 "the override" — it is the limitations clause; s. 33 is the override.
  • Treating s. 7 as a right to anything one wants — it protects against state deprivation of life/liberty/security in a way contrary to fundamental justice.
  • Forgetting that "equal" in s. 15 is substantive, not merely formal — different treatment may be required to achieve equality.

📐 2.4 Section 1 and the Oakes Test
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Section 1 of the Charter is the limitations clause: "The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees the rights and freedoms set out in it subject only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society." This single sentence is the most-cited provision in Canadian constitutional jurisprudence. It tells courts that Charter rights are not absolute — they can be limited, but only when the government meets the demanding burden of demonstrating that the limit is reasonable.

The Supreme Court structured the section 1 analysis in R v Oakes [1986] 1 SCR 103. David Edwin Oakes had been convicted under section 8 of the federal Narcotic Control Act, which created a reverse onus: once the Crown proved possession of a narcotic, the accused had to prove on a balance of probabilities that he was not in possession for the purpose of trafficking. Oakes argued this reverse onus violated his s. 11(d) right to be presumed innocent. The Crown conceded the prima facie violation but argued it was justified under s. 1. The Supreme Court agreed there was a violation and developed a two-stage test that became known as the Oakes test.

Stage one asks whether the limit is prescribed by law: is there a statute, regulation, or common-law rule that explicitly or by necessary implication authorises the limit, and is it accessible and clear enough that citizens can know what is prohibited? Stage two asks whether the limit is demonstrably justified, and breaks into four sub-elements: (i) pressing and substantial objective — does the limit serve a goal of sufficient importance to override a Charter right? (ii) rational connection — is the means rationally connected to the objective? (iii) minimal impairment — does the means impair the right as little as reasonably possible to achieve the objective? (iv) proportionality of effects — are the deleterious effects on the right proportionate to the salutary effects of the law?

In Oakes itself, the Court held that the prima facie objective of curbing drug trafficking was pressing and substantial, but the reverse onus failed the rational-connection branch: a person could be in possession of a tiny amount of a narcotic, far too small to support an inference of trafficking. The statute was struck down. In subsequent cases, the Court has applied the Oakes test with varying degrees of strictness — strict scrutiny for laws restricting political expression, more deferential review for laws regulating socio-economic policy (Irwin Toy v Quebec [1989] 1 SCR 927). The minimal-impairment branch is the most frequently litigated and the most decisive in modern cases. A government that cannot show its scheme is reasonably tailored to its objective rarely survives section 1.

  • Section 1 — "reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society."
  • Oakes test — two-stage framework for s. 1 analysis from R v Oakes [1986].
  • Prescribed by law — limit must originate in statute, regulation, or common-law rule, accessibly stated.
  • Pressing and substantial objective — the goal must be important enough to justify limiting a right.
  • Rational connection — means must be rationally linked to the objective.
  • Minimal impairment — means must impair the right as little as reasonably possible.
  • Proportionality of effects — salutary effects must outweigh deleterious effects.
  • Burden of proof — under s. 1, the government bears the burden, on a balance of probabilities.
  • Standard of justification — varies with the context (strict for political speech; deferential for socio-economic regulation).

Worked Example — Applying the Oakes Test to a Hypothetical

Hypothetical statute: A provincial law prohibits all political advertising during the 28 days before a provincial election, except by registered political parties.

Step 1 — Identify the right limited: Section 2(b) freedom of expression. The law restricts political advertising, which is core political expression entitled to the strongest constitutional protection.

Step 2 — Section 1 analysis:

  1. Prescribed by law: Yes — a clear statutory provision.
  2. Pressing and substantial objective: The government argues the law promotes electoral fairness by preventing wealthy interests from dominating debate. This is pressing and substantial (Harper v Canada (AG) [2004] 1 SCR 827 — election spending limits are valid objectives).
  3. Rational connection: Limiting advertising can plausibly limit the influence of wealthy individuals; rationally connected.
  4. Minimal impairment: Is a complete ban on all political advertising by non-parties reasonably tailored? A ban with a financial cap might achieve much of the same objective with less impairment of expression. Harper upheld a spending-cap regime, not a total ban. A total ban probably fails minimal impairment.
  5. Proportionality of effects: The salutary effect (fairness) does not outweigh the deleterious effect (silencing entire categories of political speech in the most critical period).

Conclusion: The total ban likely fails the Oakes test at the minimal-impairment stage and would be struck down. A spending cap, however, would likely survive scrutiny.

  • Treating s. 1 as a "loophole" — it is the structured way Canada permits limits on rights.
  • Forgetting which party bears the burden — government bears it under s. 1.
  • Skipping the "prescribed by law" stage — limits imposed by police discretion alone fail at this gate.
  • Treating "minimal impairment" as "the very least impairment possible" — the standard is "as little as reasonably possible," not perfection.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "explain the application of the section 1 Oakes test to limits on Charter rights."

⚠️ 2.5 Section 33 — The Notwithstanding Clause
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Of all the provisions of the Charter, section 33 — the notwithstanding clause — is the most controversial and the most distinctively Canadian. It permits Parliament or a provincial legislature to enact legislation that operates "notwithstanding" a provision of section 2 or sections 7 to 15 of the Charter. The effect is to immunise such legislation from judicial invalidation on the named Charter grounds for up to five years, after which the override expires unless re-enacted. The notwithstanding clause does not apply to democratic rights (ss. 3–5), mobility rights (s. 6), language rights (ss. 16–23), or section 28 (gender equality).

The clause was the political price of the Charter. Several provincial premiers — particularly Allan Blakeney of Saskatchewan and Sterling Lyon of Manitoba — were unwilling to subordinate parliamentary sovereignty to unelected judges. The "kitchen accord" of 5 November 1981 produced the notwithstanding clause as a compromise: rights would be entrenched, judicial review would be available, but legislatures could, in exceptional cases and through a deliberate political act, override certain rights temporarily. The clause is therefore a structural device for managing the tension between parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy.

For decades after 1982, section 33 was rarely used. Quebec invoked it pre-emptively for all its statutes in 1982 as a protest against patriation; Saskatchewan used it for back-to-work legislation; Yukon used it briefly. The federal government has never used it. Use of s. 33 declined further after the Charter's social acceptance grew. Since 2017, however, several provinces have invoked s. 33 with increasing willingness: Quebec for Bill 21 (religious symbols, 2019) and Bill 96 (language, 2022); Saskatchewan for Bill 137 (pronouns in schools, 2023); Ontario tried to use it for back-to-work legislation against CUPE education workers in 2022, then withdrew under pressure. The reasonable expectation that s. 33 would be a rare emergency tool has given way to debate about whether s. 33 risks becoming a routine vehicle for legislative override of judicial Charter rulings.

Two issues structure current debates. First, the timing question: must a legislature wait for a court to find a Charter breach before invoking s. 33, or may it invoke s. 33 pre-emptively to insulate legislation from judicial review at the outset? Quebec's Bill 21 was pre-emptive; the Supreme Court has not yet ruled definitively on whether pre-emptive use is constitutionally proper, though most scholars argue it is permitted by the text. Second, the scope question: does s. 33 protect only against judicial declarations of invalidity, or does it also block remedies (such as damages under s. 24(1)) for breaches that occurred? In Working Families Coalition (Canada) Inc v Ontario [2025] (ONCA), the Ontario Court of Appeal explored this. The five-year sunset (s. 33(3)) gives the public a periodic political check; the requirement for explicit invocation (s. 33(2)) keeps the use visible. These are deliberate structural features.

  • Section 33 — notwithstanding clause; legislature can override ss. 2 and 7–15 of the Charter.
  • Five-year sunset — overrides expire automatically after five years unless re-enacted.
  • Explicit invocation — legislation must expressly declare it operates notwithstanding.
  • Cannot override — ss. 3–6 (democratic, mobility), 16–23 (language), 28 (sex equality), 35 (Aboriginal rights).
  • Kitchen accord (1981) — political compromise that produced s. 33.
  • Bill 21 (Quebec, 2019) — religious-symbols ban; invoked s. 33 pre-emptively.
  • Bill 96 (Quebec, 2022) — French-language law; invoked s. 33.
  • Pre-emptive use — invoking s. 33 before any court ruling; legality unsettled but textually permitted.
  • Parliamentary sovereignty — the British constitutional principle the notwithstanding clause partly preserves.

Worked Example — Whether a Statute Validly Invokes s. 33

Suppose Manitoba enacts a statute prohibiting public-sector strikes in essential services, and section 12 of the statute states: "This Act shall operate notwithstanding sections 2(d) and 15 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms."

Check 1 — Explicit declaration (s. 33(2)): Yes, the section identifies the Act and the specific Charter sections being overridden. This complies with the requirement of explicit invocation (Ford v Quebec [1988] 2 SCR 712, which held a general "all rights" invocation was sufficient, though that view has been criticised).

Check 2 — Permitted sections? Yes — s. 2(d) (freedom of association) and s. 15 (equality) are both within ss. 2 and 7–15, the overridable range.

Check 3 — Five-year sunset (s. 33(3)): The override will expire five years from enactment unless the legislature re-enacts it. The statute does not say so, but the sunset operates automatically.

Check 4 — Other Charter provisions still apply. The statute is not immunised from review under ss. 3–6 (democratic / mobility) or ss. 16–23 (language) or s. 28 (sex equality). A Charter challenge based on s. 28 could still proceed.

The statute validly invokes s. 33. Whether it is good policy is a political question; whether it is constitutional in s. 33 terms, yes.

  • Believing s. 33 lets a government do anything — many Charter sections are immune (3–6, 16–23, 28).
  • Confusing s. 33 with s. 1 — s. 1 is a justified limitation, decided by courts; s. 33 is an override, decided by legislatures.
  • Forgetting the five-year sunset — the override is not permanent.
  • Believing s. 33 cannot be challenged at all — challenges to whether s. 33 was properly invoked, or to provisions outside its scope, remain open.

🪶 2.6 Section 35 — Aboriginal & Treaty Rights
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 is outside the Charter but is the constitutional foundation of Indigenous rights in Canadian law. It states: "(1) The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed. (2) In this Act, 'aboriginal peoples of Canada' includes the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada." Section 35 is constitutional but is not subject to section 1 (no Oakes-style "reasonable limits" justification by the government) and is not overridable by section 33. Limits on s. 35 rights require government to meet a different, sometimes more demanding, justification framework.

The Supreme Court worked out the analytical structure of s. 35 over a series of cases. In R v Sparrow [1990] 1 SCR 1075, the Court held that "existing" Aboriginal rights are those not extinguished prior to 1982 (extinguishment requires clear and plain intention). The Court set out a three-part test: (1) does the claimant have an existing Aboriginal right? (2) does the government action prima facie infringe it? (3) is the infringement justified? Justification requires a valid legislative objective and conduct consistent with the Crown's fiduciary duty to Aboriginal peoples (priority to Aboriginal users in resource allocation, minimal impairment, consultation, compensation where appropriate).

In R v Van der Peet [1996] 2 SCR 507, the Court refined the definition of an Aboriginal right: it is a practice, custom, or tradition integral to the distinctive culture of the Aboriginal group, exercised at the time of European contact. The "integral to a distinctive culture" test has been criticised (notably by McLachlin CJ in dissent) for freezing rights at the moment of contact, but it remains the leading test. R v Marshall; R v Bernard [2005] 2 SCR 220 applied a similar framework to treaty interpretation, emphasising that treaty rights must be read generously, with ambiguities resolved in favour of the Indigenous signatories. The Court reaffirmed Aboriginal title as a sui generis right to the land itself in Delgamuukw v British Columbia [1997] 3 SCR 1010 and recognised it on the ground for the first time in Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia [2014] 2 SCR 257. Aboriginal title is communal, inalienable except to the Crown, and includes the right to decide how the land is used.

The duty to consult, articulated in Haida Nation v British Columbia (Minister of Forests) [2004] 3 SCR 511, requires the Crown to consult Indigenous groups whose asserted (not yet proven) rights may be adversely affected by Crown decisions. The depth of consultation varies with the strength of the asserted right and the gravity of the impact. In Mikisew Cree First Nation v Canada [2005] 3 SCR 388, the Court held that the duty extends to taking-up of treaty land for development. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action (2015) and the federal United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (2021) commit Canada to align federal law with UNDRIP, including its requirements for free, prior, and informed consent. The constitutional law on Indigenous rights is the area of Canadian law currently in the most rapid doctrinal evolution.

  • Section 35 — recognises and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights.
  • Sparrow test — (1) existing right (2) prima facie infringement (3) justification.
  • Van der Peet test — integral to a distinctive culture at the time of contact.
  • Aboriginal title — sui generis communal right to the land itself; Delgamuukw, Tsilhqot'in.
  • Treaty rights — rights arising from historic and modern treaties; interpreted generously.
  • Duty to consult — Crown's obligation to consult Indigenous peoples whose rights may be affected (Haida Nation).
  • Honour of the Crown — overarching principle governing Crown–Indigenous relations.
  • UNDRIP / UNDRIP Act — UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; Canadian implementing statute (2021).
  • Free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) — UNDRIP standard for measures affecting Indigenous peoples.

Worked Example — Sparrow Applied to a Modern Problem

Scenario: Sarah Sparrow, a member of the Musqueam Indian Band (the actual claimant in the case), is charged under the federal Fisheries Act with using a drift net longer than permitted under her band's food-fishing licence. She defends on the basis of an Aboriginal right.

Step 1 — Does an existing Aboriginal right exist? The Musqueam have a historic right to fish for food, ceremonial, and social purposes. The right existed at contact and was not extinguished prior to 1982 (extinguishment requires clear and plain intention; mere regulation is not extinguishment). The right exists.

Step 2 — Does the regulation infringe the right? The net-length restriction limits the exercise of the fishing right. The infringement is prima facie established (it imposes a "meaningful diminution" of the right).

Step 3 — Is the infringement justified? The Crown must show a valid objective (conservation is valid) and conduct consistent with fiduciary duty. After conservation needs are met, Aboriginal food fishing must have priority over other users. If the regulation prioritises commercial or sport fishing over Aboriginal food fishing, justification fails.

Result in the actual case: The Supreme Court held that the regulation could not be justified on the record before it and remitted the matter for retrial. Sparrow established the s. 35 analytical framework still used today.

  • Treating s. 35 as part of the Charter — it is not; it is in Part II of the Constitution Act, 1982.
  • Forgetting that s. 35 is not subject to s. 1 — justification is a separate framework.
  • Treating "existing" as meaning "exercised continuously up to 1982" — it means "not extinguished prior to 1982."
  • Confusing Aboriginal title with Aboriginal rights — title is to land itself; rights are to specific practices.
  • Forgetting Métis and Inuit — s. 35(2) explicitly includes them.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.4 — "analyse Aboriginal and treaty rights protected by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982."
