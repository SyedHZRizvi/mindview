🌍 3.1 What is International Law? Sources and Subjects
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

International law is the body of rules and principles governing the conduct of states and other subjects of international law in their relations with one another. The classical definition — restated in countless textbooks since the 19th century — captures three crucial features. First, the subjects of international law are primarily states, but increasingly include international organisations (the UN itself, the WTO, the EU), individuals (for human-rights and international-criminal-law purposes), corporations (in investment arbitration), and Indigenous peoples (under UNDRIP). Second, international law is created by its subjects, principally states, rather than imposed by a superior authority — this consensual quality marks it as different from domestic law. Third, enforcement is decentralised: international law lacks a single police force or compulsory court, relying instead on diplomatic, reputational, economic, and occasionally military mechanisms.

The classic enumeration of sources is Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice (1945), which directs the Court to apply: (a) international conventions ("treaties"), whether general or particular; (b) international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law; (c) the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations; (d) judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists, as subsidiary means. The first three are primary; the fourth is interpretive. Modern lists usually add (e) acts of international organisations and (f) "soft law" (declarations, codes of conduct) as non-binding but influential.

The relationship among sources matters. Treaties are written agreements with binding force on the parties (pacta sunt servanda — Article 26 VCLT). Custom binds all states (except persistent objectors) and is composed of two elements: state practice (what states actually do) and opinio juris (the belief that such practice is legally required). The combination is what distinguishes custom from mere habit. The ICJ's North Sea Continental Shelf cases [1969] ICJ Reports 3 set out the analytical framework: practice must be widespread, representative, and consistent; opinio juris must be inferred from statements, votes, and conduct. Modern controversies include whether some practices have crystallised into custom (cyber operations, drone strikes, climate-change emission reductions) and how quickly custom can form (the ICJ has accepted that custom can form rapidly in some areas).

General principles of law — Article 38(1)(c) — fill gaps where treaty and custom run out. They include doctrines so widely accepted in domestic legal systems that they can be transposed to the international plane: estoppel, res judicata, good faith, proportionality, due process. The ICJ used a general principle in Corfu Channel [1949] ICJ Reports 4 to hold Albania liable for damage to British warships from mines in Albanian waters, drawing on the general principle that a state must not knowingly allow its territory to be used contrary to the rights of other states. The doctrine of jus cogens (peremptory norms) names a small set of rules from which no derogation is permitted — the prohibitions on genocide, slavery, torture, aggression, and apartheid. A treaty conflicting with jus cogens is void (Article 53 VCLT). The concept is controversial in detail but indispensable in defining the moral floor of international law.

  • Article 38 ICJ Statute — the canonical enumeration of sources.
  • Treaty — written agreement between states governed by international law (Article 2 VCLT).
  • Custom — state practice + opinio juris.
  • Opinio juris — belief that a practice is required by law (not merely habit).
  • Persistent objector — state that consistently and publicly rejects an emerging custom; not bound.
  • General principles of law — doctrines common to major domestic systems, transposed to international level.
  • Jus cogens — peremptory norm from which no derogation is permitted (genocide, slavery, torture).
  • Soft law — non-binding instruments (declarations, codes) that nonetheless shape conduct.
  • Pacta sunt servanda — agreements must be kept (VCLT Article 26).

Worked Example — Identifying Sources for a Single Question

Question: Is a state legally permitted to use force to rescue its nationals abroad?

Treaty: The UN Charter Article 2(4) prohibits the use of force; Article 51 permits self-defence in response to an "armed attack." Neither expressly addresses nationals abroad.

Custom: State practice is mixed. The Israeli rescue at Entebbe (1976) and U.S. operations in Iran (1980) were defended on this basis; many states criticised them. Opinio juris is contested.

General principles: Necessity and proportionality, recognised in domestic criminal-defence law, are also general principles in international law.

Subsidiary means: Scholars are divided. The Institute of International Law's 1989 Santiago resolution rejected the doctrine in most circumstances. Some commentators accept a narrow customary right where the host state cannot or will not protect; others reject it.

Conclusion: The legal answer is unsettled. A serious analysis must canvass all sources, identify the strongest authority for each side, and explain how the practitioner's conclusion follows. International-law analysis is rarely a simple lookup.

  • Treating international law as "not really law" because it lacks a single enforcer — many legal systems enforce decentrally.
  • Ignoring custom because it is unwritten — custom binds all states.
  • Confusing soft law with hard law — declarations and resolutions are not, in themselves, binding.
  • Asserting jus cogens to expand obligations — the category is genuinely narrow.

🏛️ 3.2 State Sovereignty, Jurisdiction, and Recognition
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The state is the principal subject of international law. The modern conception of statehood derives from the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended Europe's Wars of Religion and established the principle that each sovereign has exclusive authority within its own territory and that no external authority may intervene in its internal affairs. The Westphalian system — secularised, territorialised, and centralised — remains the foundation of contemporary international law, though it has been substantially modified by human-rights law, the law of armed conflict, and the development of international institutions.

The four criteria of statehood, codified in Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933), are: (a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) a government; and (d) capacity to enter into relations with other states. These criteria are widely treated as customary. They do not require formal recognition by other states — under the declaratory theory, statehood is a question of fact; under the constitutive theory, recognition is required. Most modern lawyers and the ICJ favour the declaratory view, though political reality means recognition matters enormously for diplomatic, treaty, and enforcement purposes.

Sovereignty has two faces. Internal sovereignty is the state's supreme authority within its territory: the power to legislate, adjudicate, tax, and use force. External sovereignty is the state's freedom from outside interference: territorial integrity, political independence, and equal standing in international relations. These are protected by the UN Charter's Article 2(1) ("sovereign equality of all its Members") and Article 2(4) (prohibition on the use of force against territorial integrity or political independence) and Article 2(7) (non-intervention in matters within domestic jurisdiction). Sovereignty is not absolute; it is conditioned by international obligations a state has voluntarily accepted (treaties, custom binding it).

Jurisdiction is the legal authority to apply law. International law recognises several heads of jurisdiction. Territoriality — the state has jurisdiction over conduct within its territory (the strongest claim). Nationality — over conduct of its nationals abroad. Passive personality — over conduct against its nationals abroad (more controversial). Protective principle — over conduct abroad threatening the state's security or vital functions. Universal jurisdiction — over a small set of crimes (piracy, slavery, genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, torture) regardless of where committed or by whom, because the crimes are of universal concern. Universal jurisdiction was used by Spain in the Pinochet litigation and by Belgium in the early 2000s; Canada used it under the Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act (2000) in R v Désiré Munyaneza (2009), the first Canadian universal-jurisdiction prosecution for genocide and crimes against humanity committed in Rwanda. Multiple states can have concurrent jurisdiction; conflicts are resolved by extradition treaties, comity, and prudential rules of decline (forum non conveniens).

  • Westphalian system — territorial sovereign equality (1648 origin).
  • Montevideo Convention (1933) — four criteria of statehood.
  • Declaratory theory of recognition — statehood is a fact; recognition is acknowledgment.
  • Constitutive theory of recognition — recognition is required for full statehood (minority view).
  • Sovereign equality — UN Charter Article 2(1); all states are formally equal.
  • Non-intervention — UN Charter Article 2(7).
  • Territorial jurisdiction — over conduct within the state's territory.
  • Nationality jurisdiction — over the state's own nationals.
  • Universal jurisdiction — over a small set of grave crimes regardless of location.
  • Sovereign immunity — a state cannot generally be sued in another state's courts (with exceptions for commercial activity).

Worked Example — Concurrent Jurisdiction Over a Single Crime

Scenario: A Canadian dual citizen, residing in France, embezzles funds from a German company doing business in Brazil. The funds are deposited in a Cayman Islands bank.

Which states can claim jurisdiction?

  1. France: Territoriality — conduct occurred in French territory.
  2. Canada: Nationality — over its citizen abroad.
  3. Germany: Passive personality / nationality of the corporate victim — controversial but plausible.
  4. Brazil: Territoriality, if the embezzlement affected operations in Brazil; possibly effects-based jurisdiction.
  5. Cayman Islands (and via the UK): Banking jurisdiction over funds within its territory; possible money-laundering offences.

Concurrent jurisdiction is the rule, not the exception. Resolution turns on extradition treaties, prosecution priority, double-jeopardy considerations (the rule against being prosecuted twice for the same conduct under ne bis in idem), and prudential factors. A practitioner identifies all heads of jurisdiction, then narrows by treaty and practice.

  • Confusing sovereignty with non-interference — sovereignty allows voluntary acceptance of obligations.
  • Treating recognition as constitutive — most modern lawyers treat it as declaratory.
  • Forgetting that universal jurisdiction is narrowly defined — only specific crimes qualify.
  • Asserting jurisdiction without identifying the basis — practitioners must name the head.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "explain the concepts of state sovereignty, jurisdiction, and immunity."

📜 3.3 The Law of Treaties — VCLT 1969
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Treaties are the most familiar and most important source of modern international law. The legal regime governing how treaties are formed, interpreted, modified, and terminated is set out in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), opened for signature in Vienna on 23 May 1969 and in force from 27 January 1980. The VCLT codifies the customary law of treaties and supplements it with new rules. Canada acceded to the VCLT in 1970. The Convention applies directly only to treaties between states, but its principles have been extended by analogy (and by the VCLT 1986) to treaties involving international organisations.

Definition (Article 2): A treaty is "an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation." Names vary: convention, charter, covenant, agreement, protocol, statute, exchange of notes — what matters is legal effect, not nomenclature. The UN Charter, the Geneva Conventions, NAFTA / USMCA, the Rome Statute, the Paris Agreement on climate change, and Canadian bilateral tax treaties are all treaties in the VCLT sense.

Formation proceeds through identifiable stages. Negotiation produces a text. Adoption (Article 9) is the formal act that fixes the text. Signature indicates a state's intention to become bound but does not bind. Ratification, acceptance, approval, or accession (Articles 11–16) are the acts of formal consent. The treaty enters into force as the treaty itself provides (Article 24); commonly after a specified number of ratifications. Reservations (Articles 19–23) allow states to exclude or modify the effect of certain provisions when expressing consent to be bound — but only if reservations are not prohibited by the treaty itself and not incompatible with the treaty's object and purpose.

Interpretation is governed by Articles 31–33 of the VCLT, which set out a three-part method that should be addressed to before any other approach. Article 31(1): "A treaty shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose." This three-element formula — ordinary meaning, context, object and purpose — has been quoted by tribunals more often than perhaps any other treaty provision. Article 32 permits recourse to supplementary means (preparatory work, circumstances of conclusion) in two cases: to confirm the meaning derived from Article 31, or to determine meaning when Article 31 leaves it ambiguous or leads to a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result. Termination (Articles 54–64) can occur by consent, breach, supervening impossibility of performance, fundamental change of circumstances (rebus sic stantibus), or emergence of a new peremptory norm. The high bar for unilateral withdrawal preserves treaty stability.

  • VCLT 1969 — Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties; codifies customary treaty law.
  • Treaty (Article 2) — written international agreement between states governed by international law.
  • Signature — preliminary consent; does not bind.
  • Ratification — definitive consent to be bound.
  • Reservation — unilateral statement excluding or modifying effect of a provision.
  • Pacta sunt servanda (Article 26) — agreements must be performed in good faith.
  • Article 31 interpretation — ordinary meaning + context + object and purpose.
  • Article 32 supplementary means — preparatory work to confirm or resolve ambiguity.
  • Rebus sic stantibus — fundamental change of circumstances as ground for termination (narrowly construed).
  • Jus cogens conflict — treaty conflicting with peremptory norm is void (Article 53).

Worked Example — Treaty Interpretation Under Article 31

Suppose State A and State B are parties to a treaty that provides: "Each Party shall ensure that its commercial fisheries do not deplete shared fish stocks below sustainable levels."

State A reduces its catch by 10% but its fleet still appears to be contributing to depletion. State B claims breach. State A says it has done all that the treaty requires.

Step 1 — Ordinary meaning: "Ensure" is stronger than "endeavour" or "use best efforts." "Shall ensure" imposes a result-obligation: the depletion must not occur, not merely that the state must try.

Step 2 — Context: Look at neighbouring provisions. If other clauses use "endeavour" while this one uses "ensure," the drafters knew the difference and chose "ensure" deliberately. Read with the preamble, which speaks of "long-term conservation."

Step 3 — Object and purpose: The treaty's purpose is sustainable fisheries. Interpreting "ensure" as a mere best-efforts obligation would defeat the object.

Step 4 — Supplementary means (if needed): If ambiguity persists, consult preparatory work. If the negotiating history shows a deliberate move from "endeavour" to "ensure," that confirms the strong reading.

Conclusion: "Shall ensure" creates a result-obligation. State A's mere reduction may not satisfy it if depletion continues. Article 31 produces an answer through a disciplined sequence.

  • Treating signature as binding — only ratification (or equivalent) binds.
  • Reading treaty provisions in isolation — Article 31 requires context.
  • Ignoring object and purpose — purposive interpretation is part of Article 31, not optional.
  • Treating preparatory work as primary — it is supplementary under Article 32.
  • Confusing "withdrawal" (a permissible exit consistent with the treaty's terms) with "breach" (unilateral failure to perform).

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "analyse the principles of treaty formation, interpretation, and termination under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969)."

🏛️ 3.4 The United Nations System
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The United Nations was established by the Charter signed in San Francisco on 26 June 1945, replacing the failed League of Nations that had been founded after World War I. The UN's Preamble states the founders' commitments: "to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, … to reaffirm faith in fundamental human rights, … to establish conditions under which justice and respect for … obligations arising from treaties and other sources of international law can be maintained, and to promote social progress and better standards of life in larger freedom." These four commitments — peace, rights, justice, development — structure the UN's six principal organs.

The General Assembly (GA) is the plenary body of the UN; every member state has one seat and one vote, regardless of size or population. The GA's competence covers any question within the Charter (Article 10) but its resolutions are generally recommendatory, not legally binding (except for internal matters such as the UN budget and admission of new members). GA resolutions can nonetheless evidence opinio juris and contribute to the formation of customary international law (the GA's Friendly Relations Declaration, GA Res 2625 (XXV) 1970, is treated as authoritative).

The Security Council (SC) has "primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security" (Article 24). It has fifteen members: five permanent members (P5 — China, France, Russia, the UK, and the U.S.) and ten non-permanent members elected by the GA for two-year terms. The P5 each have a veto (Article 27): a substantive resolution requires nine affirmative votes and no negative vote from a P5 member. The veto has shaped — and limited — the SC's effectiveness since 1945. Under Chapter VI, the SC may recommend pacific settlement. Under Chapter VII, it may determine the existence of a threat to peace, a breach of peace, or an act of aggression (Article 39), and then take measures not involving force (Article 41: sanctions) or measures involving force (Article 42: authorisation of military action). Chapter VII resolutions are binding on all member states (Article 25). Chapter VII has been invoked to authorise force in Korea (1950, technically via uniting-for-peace), Iraq (1990, Resolution 678), Libya (2011, Resolution 1973), and many peacekeeping mandates.

The Secretariat, headed by the Secretary-General, conducts the day-to-day work of the UN. The Secretary-General has functions in negotiation and diplomacy (good offices), in alerting the SC to threats (Article 99), and in representing the UN. The Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) coordinates economic, social, and related work. The Trusteeship Council is now dormant (the last trust territory, Palau, became independent in 1994). The International Court of Justice is the UN's principal judicial organ, treated in section 3.5 of this chapter. Beyond the six principal organs, the UN system includes specialised agencies (WHO, ILO, UNESCO, WIPO, IMO, ICAO), funds and programmes (UNICEF, UNDP, UNHCR), and treaty bodies (Human Rights Committee, CERD Committee). A graduate of CLN4U should be able to name the principal organs and explain the legal force of their outputs.

  • UN Charter — founding treaty; signed 26 June 1945; in force 24 October 1945.
  • General Assembly — plenary; one vote per state; recommendatory resolutions.
  • Security Council — 15 members; primary responsibility for peace and security; binding resolutions under Chapter VII.
  • P5 — permanent members with veto: China, France, Russia, UK, US.
  • Veto (Article 27) — a negative vote by any P5 member blocks substantive SC resolutions.
  • Chapter VI — pacific settlement; recommendations.
  • Chapter VII — coercive measures; binding sanctions or authorisation of force.
  • Article 39 determination — threat to peace, breach of peace, or act of aggression.
  • ICJ — UN's principal judicial organ.
  • Specialised agencies — WHO, ILO, UNESCO, IMO, ICAO, WIPO.

Worked Example — Distinguishing GA vs SC Resolutions

Resolution A: GA Resolution 60/1 (2005), the World Summit Outcome Document, articulating the responsibility to protect (R2P).

Resolution B: SC Resolution 1973 (2011), authorising "all necessary measures" to protect civilians in Libya.

GA Resolution 60/1: Adopted by consensus; in itself not legally binding; carries great political weight; evidence of opinio juris on the emerging R2P norm; subsequently invoked but does not, alone, authorise force.

SC Resolution 1973: Adopted 10–0 with 5 abstentions; explicitly invoked Chapter VII (Articles 39, 41, 42); contained the operative phrase "all necessary measures," which is the SC's standard authorisation-of-force language; legally binding on all UN members (Article 25); served as the legal basis for the NATO-led intervention in Libya.

The distinction matters. GA resolutions inform; SC Chapter VII resolutions command. A practitioner must always identify which type a given resolution is.

  • Treating GA resolutions as binding — they are recommendatory except in internal matters.
  • Forgetting the veto — five permanent members can block substantive SC action.
  • Confusing Chapter VI (recommendatory) with Chapter VII (binding) action.
  • Treating the Secretary-General as the leader of the UN in a political sense — the SG is an administrator and convener.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "analyse the structure and functions of the United Nations (General Assembly, Security Council, ICJ, Secretariat, ECOSOC, specialised agencies)."

⚖️ 3.5 The International Court of Justice (ICJ)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The International Court of Justice (ICJ), seated in The Hague, is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations (UN Charter Article 92). Established in 1945 to succeed the Permanent Court of International Justice (1922–1946), the ICJ has been operating since April 1946. Its Statute is annexed to and integral with the UN Charter. The Court consists of fifteen judges, elected for nine-year terms by concurrent absolute majorities of the General Assembly and the Security Council, with attention to representation of "the main forms of civilization" and "the principal legal systems of the world" (ICJ Statute Article 9). No two judges may be nationals of the same state. Canada's Yves Fortier sat on the Court; Gilbert Guillaume of France was President 2000–2003.

The ICJ has two distinct jurisdictions. Contentious jurisdiction (Article 36) decides disputes between states. The Court's contentious jurisdiction is consensual: a state can be brought before the ICJ only if it has consented to jurisdiction. Consent can be expressed in several ways. Special agreement (compromis): the parties to a specific dispute conclude an agreement to submit it to the Court. Compromissory clauses: a treaty article providing that disputes about the treaty's interpretation will be decided by the ICJ. Optional clause declarations (Article 36(2)): a state declares it accepts the Court's jurisdiction as compulsory in relation to any other state accepting the same obligation. Canada has filed an optional-clause declaration, with reservations.

Advisory jurisdiction (Article 65) allows the Court to give legal opinions on questions submitted by the GA, the SC, or other authorised organs. Advisory opinions are not binding but carry enormous authority. The ICJ's advisory opinions include the Reparation for Injuries Suffered in the Service of the UN [1949] ICJ 174 (establishing the UN's international legal personality); the Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons [1996] ICJ 226 (the Court found the use or threat of nuclear weapons "generally contrary" to international law, but could not definitively rule on the extreme case of self-defence); the Wall in the Occupied Palestinian Territory [2004] ICJ 136; and the 2024 advisory opinion on the legal consequences of Israel's policies in the occupied territories.

ICJ procedure resembles, in formal terms, civil litigation in a domestic court. Pleadings are filed in writing (Memorial, Counter-Memorial, Reply, Rejoinder), oral arguments are heard, and the Court deliberates in private. Judgments are by majority; dissenting and separate opinions are common. Compliance is, in formal terms, mandatory under Article 94(1) of the Charter, and the SC can take measures to enforce judgments under Article 94(2). In practice, compliance has been mixed: states generally comply, but the most contested cases — Nicaragua v United States [1986] (the U.S. withdrew from compulsory jurisdiction after losing on the merits) — illustrate the limits. The ICJ's docket has grown substantially since 1990 and remains active, with cases on genocide (The Gambia v Myanmar; South Africa v Israel), maritime delimitation, environmental law, and many others.

  • ICJ — principal judicial organ of the UN, seated in The Hague.
  • ICJ Statute — annexed to and integral with the UN Charter.
  • Fifteen judges — nine-year terms; representative of the world's legal systems.
  • Contentious jurisdiction — disputes between states; requires consent.
  • Compromis — special agreement to submit a specific dispute.
  • Compromissory clause — treaty article giving ICJ jurisdiction over disputes about the treaty.
  • Optional clause — Article 36(2); reciprocal compulsory jurisdiction.
  • Advisory jurisdiction — non-binding legal opinions for UN organs.
  • Provisional measures — Article 41; analogue to interlocutory injunctions; binding on parties.
  • Article 94 enforcement — Security Council can enforce judgments (rarely used).

Worked Example — Establishing ICJ Jurisdiction

Scenario: State A claims that State B has breached a bilateral treaty on the protection of cultural property. State A wishes to bring the matter to the ICJ.

Step 1 — Is there consent? Check the treaty for a compromissory clause. If Article 24 provides "Any dispute concerning the interpretation or application of this Treaty shall be settled by the International Court of Justice unless the Parties agree to another mode of settlement," consent is established.

Step 2 — Failing that, check optional clause declarations. If both states have filed declarations under Article 36(2) covering the type of dispute, jurisdiction is established within the scope of the more restrictive declaration (the reciprocal principle).

Step 3 — Failing that, attempt a compromis. The parties can specifically agree to submit this dispute.

Step 4 — Without consent, jurisdiction is not established. The ICJ has no inherent jurisdiction over states. Diplomatic protest, international negotiation, or other dispute-settlement mechanisms must be used.

  • Treating the ICJ as a world supreme court with jurisdiction over anything — it requires consent.
  • Confusing advisory opinions with binding judgments.
  • Forgetting Article 94(2) enforcement is dependent on the Security Council, where the veto operates.
  • Treating individuals as ICJ parties — only states are parties in contentious proceedings.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "analyse the jurisdiction and procedure of the International Court of Justice and the International Criminal Court."

🍁 3.6 Reception of International Law in Canada
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The relationship between international law and domestic Canadian law turns on a distinction with deep historical roots: monism vs dualism. Monism treats international and domestic law as parts of a single legal order; an international rule, once binding internationally, is automatically part of domestic law. Dualism treats them as distinct systems; international rules must be transformed into domestic law (typically by legislation) before they have direct domestic effect. Canada, following the British constitutional tradition, is generally dualist for treaties: a treaty is not law in Canada simply because Canada has ratified it. Parliament or a legislature must enact implementing legislation. For customary international law, Canada follows a more monist approach: rules of customary international law are received into the common law without need for legislation (R v Hape [2007] 2 SCR 292).

The dualist approach for treaties has practical consequences. Canada has ratified many treaties — the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the Convention against Torture, the Refugee Convention — that are not directly enforceable in Canadian courts as such. They become legally enforceable through statutes. The Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (2002) implements the Refugee Convention; the Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act (2000) implements the Rome Statute. Some treaties bind Canada internationally but have no implementing legislation; their domestic legal force is therefore limited, though they may still influence statutory interpretation.

The presumption of conformity is the doctrine that Canadian statutes should be interpreted, where possible, consistently with Canada's international obligations. The Supreme Court explained this in R v Hape: courts presume that the legislature does not intend to legislate in violation of international law. The presumption can be overcome by clear statutory language, but it provides substantial interpretive force. Baker v Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) [1999] 2 SCR 817 illustrated the principle in administrative law: the Convention on the Rights of the Child, though not directly implemented, informed the reasonableness review of a deportation decision affecting a Canadian-born child. Justice L'Heureux-Dubé wrote that "the values reflected in international human rights law may help inform the contextual approach to statutory interpretation."

The Charter presents its own pathway. Section 11(g) of the Charter — protecting against retroactive criminalisation except for acts that were criminal under international law at the time committed — gives international law a direct constitutional footing. Charter interpretation is also informed by Canada's international obligations: the Supreme Court has repeatedly noted that the Charter "should be presumed to provide protection at least as great as that afforded by similar provisions in international human rights documents which Canada has ratified" (Slaight Communications v Davidson [1989] 1 SCR 1038). Customary international law is, since Hape, received automatically into the common law and can inform Charter interpretation directly. The interaction is rich: dualist for treaties but porous through statutory interpretation, presumption of conformity, and Charter interpretation; monist for custom; constitutionally entrenched at one specific point (s. 11(g)). A Canadian lawyer ignores international law at her peril.

  • Monism — international and domestic law form a single system.
  • Dualism — international and domestic law are distinct; transformation required.
  • Canadian position — dualist for treaties; monist (since Hape) for customary international law.
  • Treaty implementation — Parliament must enact implementing legislation for treaty terms to bind domestically.
  • Presumption of conformity — statutes interpreted consistently with international obligations.
  • Baker v Canada (1999) — unimplemented treaty as informing administrative discretion.
  • R v Hape (2007) — customary international law automatically part of Canadian common law.
  • Charter s. 11(g) — international law as constitutional grounding for non-retroactivity exception.
  • Crimes Against Humanity and War Crimes Act (2000) — implementing legislation for Rome Statute.

Worked Example — A Treaty Without Implementing Legislation

Suppose Canada ratifies a treaty obligating states to enact minimum-wage standards. Parliament does not pass implementing legislation. A worker in Ontario argues, in court, that his employer violated the treaty by paying below the international minimum.

Dualist answer: The treaty, without implementing legislation, does not create a directly enforceable right in Canadian domestic law. The employer has not breached Canadian statute (assuming provincial minimum wage was paid).

Presumption of conformity: If a Canadian statute on labour standards is ambiguous, a court will prefer the reading consistent with Canada's treaty obligations. Where the statute is silent, the treaty does not fill the gap.

Charter intersection: If the worker can frame the claim as a Charter equality issue (s. 15), the international treaty may inform the Charter analysis under Slaight Communications, providing interpretive weight.

Customary international law: If the treaty norm has crystallised into custom (rare for labour standards), Hape would receive it directly into the common law, with stronger force.

Conclusion: The worker's strongest argument is not the treaty directly but a Charter or statutory argument informed by the treaty. This is the typical shape of international-law litigation in Canada.

  • Believing ratification alone makes a treaty domestic law in Canada — it does not.
  • Confusing treaty law with customary international law — different reception rules.
  • Ignoring the presumption of conformity — it is a powerful interpretive tool.
  • Forgetting that some statutes are implementing legislation — they are themselves the bridge from treaty to enforceable domestic law.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "explain the monism–dualism distinction and Canada's dualist approach to treaty reception."
