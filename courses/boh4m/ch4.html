4.1 — Types of Plans: Strategic, Tactical, Operational
Time horizons; single-use vs. standing plans; contingency planning and the planning hierarchy.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Planning is the management function that decides what the organization will do and how. A useful taxonomy distinguishes plans by their time horizon and scope. Strategic plans are long-term (typically 3–10 years), set by top management, and address the fundamental direction of the organization — markets to enter, businesses to exit, major capital investments. Tactical plans (1–3 years), made by middle management, translate strategy into department-level objectives — a marketing plan for the year, an operations plan to expand capacity 20%. Operational plans (days to a year), made by first-line managers, define the specific actions that produce day-to-day output — a weekly staffing schedule, a monthly production target, a daily call quota.

A second axis separates single-use plans from standing plans. Single-use plans — programs, projects, budgets — are designed for unique, non-recurring situations. A new-store opening, a software migration, or a one-time charity drive all need single-use plans. Standing plans — policies, procedures, rules — guide recurring decisions. A vacation policy, a customer-refund procedure, or a "no shoes, no service" rule each apply repeatedly without re-deciding from scratch every time.

Smart organizations also build contingency plans — alternative courses of action prepared in advance for likely future events. The Canadian banking sector famously requires "living wills" since 2010 documenting how each bank would unwind without contagion. Less dramatic examples: snow-day operating plans for retailers, supplier-failure backups, social-media crisis protocols. The 2020 pandemic exposed how poorly most firms had planned for low-probability/high-impact events; the same exposure pushed contingency planning back into the spotlight. The general rule: ask "what is most likely to disrupt us in the next 12–36 months?", then write the response now while heads are calm.

  • Strategic plan: long-term, top-management, sets organizational direction.
  • Tactical plan: 1–3 years, middle management, departmental.
  • Operational plan: short term, first-line, day-to-day execution.
  • Single-use plan: programs, projects, budgets — used once.
  • Standing plan: policies, procedures, rules — used repeatedly.
  • Contingency plan: pre-prepared response to likely future disruption.

Case: Tim Hortons rolls out a new oat-milk latte.

Strategic plan: enter the plant-based-beverage market over five years to capture a Gen-Z audience.
Tactical plan (Year 1): launch the oat-milk latte nationally in March; allocate $4M of marketing budget; train 2,500 store managers.
Operational plan: each store's daily production schedule reserves 12 minutes per shift for product training; the Q2 weekly sales target rises 8%.
Single-use plan: a $300K national TV launch campaign with a defined start and end.
Standing plan: the company's general beverage-handling SOP applies; the franchise agreement covers product introductions in general.
Contingency plan: if supplier of oat base fails, an approved Canadian-grown backup supplier can be activated within 72 hours.

  • Treating strategy as something written once a year and then ignored. Living strategy is reviewed quarterly.
  • Writing standing plans (policies) in vague language. Procedures should be specific enough to follow without interpretation.
  • Skipping contingency planning because "it probably won't happen." It is precisely low-probability events that catch organizations unprepared.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "describe types of plans (strategic, tactical, operational, contingency, single-use, standing) and the time horizons of each."

4.2 — SWOT Analysis & SMART Goals
Identifying strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats; writing goals that drive performance.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Two simple frameworks dominate practical planning. SWOT — Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats — was popularized at Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s and remains the most-used planning tool in the world. Strengths and weaknesses are internal attributes — what the organization is good at, what holds it back. Opportunities and threats are external conditions — favourable or unfavourable forces in the environment. A complete SWOT pairs the cells: how can we use a Strength to capture an Opportunity? How can we shore up a Weakness against a Threat? The pairing — not the listing — is where insight lives. Many corporate SWOTs fail because they devolve into laundry lists with no resulting decisions.

Goals translate planning insights into commitments. The SMART mnemonic, attributed to George Doran in Management Review (1981), requires goals to be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Variations replace A with "Agreed" or "Aspirational" and add R as "Reviewed" — the spirit is the same. SMART criteria are the practical implementation of Locke & Latham's goal-setting research (from Chapter 2). "Increase sales" is not a SMART goal; "Increase Q3 sales of the bakery line by 12% by August 31, with a $40K marketing budget" is.

A complementary practice is Management by Objectives (MBO), originally proposed by Peter Drucker in 1954. MBO cascades organizational goals into department goals, then individual goals, with each employee jointly agreeing to specific objectives with their manager and reviewing them quarterly. MBO is the historical ancestor of today's OKRs (Objectives and Key Results), popularized by Andy Grove at Intel and adopted at Google, where ambitious "objectives" are paired with three to five measurable "key results."

  • SWOT: Strengths, Weaknesses (internal) + Opportunities, Threats (external).
  • SWOT pairing: the value is in cross-cell strategy generation, not the lists themselves.
  • SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
  • MBO (Drucker): cascade objectives, jointly set with employees, review regularly.
  • OKRs: the modern descendant of MBO; ambitious objectives + measurable key results.

Case: SWOT for a Toronto-area independent bookstore in 2026.

Strengths: loyal local community, knowledgeable staff, café revenue stream.
Weaknesses: small inventory vs. Amazon, no online ordering, ageing point-of-sale system.
Opportunities: rising interest in local independent businesses; municipal small-business grants; partnership with the local library.
Threats: Amazon's continued discounting; rising commercial rent; declining youth reading rates.
S/O pairing: launch in-store author events using loyal community + local-business grant.
W/T pairing: build a simple online-ordering page within 90 days to counter Amazon convenience.
SMART goal: "Implement online ordering by August 31, 2026, with at least 50 weekly online orders by Q4 — using the $5,000 municipal grant."

  • Listing SWOT items without then making decisions. SWOT is a tool, not an outcome.
  • Conflating opportunity (external) with strength (internal). "Great staff" is a strength; "local business renaissance" is an opportunity.
  • Writing aspirational goals without measurability. "Be the best bookstore" is a slogan; "achieve 4.7 stars and 200 reviews by year-end" is a goal.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "apply the SWOT framework and SMART-goal criteria (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound) to a business scenario."

4.3 — Organizational Structures
Functional, divisional, matrix, network, team-based; centralization, span of control, chain of command.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Organizing is the function of arranging people and resources so the plan can be executed. Every structure makes trade-offs along three classic dimensions. Specialization: how narrowly is work divided? Highly specialized jobs (assembly-line tasks, specialized medical roles) gain efficiency but risk boredom and silos. Centralization: how much decision authority sits at the top? Centralized organizations have consistent decisions but slow response. Decentralized organizations push authority to local managers — faster but more variable. Span of control: how many subordinates report directly to each manager? Narrow spans (5–7) give close supervision but require more managers; wide spans (15+) reduce managerial cost but require self-directed staff. These three choices, plus the choice of chain of command (who reports to whom), define an organization's structure.

Five structural archetypes dominate. Functional structure groups employees by expertise — marketing department, finance department, operations department. It is efficient within a single business but slow to coordinate cross-function work. Divisional structure groups by product, market, or geography — a "Canadian division" or a "personal care products division." It is responsive to its market but duplicates functions (each division has its own marketing). Matrix structure overlays both: an employee belongs to a function (e.g., engineering) and to a product team simultaneously, with two bosses. Matrix structures are powerful for complex environments (aerospace, professional services, NASA) but require excellent communication and conflict-management skills.

Two newer forms have grown rapidly. Network structure: the organization contracts out most non-core work to suppliers and partners, retaining only strategic core functions. Nike's classic example: design and marketing inside, manufacturing entirely outsourced. Team-based / flat structure: removes most middle-management layers and organizes work in cross-functional teams with high autonomy. Tech firms like Spotify (squad model) and self-managed companies like Buurtzorg (Dutch home-care) are extreme examples. The right structure depends on the organization's strategy, size, environment, and culture; no single form is "best."

  • Functional: grouped by expertise; efficient, weak on cross-function coordination.
  • Divisional: grouped by product/region/market; responsive, duplicates functions.
  • Matrix: dual reporting; powerful for complex work, hard to run.
  • Network: contract out non-core; lean core organization.
  • Team-based / flat: few layers; autonomous cross-functional teams.
  • Trade-offs: specialization, centralization, span of control, chain of command.

Case: A growing software startup outgrows its founding structure.

At 10 people, a flat structure with one boss and direct collaboration works perfectly. At 50, communication overhead explodes and the company switches to a functional structure (engineering, sales, marketing, customer success). At 250, with three product lines serving different industries, the functional structure causes constant prioritization fights, so the company adopts a matrix: functional managers own career development; product-line directors own roadmaps. At 1,000+, the matrix becomes too slow and the company restructures into product divisions with shared services. Same company, four structures, each fitting the stage.

  • Treating a span of control number as universally optimal. Optimal span depends on task complexity, employee experience, and technology.
  • Confusing decentralization with disorganization. Well-decentralized organizations have very clear local accountability and reporting.
  • Believing one structure works for the life of the organization. Structure must evolve with strategy and scale.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "compare organizational structures (functional, divisional, matrix, network, team-based) and explain trade-offs in centralization, span of control, and chain of command."

4.4 — Recruitment & Selection
Job analysis → posting → screening → interviewing → testing → reference checks → offer; fair hiring under the Ontario Human Rights Code.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Hiring well is one of the highest-leverage actions a manager takes. A bad hire costs roughly 30% of first-year compensation in lost productivity, manager time, and replacement cost — often $20,000–$100,000 in Canada depending on role. The hiring process should be structured to maximize both quality and fairness. The standard sequence is: job analysis (what does the role do; what skills, knowledge, and abilities are required?), job description and job specification (the document derived from the analysis), job posting (internal first, then external), screening (filtering applications against criteria), interviewing, testing (skills, work samples), reference checks, and offer.

Three interview types are common. Unstructured interviews — different questions for each candidate — feel personal but research consistently shows they are very poor predictors of job success and are highly vulnerable to bias. Structured interviews — same questions in the same order for all candidates, scored against a rubric — are the gold standard and double or triple predictive validity. Behavioural interviews ("Tell me about a time when…") ask for specific past examples following the STAR pattern (Situation, Task, Action, Result). For technical roles, work samples (a coding exercise, a sample marketing brief, a teaching demonstration) are even better predictors than interviews.

Canadian employers must comply with the Ontario Human Rights Code, which prohibits discrimination in employment on 17 protected grounds including race, ancestry, place of origin, colour, ethnic origin, citizenship, creed (religion), sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, age, marital status, family status, disability, record of offences, and receipt of public assistance. Employers must not ask questions related to these grounds during interviews (e.g., "Do you plan to have children?" is forbidden). Selection criteria must be bona fide occupational requirements — genuinely necessary for the job. Employers also have a duty to accommodate employees and candidates with disabilities or other protected characteristics up to the point of undue hardship. The federal Pay Equity Act and Ontario's Employment Standards Act add further obligations. These rules are not optional — violation carries real legal and reputational cost, and the right way to think of them is as a floor, with most ethical employers building higher standards on top.

  • Job analysis → description → specification: foundation of every hire.
  • Internal posting: consider current employees before external search; improves morale and retention.
  • Structured interview: same questions, same rubric, double or triple the predictive validity of unstructured.
  • Behavioural (STAR): Situation, Task, Action, Result.
  • Work sample: often the strongest predictor.
  • Ontario Human Rights Code: 17 protected grounds; bona fide occupational requirements only.
  • Duty to accommodate: up to undue hardship.

Case: Hiring a barista at a coffee shop.

Job analysis: greet customers, prepare drinks per recipe, operate POS, maintain food-safety standards.
Job specification: 6+ months customer service, ability to lift 15 kg, food-handler certificate (or willing to obtain within 30 days).
Posting: internal Slack for two weeks, then Indeed and Workopolis.
Screening: rubric (customer service experience, availability, transit access, certifications) — yes/partial/no for each.
Structured interview (3 candidates × 30 min): 6 questions, same order. Behavioural: "Tell me about a time you handled a frustrated customer (STAR)."
Work sample: 10-minute trial making three drinks under observation.
References: 2 work references via phone.
Offer: based on overall rubric score. Decline questions: NO questions about marital status, plans for children, religion, country of origin — these are forbidden under the Ontario Human Rights Code.

  • Hiring on "gut feeling" or "fit" without specifying what fit means. Research shows these phrases often mask unconscious bias.
  • Asking forbidden questions to be friendly ("So, are you planning a family?"). This is illegal under the Ontario Human Rights Code regardless of intent.
  • Skipping reference checks. Roughly 1 in 4 candidates exaggerate credentials on a resume; reference checks catch most of it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.4 — "describe the recruitment-and-selection process (job analysis, posting, screening, interviewing, testing, reference checks, offer) and the principles of fair hiring under the Ontario Human Rights Code."

4.5 — Onboarding, Training & Development
Orientation, on-the-job and classroom training, mentoring, coaching, e-learning, leadership pipelines.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A new hire arrives with potential. The organization's job is to convert that potential into productive contribution as quickly as possible. The process begins with orientation / onboarding: introducing the company, the role, the team, the technology, the policies. Strong onboarding typically extends over 90 days (not just one chaotic first week) and includes scheduled check-ins, role-specific training, social integration, and clear 30/60/90-day goals. Research shows that the quality of the first 90 days has a measurable effect on retention out to two years — half of all turnover within two years can be predicted by early-onboarding experience.

The Canadian training-method landscape covers several options. On-the-job training (OJT) — learning by doing alongside an experienced worker — is the oldest method and remains powerful for hands-on roles. Apprenticeships, formalized in Ontario through the Skilled Trades Ontario framework, combine OJT with classroom learning over multi-year programs in roughly 150 designated trades. Classroom training works for foundational knowledge. E-learning (online courses, micro-learning, mobile apps) has scaled dramatically since the pandemic. Simulations — for pilots, surgeons, nuclear operators — let high-stakes practice happen without high-stakes consequences. Mentoring pairs a junior employee with a senior advisor over a longer arc. Coaching is shorter, performance-focused, and often delivered by a manager or external coach.

Beyond initial training comes development — building skills for future roles, not just the current one. Strong development practices include rotational programs (move through several functions in the first three years), stretch assignments (deliberately giving someone work slightly beyond current capability), tuition support, professional certifications, and structured leadership programs. The 70-20-10 model — popular at General Electric and many Canadian organizations — proposes that adults learn 70% on the job, 20% from others, and 10% from formal training. The implication is that designing the on-the-job experience deliberately matters more than designing the training course.

  • Onboarding: first 90 days; structured introduction predicts long-run retention.
  • On-the-job training (OJT): learn by doing alongside a skilled coworker.
  • Apprenticeship: Skilled Trades Ontario programs combine OJT with classroom learning.
  • E-learning / micro-learning: scalable, asynchronous.
  • Mentoring vs. coaching: long-arc advising vs. short-cycle performance support.
  • 70-20-10: 70% on the job, 20% from others, 10% formal training.

Case: A bank's 90-day onboarding for new branch advisors.

Week 1: Welcome breakfast; HR paperwork; IT setup; meet the team; corporate orientation video (4 hours); buddy assigned.
Weeks 2–4: Classroom training in financial products (mornings) + shadow a senior advisor (afternoons).
Weeks 5–8: Take own client appointments under direct supervision; weekly 30-minute check-ins with manager.
Weeks 9–12: Independent caseload with light supervision; complete two compliance e-learnings; 30-day formal review at week 12.
Day 100: Formal onboarding closeout with feedback both ways; transition to standard quarterly review cycle.
Banks using this style of structured 90-day onboarding report 2-year retention rates ~20 percentage points higher than peers with informal onboarding.

  • Treating orientation as "Day 1 paperwork." Real onboarding extends over 90 days with clear milestones.
  • Investing in formal training only. The 70-20-10 model implies on-the-job design deserves the most attention.
  • Confusing mentoring with coaching. Mentoring is long-arc career advice; coaching is short, performance-focused.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.5 — "describe orientation, training methods (on-the-job, classroom, e-learning, mentoring, coaching), and performance-appraisal techniques (rating scales, MBO, 360-degree)."

4.6 — Performance Appraisal
Rating scales, BARS, MBO, 360-degree feedback, and the limits of forced ranking.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Performance appraisal is the formal process by which an organization assesses employee performance and provides feedback. Done well, it improves performance, supports development, and informs decisions about pay, promotion, and termination. Done badly, it demotivates employees and erodes manager–employee trust. Several methods are common.

Graphic rating scales rate the employee on a series of traits or behaviours (cooperation, communication, productivity) from 1 to 5. They are quick to use but vulnerable to "halo error" (one strong impression colours all ratings), "leniency error" (everyone gets 4s), and "central tendency" (everyone gets 3s). Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales (BARS) reduce these problems by anchoring each scale point with specific observed behaviours ("Anticipates customer needs before being asked" = 5; "Responds politely when prompted" = 3). They take longer to design but produce far more useful conversations.

Management by Objectives (MBO) assesses performance against jointly-set objectives, which is highly engaging when the objectives are specific and the conversation is honest. 360-degree feedback collects ratings from the employee's manager, peers, direct reports, and sometimes customers — giving a fuller picture, particularly of interpersonal and leadership behaviours. It works well for development conversations but is controversial when used for pay or termination decisions because of rater politics. Forced ranking (popularized at General Electric under Jack Welch in the 1990s — rank everyone, fire the bottom 10%) has fallen out of favour at most Canadian organizations because it damages collaboration and produces winner-take-all dynamics. Continuous-feedback systems (frequent short check-ins replacing annual reviews) have become widespread since the mid-2010s and are increasingly the dominant practice in tech and professional services.

Regardless of method, three principles improve any appraisal. First, separate development conversations from compensation conversations — mixing them undermines honest feedback. Second, gather specific behavioural examples over the period, not impressions from the last week. Third, make the conversation a dialogue — the employee should leave with a clear understanding of what to keep doing, what to change, and what support they will get.

  • Graphic rating scale: quick but vulnerable to halo, leniency, central tendency.
  • BARS: behaviourally anchored; more useful conversations.
  • MBO: appraisal against jointly-set objectives.
  • 360-degree feedback: manager + peers + direct reports + customers.
  • Forced ranking: rank and cull bottom; widely abandoned for damaging collaboration.
  • Continuous feedback: frequent check-ins replace the annual review.

Case: Rebuilding the appraisal system at a 200-person organization.

Old system: annual rating-scale review tied directly to bonus; managers spent November filling out forms and dreaded December conversations; ratings clustered at "3 — meets expectations."
New system: quarterly 30-minute check-ins on objectives (MBO), an annual 360-degree feedback round used only for development, and a separate January compensation discussion. Outcomes after 18 months: employees report 35% higher satisfaction with feedback quality; managers report less procrastination; turnover among high performers falls 8 percentage points.

  • Saving feedback for the annual review. Once-a-year feedback is too late to change behaviour.
  • Confusing development with compensation conversations. Honest feedback requires separating them.
  • Relying on rating scales alone. Without behavioural examples, ratings tell employees almost nothing about how to improve.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.5 — "describe orientation, training methods (on-the-job, classroom, e-learning, mentoring, coaching), and performance-appraisal techniques (rating scales, MBO, 360-degree)."
