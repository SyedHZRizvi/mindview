1.1 — What Managers Do: The Four Functions
Planning, organizing, leading, and controlling — Henri Fayol's enduring framework for what every manager does.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The single most important question an introductory management course must answer is almost embarrassingly simple: what does a manager actually do all day? The French industrialist Henri Fayol (1841–1925), writing in Administration Industrielle et Générale (1916), gave the answer that has stuck for more than a century. He observed that, regardless of industry — a coal mine, a hospital, a school, a software firm — managers spend their time doing four things: they plan, they organize, they lead, and they control. Fayol called it the "POLC" framework, and every contemporary textbook still organizes its chapters around it.

Planning means deciding what the organization will do and how it will do it: setting goals, scanning the environment, choosing strategies, and writing budgets. Organizing means arranging the resources — people, money, equipment, information — so the plan can actually be executed: drawing the org chart, defining jobs, allocating authority. Leading means influencing and motivating people to do the work willingly and well: communicating the vision, coaching, resolving conflict, recognizing performance. Controlling means measuring whether the plan is being met and taking corrective action when it isn't: budgets vs. actuals, dashboards, performance appraisals, quality audits.

These four functions are not done in sequence and then "finished"; they form a continuous loop. A retail-store manager opens her e-mail at 8:00 a.m. and within thirty minutes she has reviewed yesterday's sales (controlling), rewritten today's staffing rota because an associate called in sick (organizing), texted encouragement to a new hire (leading), and revised the marketing budget for the next quarter (planning). Every manager — whether the CEO of Loblaw or the assistant manager of a Tim Hortons — performs these same four functions, only the scope and complexity change.

  • Planning: setting goals and choosing means; producing strategy and budgets.
  • Organizing: assigning tasks, grouping people, allocating resources, defining authority.
  • Leading: motivating, communicating, coaching, resolving conflict.
  • Controlling: measuring results against standards and taking corrective action.
  • POLC: Fayol's enduring four-function framework — Planning, Organizing, Leading, Controlling.

Case: A coffee-shop manager's Monday morning.

From 7 to 10 a.m. she does the following actions. Classify each:

(a) Writes a sales target of $12,000 for the week → Planning.
(b) Re-assigns barista Jamal to the espresso bar so the lineup moves faster → Organizing.
(c) Coaches new hire Priya on how to upsell the seasonal latte → Leading.
(d) Counts the cash drawer and reconciles it against the POS report → Controlling.

In three hours she has cycled through all four functions — proving Fayol's claim that POLC is the universal management job description.

  • Listing the functions as if they happen in a fixed sequence (Plan → Organize → Lead → Control). They overlap and recycle constantly.
  • Treating "leading" and "managing" as the same word. Leading is one of four management functions, not a synonym for management.
  • Forgetting controlling. Students often describe planning and leading at length and skip the measurement loop.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "describe the role of management and the functions performed by managers (planning, organizing, leading, controlling) in organizations."

1.2 — Levels and Types of Managers
Top, middle, and first-line managers; line vs. staff; functional vs. general managers; the changing pyramid.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Not all managers are alike. A useful mental model is the management pyramid: a small number of top managers at the apex, a larger band of middle managers in the centre, and a wide base of first-line (or "supervisory") managers above the operating employees. Top managers — CEO, COO, CFO, executive vice-presidents — are responsible for the organization's overall direction, long-range strategy, and external relationships with investors, regulators, and major customers. Middle managers — regional directors, plant managers, department heads — translate top-level strategy into operational plans and coordinate the work of first-line managers. First-line managers — shift supervisors, team leads, store managers — directly oversee the people who make the product or deliver the service.

The pyramid metaphor is useful but increasingly out of date. Since the 1990s, organizations have been delayering (removing levels of middle management) and flattening (broadening the span of control of each manager). Modern technology companies like Spotify or Shopify use squad-based or matrix structures with very few formal layers. Cross-cutting these vertical levels, managers are also categorized horizontally as line (directly responsible for producing the organization's output) or staff (advisory — HR, legal, IT), and as functional (one specialty — marketing only) or general (multiple specialties — branch manager who handles operations, sales, and HR).

  • Top management: sets vision, strategy, and external relationships (CEO, CFO, COO).
  • Middle management: translates strategy into operations (directors, regional managers).
  • First-line management: supervises non-managerial employees (shift leads, team leaders).
  • Line manager: directly produces the organization's output.
  • Staff manager: advises and supports line operations (HR, legal, IT).
  • Functional vs. general: one specialty vs. multiple.

Case: Loblaw Companies Limited (Canadian grocery and pharmacy chain).

(a) Galen Weston Jr., as Chair and President, sets corporate strategy across Loblaw, Shoppers Drug Mart, and President's Choice — top management.
(b) The Vice-President of Store Operations for Ontario translates that strategy into regional store targets — middle management.
(c) A Real Canadian Superstore evening shift manager schedules cashiers and orders inventory — first-line management.
(d) The Chief Human Resources Officer advises all three levels on hiring policy but does not directly sell groceries — staff manager.

  • Equating "manager" with "senior executive." Most managers in Canada are first-line supervisors, not C-suite officers.
  • Confusing "staff" managers with administrative clerical staff. A staff manager is a senior advisory role, not entry-level support.
  • Assuming a flatter org chart means fewer managers overall — it usually means broader spans of control, not fewer responsibilities.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "identify and describe the levels of management (top, middle, first-line) and the kinds of tasks each level performs."

1.3 — Katz's Three Managerial Skills
Technical, human, and conceptual skills; how the mix shifts as managers move up the pyramid.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In the September 1974 Harvard Business Review, Robert L. Katz published "Skills of an Effective Administrator," in which he argued that successful managers possess three categories of skills in different proportions. The article is one of the most cited in the management literature, and it gives us a vocabulary that high-school and university students still use today.

Technical skills are the specialized knowledge needed to do a particular kind of work — programming a database, balancing a trial balance, baking a soufflé, performing surgery. Human (or interpersonal) skills are the ability to work effectively with other people: listening, motivating, coaching, resolving conflict, building trust. Conceptual skills are the ability to think analytically, see the big picture, recognize how the parts of the organization fit together, and analyse complex situations.

Katz's most provocative claim was that the mix of these three skills shifts dramatically as you move up the management pyramid. First-line managers (shift supervisors, junior accountants who become team leads) rely heavily on technical skills — they need to know how to do the work to credibly supervise it. Middle managers need a balance: enough technical to oversee specialists, strong human skills to coordinate across departments, and growing conceptual ability. Top managers rely heavily on conceptual skills — by the time you are CEO of TD Bank, you are not closing individual mortgages; you are reading economic forecasts, evaluating acquisitions, and designing organizational change. Human skills, notably, are roughly equally important at every level. Promotion failures often come from a manager who has plenty of technical skill but never developed the human and conceptual skills needed for the next level.

  • Technical skill: specialized job knowledge; most important for first-line managers.
  • Human skill: the ability to work effectively with people; important at every level.
  • Conceptual skill: big-picture, analytical, system-level thinking; most important for top managers.
  • Skill mix: shifts from technical-heavy to conceptual-heavy as managers move up.

Case: A high-performing software engineer is promoted to engineering manager at Shopify.

As a senior engineer her time was 80% technical (writing code, debugging) and 20% human (code reviews, mentoring). Three months into the manager role her boss notes she is "spending all day in code and ignoring the team." Katz's framework diagnoses the problem: a manager's required mix is roughly 30% technical, 40% human, 30% conceptual. Her plan: schedule one-on-ones, learn to delegate code-writing to her senior engineers, and start reading the company's quarterly OKRs to grow conceptual skill.

  • Believing technical skill alone gets you promoted to top management. Many highly technical specialists hit a "managerial wall" because they never built human or conceptual skill.
  • Treating conceptual skill as "common sense" or innate. It can be developed through reading, mentorship, executive education, and deliberate reflection.
  • Ignoring human skills because they feel "soft." Research repeatedly shows interpersonal skills are the strongest predictor of long-run management success.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — "identify and describe the skills required of an effective manager (Katz: technical, human, conceptual)."

1.4 — Mintzberg's Ten Managerial Roles
Three interpersonal, three informational, and four decisional roles — what managers really do, observed.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

While Fayol gave us functions — the abstract what of management — the Canadian scholar Henry Mintzberg, of McGill University, gave us roles — the concrete how. In his 1973 PhD thesis (later published as The Nature of Managerial Work), Mintzberg shadowed five CEOs continuously and recorded literally every activity they performed. His findings were revolutionary: managers do not sit and think strategically; they react constantly to a flood of short, fragmented, oral interactions. From this data he distilled ten distinct managerial roles, grouped into three categories.

The three interpersonal roles arise from the manager's formal authority and status. The figurehead performs ceremonial duties (cutting ribbons, signing documents, speaking at a retirement lunch). The leader motivates, hires, and develops subordinates. The liaison builds and maintains a network of contacts outside the chain of command. The three informational roles grow naturally out of those contacts. The monitor scans for information relevant to the organization. The disseminator passes that information to subordinates who would not otherwise have access. The spokesperson communicates the organization's plans, policies, and results to outsiders — investors, regulators, the press.

Finally, the four decisional roles are where the manager turns information into action. The entrepreneur initiates change — launching new products, restructuring teams, improving processes. The disturbance handler deals with unexpected crises — a key supplier shutting down, a PR scandal, an angry customer. The resource allocator decides who gets time, money, and people. The negotiator represents the organization in major deals — collective-bargaining, contracts, acquisitions. Mintzberg argued that no manager spends all day in any one role; effective managers fluidly move among all ten roles, often within a single hour.

  • Interpersonal: Figurehead, Leader, Liaison.
  • Informational: Monitor, Disseminator, Spokesperson.
  • Decisional: Entrepreneur, Disturbance Handler, Resource Allocator, Negotiator.
  • Fragmented work: the average managerial activity lasted only nine minutes in Mintzberg's data.

Case: A school principal between 9:00 a.m. and 10:30 a.m.

9:00 — Greets a parent committee at the front door (Figurehead).
9:10 — Calls a board contact to clarify a new mental-health policy (Liaison).
9:25 — Reads the weekly attendance dashboard (Monitor).
9:35 — Shares enrolment data with the vice-principal (Disseminator).
9:45 — Meets a teacher to discuss a classroom-management plan (Leader).
10:00 — A fire alarm goes off; she coordinates an unscheduled evacuation (Disturbance Handler).
10:20 — Approves the literacy budget for the next month (Resource Allocator).

In ninety minutes she has performed seven of Mintzberg's ten roles — exactly the fragmented, role-switching reality Mintzberg observed.

  • Confusing roles with functions. Fayol's POLC describes what managers must accomplish; Mintzberg's roles describe how they accomplish it through micro-interactions.
  • Treating the ten roles as a "to-do checklist." Effective managers move fluidly among them as situations demand.
  • Underestimating informational roles. Up to 80% of a manager's time is spent gathering or sharing information.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — "describe Mintzberg's ten managerial roles (interpersonal, informational, decisional) and provide examples of each in modern workplaces."

1.5 — Evolution of Management Thought: Classical & Behavioural Schools
Taylor, the Gilbreths, Fayol, Weber, the Hawthorne studies, Mary Parker Follett, and the rise of human relations.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Modern management thought is barely 125 years old, and its evolution tells the story of how we came to see workers — first as machines, then as social beings, then as knowledge professionals. The story begins around 1900 with the classical school. Frederick W. Taylor, working at Bethlehem Steel, published The Principles of Scientific Management in 1911. He used time-and-motion studies (stopwatches, detailed observation) to find the "one best way" to perform each task, then trained workers to do exactly that way. Productivity surged — pig-iron loading at Bethlehem more than tripled — and so did wages, but workers complained that scientific management treated them as cogs.

Around the same time, Frank and Lillian Gilbreth refined motion study (Lillian, with a PhD in industrial psychology, was arguably the first woman in management theory). In France, Henri Fayol codified the four functions and 14 principles of administration (unity of command, scalar chain, esprit de corps). In Germany, sociologist Max Weber theorized the bureaucracy — clear hierarchy, written rules, technical qualifications, impersonal decisions — as the most efficient form of large organization. All three lines of thought treated the organization as a rational, mechanical system.

The pendulum swung in the 1920s and 1930s with the behavioural / human-relations school. The famous Hawthorne studies at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works in Cicero, Illinois (1924–1932), led by Elton Mayo, showed that productivity rose simply because workers knew they were being studied — the "Hawthorne effect." More importantly, the studies revealed that informal social groups, attention from supervisors, and a sense of belonging were powerful drivers of performance. Mary Parker Follett, often called the "mother of modern management," wrote about power-with rather than power-over, group dynamics, and conflict resolution decades before the field caught up. Together these scholars set the stage for everything that followed: Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor, the Japanese quality movement, and today's emphasis on engagement and well-being.

  • Scientific management (Taylor): time-and-motion study, the "one best way," piece-rate pay.
  • Administrative principles (Fayol): the four functions plus 14 principles of management.
  • Bureaucracy (Weber): hierarchy, rules, impersonality, qualifications — the rational-legal organization.
  • Hawthorne studies (Mayo): social factors and attention matter more than physical working conditions.
  • Mary Parker Follett: integration, conflict as constructive, power-with not power-over.

Case: A fast-food chain redesigns its kitchen.

The chain hires consultants who run time-and-motion studies (Taylor) and standardize every drink to ten precise steps. The result: average drive-thru time drops from 4:10 to 2:45. But employee turnover jumps from 80% to 130% within six months. Diagnosed through the Hawthorne lens: workers feel like robots and unrecognized; the chain reinstates daily team huddles, recognition boards, and rotational stations. Turnover falls back to 90% within a year. The lesson: classical efficiency without human-relations attention is brittle.

  • Treating these schools as obsolete. Lean manufacturing, time-tracking apps, and Six Sigma are all direct descendants of Taylor.
  • Believing the "Hawthorne effect" proved that more attention always improves productivity — later re-analyses showed lighting and incentives also mattered.
  • Forgetting Mary Parker Follett. She was decades ahead of her time but largely ignored because she was a woman writing in the 1920s.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.1 — "describe the historical development of management thought (scientific management, administrative principles, human-relations movement, behavioural science, systems theory, contingency theory)."

1.6 — Contemporary Approaches: Systems, Contingency & the Learning Organization
Systems thinking, contingency ("it depends"), TQM, learning organizations, evidence-based management.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The post-war era added several frameworks that remain central to today's management. Systems theory, drawn from biology and engineering, treats the organization as an open system that takes inputs (raw materials, labour, capital, information), transforms them through processes, and produces outputs (goods, services). The implication is that no function — marketing, operations, HR — can be optimized in isolation; managers must think about how parts fit together and interact with the environment. The famous quote attributed to W. Edwards Deming, "A bad system will beat a good person every time," is the systems message in plain English.

Contingency theory, championed by Fred Fiedler, Joan Woodward, and Paul Lawrence & Jay Lorsch, replaced the classical search for "one best way" with the more honest answer: it depends. The right structure, leadership style, or control system varies with the size of the organization, the technology it uses, the stability of its environment, and the kind of work being done. A nuclear power plant requires tight, rule-based control; a creative ad agency requires loose, autonomous teams. Contingency thinking continues to shape how we teach leadership (situational leadership) and structure (mechanistic vs. organic).

Three more recent strands round out the picture. Total Quality Management (TQM), refined in post-war Japan by W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran, embeds continuous improvement and customer focus at every level — leading to lean manufacturing, Six Sigma, and ISO 9000. The learning organization, articulated by Peter Senge in The Fifth Discipline (1990), argues that in fast-moving environments the only durable competitive advantage is the ability to learn faster than competitors. And evidence-based management, advocated by Stanford's Jeffrey Pfeffer and Bob Sutton, urges managers to use the best available data, not fads or gut feel, when deciding how to run their organizations.

  • Systems theory: the organization as inputs → process → outputs, embedded in an environment.
  • Contingency theory: "it depends" — the right answer varies with size, technology, environment.
  • Total Quality Management (TQM): continuous improvement, customer focus, statistical thinking.
  • Learning organization: Senge's five disciplines — systems thinking, personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning.
  • Evidence-based management: use the best available data, not fads or gurus.

Case: Shopify decides between two organizational responses to rapid growth.

Option A (classical): hire more middle managers, standardize processes, write detailed playbooks. Option B (learning organization): create cross-functional squads, run regular retrospectives, encourage internal mobility.
A contingency analysis says: because Shopify operates in a fast-changing technology environment with creative knowledge work, the organic, learning-organization response is more likely to sustain innovation. Shopify, like Spotify and Netflix, chose B — and credits much of its scale to that choice.

  • Treating "systems" as just a metaphor. The discipline of systems thinking includes specific tools (feedback loops, causal-loop diagrams, stocks & flows) that change how you diagnose problems.
  • Reducing contingency theory to "every situation is unique." It actually offers concrete variables (environmental uncertainty, technology, size) that predict which structures and styles work.
  • Confusing TQM with one-off "quality initiatives." TQM is a continuous, organization-wide philosophy, not a project.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "compare and contrast major contemporary management approaches (Total Quality Management, learning organizations, lean management, evidence-based management)."

1.7 — Internal & External Environments and Organizational Culture
PEST/PESTLE analysis, the task environment, mission, vision, values, and the iceberg of culture.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Managers do not operate in a vacuum; every decision is constrained by internal conditions (the organization's own resources, structure, and culture) and shaped by an external environment (competitors, customers, regulations, technology, society). A useful scanning tool is the PEST framework — Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological — sometimes expanded to PESTLE by adding Legal and Environmental. Each letter prompts the manager to ask, "What is changing in the world, and how does it affect us?" In 2020, every Canadian retailer ran a PEST analysis on the pandemic: political (lockdown orders), economic (CERB transfers, recession), socio-cultural (work-from-home, e-commerce surge), technological (curbside pick-up apps).

Closer in is the task environment — the specific actors a firm interacts with daily: customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, labour unions, the local community. Michael Porter's Five Forces framework (rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, threat of substitutes, threat of new entrants) is the canonical tool for analysing the competitive part of this task environment. While Five Forces belongs more to strategic management, BOH4M students should understand it as a tool that turns external scanning into a structured map.

Internally, every organization has a culture — the shared values, beliefs, assumptions, and norms that shape how people behave. Edgar Schein's cultural iceberg model says only a small fraction is visible above the waterline (artifacts: dress code, office layout, slogans). Below the waterline are espoused values (the mission, vision, and value statements) and, deeper still, basic underlying assumptions (what people actually believe is true about customers, competitors, and each other). Strong cultures align all three levels; weak cultures have a glossy mission statement above the waterline and a cynical reality below it. Managers shape culture through the people they hire, the behaviour they reward, the stories they tell, and most powerfully, by the example they personally set.

  • Internal environment: the organization's own people, resources, structure, and culture.
  • External (general) environment: broader forces — political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal, environmental.
  • PEST / PESTLE: a structured scanning framework for the external environment.
  • Task environment: immediate actors — customers, competitors, suppliers, regulators.
  • Organizational culture: shared values, beliefs, and assumptions; Schein's iceberg has three layers (artifacts, espoused values, basic assumptions).
  • Mission / Vision / Values: the formal expression of what an organization does, aspires to, and stands for.

Case: PEST analysis for a Canadian electric-vehicle (EV) startup in 2026.

Political: federal ZEV mandate (100% new-vehicle sales must be zero-emission by 2035) → strong tailwind. Economic: high interest rates and recession risk depress big-ticket purchases → headwind. Socio-cultural: rising climate awareness among under-30 buyers → tailwind. Technological: battery costs dropping ~10%/year, but Canadian grid still mostly mid-emission → mixed. Conclusion: the regulatory and cultural environment favours EVs, but financing and infrastructure are bottlenecks the startup must explicitly plan around.

  • Treating PEST as a one-time exercise. The environment changes; PEST should be revisited at least annually.
  • Confusing mission (what the organization does today) with vision (what it aspires to become). They serve different purposes.
  • Believing culture is what is printed on the wall. Culture is what people actually do when no one is watching.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.3 — "analyse the impact of internal (mission, culture, resources) and external (economic, technological, socio-cultural, political–legal) factors on management decisions."
