3.1 — The Communication Process & Common Barriers
Shannon-Weaver model, encoding, channel, noise, decoding, feedback, and barriers to effective communication.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Mintzberg's research showed managers spend nearly 80% of their time communicating — speaking, listening, reading, writing — so understanding the communication process is foundational. The classic engineering model is Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver's 1949 framework, refined for organizational contexts. A sender with an idea encodes it into a message (words, gestures, images), transmits it through a channel (face-to-face conversation, email, video call, written report), the receiver decodes the message, and provides feedback. Everything that distorts or interrupts the message along the way is called noise.

Noise comes in several varieties. Physical noise is literal: a loud HVAC system, a poor video-call connection, a typo. Semantic noise is meaning-based: jargon the receiver does not know, ambiguous words, untranslated cultural references. Psychological noise includes preconceptions, mood, defensiveness, and selective listening (hearing only what we want to hear). And physiological noise includes fatigue, hunger, hearing impairment. Effective managers actively reduce each kind of noise — choosing quiet rooms, plain language, the right time of day, and the right channel for the message's complexity (face-to-face for emotional or complex topics; email for routine information).

Major barriers to communication include the filtering of bad news as it travels up the hierarchy (the "shoot the messenger" effect), information overload (receivers cannot process all incoming messages), emotional reactions that distort interpretation, cultural differences (high-context cultures like Japan rely more on non-verbals; low-context cultures like Germany rely on explicit words), language and gender differences, and nonverbal cues that contradict the spoken message. Albert Mehrabian's often-misquoted finding that "55% of meaning is non-verbal" applies only to emotional messages where verbal and non-verbal cues conflict; nonetheless, body language, tone, and facial expression carry real information and should align with the spoken words.

  • Communication process: sender → encode → channel → decode → receiver → feedback (with noise throughout).
  • Channel richness: face-to-face is richest; email is leanest; choose for message complexity.
  • Noise: physical, semantic, psychological, physiological.
  • Filtering: distortion as messages move up the chain of command.
  • Information overload: too many messages, too little capacity to process.
  • High-context vs. low-context cultures: implicit vs. explicit communication norms.

Case: A project manager sends a 12-paragraph email at 8 p.m. announcing schedule changes.

Outcomes by morning: half the team is confused, two members feel insulted by the implied criticism in paragraph 6, and a key change in paragraph 11 is missed entirely.
Diagnosis: wrong channel (email is too lean for complex changes), poor timing (late evening invites psychological noise), information overload (12 paragraphs is too dense), and lack of feedback loop (no opportunity to clarify).
Better approach: hold a 30-minute video meeting to explain the changes and answer questions, then follow up with a 4-bullet email summary, then ask each team lead to confirm in writing what they will do differently.

  • Defaulting to email for everything. Rich messages (negative feedback, complex change) need richer channels.
  • Assuming the message you intended is the message that was received. Always check with feedback.
  • Underestimating non-verbal mismatch. Saying "I'm fine" with crossed arms and a scowl tells the receiver something very different.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "describe the communication process (sender, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback, noise) and identify common barriers to effective communication."

3.2 — Communication Channels & Active Listening
Upward, downward, horizontal, diagonal channels; the grapevine; active-listening techniques.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Organizations carry information through both formal and informal channels. Downward communication flows from manager to subordinate — strategy announcements, instructions, performance feedback. Upward communication flows from subordinate to manager — status reports, concerns, suggestions, employee surveys. Horizontal (or lateral) communication flows between peers in the same level — coordination across departments. Diagonal communication crosses both levels and functions (a marketing analyst directly emailing the VP of Operations). Each channel has different speed, accuracy, and political weight.

Beyond these formal channels runs the grapevine — the informal network through which gossip, rumour, and unofficial information travel. Research suggests the grapevine is surprisingly accurate (70–90%) and remarkably fast. Smart managers do not try to eliminate the grapevine (impossible) but listen to it carefully and "feed" it with accurate information to crowd out rumours. The corollary of this is radio silence — when leadership stops communicating during a difficult time, the grapevine fills the vacuum with worst-case speculation.

The single most important communication skill for a manager is active listening. It is not the absence of speaking; it is a set of deliberate behaviours. Carl Rogers and Richard Farson's classic 1957 essay describes it. Pay attention — minimize distractions, make eye contact, lean in. Suspend judgment — listen to understand, not to plan your response. Reflect — paraphrase what you heard ("So what you're saying is…") to confirm understanding. Clarify — ask open-ended questions to dig deeper. Summarize — recap the conversation. Empathize — acknowledge the speaker's feelings without necessarily agreeing with their position. Active listening is the gold standard in performance conversations, conflict resolution, and customer service, and one of the highest-leverage skills a manager can develop.

  • Downward: top to bottom; strategy, policy, instructions, feedback.
  • Upward: bottom to top; reports, suggestions, complaints (often filtered).
  • Horizontal: peer to peer; coordination across departments.
  • Diagonal: across levels and functions; bypasses chain of command for speed.
  • Grapevine: informal, fast, 70–90% accurate; cannot be eliminated.
  • Active listening: attend, suspend judgment, reflect, clarify, summarize, empathize.

Case: Layoff rumours sweep a Canadian retailer.

The CEO writes a single all-staff email saying "no comment on rumours, focus on serving customers." Within 48 hours productivity drops 25% and several top performers begin job-searching.
Better approach: hold a town hall, acknowledge the rumour, explain what is and is not being considered, commit to a clear update date, and create an anonymous question channel. Whatever the actual decision turns out to be, the loss of trust caused by silence is usually larger than the loss caused by honesty.

  • Confusing hearing with listening. Hearing is automatic; listening requires deliberate attention.
  • Believing the grapevine can be silenced. It cannot — it can only be informed.
  • Interrupting to "fix" the problem during a listening conversation. The speaker often just needs to feel heard before they can solve.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "compare formal and informal communication channels (upward, downward, horizontal, diagonal, the grapevine) in organizations."

3.3 — The Rational Decision-Making Model
Eight-step rational model, bounded rationality, intuition, and biases in decisions.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Decision-making is the central act of management. The rational decision-making model assumes that a manager can identify the problem, gather all relevant information, generate all reasonable alternatives, evaluate them objectively, choose the best, implement it, and learn from the result. Most textbooks list seven or eight steps. A workable version: (1) identify and define the problem, (2) gather relevant data, (3) generate alternatives, (4) evaluate alternatives against criteria, (5) select the best alternative, (6) implement the decision, (7) monitor and evaluate results, (8) adjust if necessary.

The rational model is a useful ideal, but pioneering work by Nobel laureate Herbert Simon showed that real decision-making is constrained by bounded rationality: managers have limited time, limited information, and limited cognitive capacity. Instead of optimizing, they often satisfice — accepting the first alternative that meets a minimum threshold. Real-world decisions also rely heavily on intuition — pattern recognition built from experience that fires faster than conscious analysis (Gary Klein's "recognition-primed decision" model). Intuition is valuable in familiar, time-pressured domains but dangerous in novel ones.

Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky, and a generation of behavioural-economics researchers documented dozens of cognitive biases that distort decisions. The most important for managers are confirmation bias (seeking information that supports the prior view), anchoring (over-weighting the first number you see), availability heuristic (judging probability by what comes to mind easily), overconfidence (over-estimating one's own ability and information), and sunk-cost fallacy (continuing a failing project because of past investment). Effective managers cannot eliminate these biases but they can build practices that resist them: pre-mortems (imagine the project failed — why?), red-team reviews (assign someone to argue against the favoured option), and explicit consideration of disconfirming evidence.

  • Rational model: 8 steps from problem definition to monitoring.
  • Bounded rationality (Simon): real decisions are made under cognitive and time limits.
  • Satisficing: accepting "good enough" rather than optimizing.
  • Intuition: pattern-based fast thinking; valuable in familiar domains.
  • Biases: confirmation, anchoring, availability, overconfidence, sunk-cost.
  • De-biasing: pre-mortems, red teams, devil's advocate roles.

Case: A small-business owner deciding whether to launch a new product.

Step 1 Problem: Sales of the current product are flat; should we launch product B?
Step 2 Data: Current sales = $400K/year, margin 30%. Survey of 200 customers shows 35% interested in B. Required investment = $80K.
Step 3 Alternatives: (a) launch B fully, (b) launch B as a soft pilot, (c) extend product A with feature upgrades, (d) do nothing.
Step 4 Evaluate against criteria (financial return, risk, fit with brand): a soft pilot (b) scores best — modest risk, learns real demand.
Step 5 Choose: (b) with a $20K pilot budget.
Step 6 Implement: pilot launches in two stores for 90 days.
Step 7 Monitor: sales conversion, customer feedback, cost-to-serve.
Step 8 Adjust: if pilot conversion exceeds 8%, scale to all stores; if below 3%, pause and reassess.

  • Spending all effort on step 5 (choosing) and skipping step 1 (defining the right problem). Most bad decisions are answers to the wrong question.
  • Treating intuition as either always right or always wrong. It is reliable in familiar, fast-feedback environments and unreliable in novel ones.
  • Ignoring sunk-cost fallacy. "We've already invested $2M; we have to finish" is irrelevant if the next $1M won't pay off.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "describe the steps of the rational decision-making model (identify problem, gather data, generate alternatives, evaluate, choose, implement, follow up)."

3.4 — Group Decision-Making: Brainstorming, Delphi, Nominal Group, Groupthink
When groups are better than individuals; techniques to maximize benefits and avoid Janis's groupthink.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Groups can outperform individuals in decision quality — but only with the right structure. Group decisions benefit from more information, more perspectives, better acceptance by those who must implement them, and collective creativity. They suffer from slower decisions, conformity pressure, domination by a few voices, and diffusion of responsibility. Several techniques have been developed to capture the upside while controlling the downside.

Brainstorming, developed by ad executive Alex Osborn in 1942, asks the group to generate as many ideas as possible without judging them in real time. The four rules: defer judgment, encourage wild ideas, build on others' ideas, and aim for quantity. Research shows that individual brainstorming followed by group sharing generates more and better ideas than pure group brainstorming, where productivity loss to social inhibition is real.

The Delphi technique, developed at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s, uses anonymous, iterative rounds of expert input. Experts answer a questionnaire, results are summarized and shared, experts revise, and the process repeats until convergence. Anonymity removes status effects; iteration allows experts to learn from each other without one strong voice dominating. The nominal group technique (NGT) blends individual and group work in one meeting: members silently write ideas, take turns sharing without discussion, discuss for clarification only, then vote privately. This dramatically reduces the social-pressure problem.

The danger of group decision-making is groupthink, identified by Yale psychologist Irving Janis after studying U.S. foreign-policy disasters (the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961, escalation in Vietnam). When a cohesive group prizes harmony above honest analysis, it can produce confident but disastrous decisions. Symptoms include the illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalization, stereotyping of outsiders, self-censorship, the illusion of unanimity, and "mindguards" who shield the group from disconfirming information. Antidotes include actively encouraging dissent (a designated devil's advocate), bringing in outside experts, breaking into sub-groups to debate alternatives, and reviewing decisions after a "second-chance" meeting.

  • Brainstorming: defer judgment, build on ideas, quantity over quality.
  • Delphi technique: anonymous, iterative expert rounds; removes status effects.
  • Nominal group technique: silent writing, structured sharing, private voting.
  • Groupthink (Janis): cohesive group prizes harmony over reality; produces bad decisions.
  • Antidotes: devil's advocate, outside experts, sub-groups, second-chance meetings.

Case: A school district decides whether to adopt a controversial new curriculum.

Plan A: a single large meeting of 25 administrators. Outcome: three loud voices dominate, the superintendent's preference becomes clear, dissenters self-censor, decision is unanimous in favour. Six months later, parent backlash forces a reversal.
Better plan B: distribute background documents one week in advance; convene three sub-groups of 8, each charged with finding strongest objections; rotate sub-group leaders; use NGT for final ranking; appoint one administrator as designated devil's advocate; defer final vote by one week ("second-chance meeting"). Most groupthink failures could have been prevented by these straightforward design choices.

  • Believing groups always outperform individuals. Without structure, groups generate fewer good ideas than the same people working alone.
  • Confusing consensus with agreement. Genuine consensus comes after honest disagreement is aired and considered.
  • Skipping the second-chance meeting because "we already decided." A 48-hour cooling-off period catches many flaws.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "compare individual and group decision-making, including the benefits and pitfalls of brainstorming, Delphi technique, nominal-group technique, and the risk of groupthink."

3.5 — Conflict in Organizations: Thomas-Kilmann Styles
Sources of conflict (task, relationship, process, value) and the five conflict-handling styles.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Conflict is unavoidable wherever people work together — and not all conflict is bad. Researchers distinguish four types. Task conflict is disagreement about the content and goals of work; in moderation it improves decision quality. Relationship conflict is interpersonal tension and animosity; it is almost always destructive. Process conflict is disagreement about how work should be done. Value conflict arises from differences in fundamental beliefs or priorities. The leader's job is not to eliminate all conflict but to channel task and process conflict constructively while preventing relationship and value conflicts from poisoning the team.

The most widely taught framework for managing conflict is the Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI), published by Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann in 1974. They map five styles on two dimensions: assertiveness (concern for one's own outcomes) and cooperativeness (concern for the other party's outcomes). The five styles are: Competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness — win/lose), Collaborating (high/high — win/win), Compromising (medium/medium — split the difference), Avoiding (low/low — withdraw), and Accommodating (low/high — give in to preserve the relationship).

No single style is universally "best"; effective conflict managers choose strategically. Competing fits emergencies and ethical lines. Collaborating fits important issues with time to work them through. Compromising fits time-pressured situations with roughly equal power. Avoiding fits trivial issues, or when emotions need to cool. Accommodating fits issues that matter more to the other party. The error most students make is treating their preferred style as universally appropriate; the better practice is to diagnose the situation first.

  • Task conflict: healthy in moderation; improves decisions.
  • Relationship conflict: personal animosity; almost always destructive.
  • Process / value conflict: about how, or about fundamental beliefs.
  • Competing (forcing): high assertiveness, low cooperativeness — win/lose.
  • Collaborating: high/high — win/win, the gold standard for important issues.
  • Compromising: medium/medium — split the difference.
  • Avoiding: low/low — withdraw or postpone.
  • Accommodating: low/high — yield to preserve the relationship.

Case: A retail manager faces three conflicts on the same Tuesday.

9:00 — A staff member shouts at a customer (safety/ethics issue). The right style is Competing — intervene immediately, end the behaviour, no negotiation.
11:00 — Two senior associates disagree about how to redesign the floor plan. The right style is Collaborating — schedule a 60-minute session, surface assumptions, design jointly.
14:00 — A new hire complains the schedule rotation is unfair, but it is the established norm that the team prefers. The right style is Accommodating — adjust this hire's schedule for the first month rather than overhaul the rotation, preserving team norms while supporting the newcomer.
Same manager, three styles, all appropriate.

  • Treating all conflict as bad. Task and process conflict in moderation improve performance.
  • Defaulting to one style (often avoiding or competing) as a personality habit rather than a diagnostic choice.
  • Confusing compromise with collaboration. Compromise = each gives something up. Collaboration = creates a solution better than either initial position.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.3 — "identify common sources of workplace conflict (task, relationship, process, value) and apply Thomas–Kilmann conflict-handling styles (competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating)."

3.6 — Workplace Stress & Mental Health
Sources, signs, and management of stress; the manager's role in psychological safety.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Workplace stress is the body's response to demands. Hans Selye, the Hungarian-Canadian endocrinologist working at McGill and the Université de Montréal, coined the modern medical use of the word and distinguished eustress (positive, motivating stress that energizes performance) from distress (chronic, depleting stress that harms health and work). The relationship between arousal and performance is captured by the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U curve: too little stress and we are bored and unfocused; too much and we are overwhelmed; somewhere in the middle is optimal.

Common sources of workplace stress include workload (too much or too little), role ambiguity / role conflict (unclear expectations or competing demands), poor relationships with managers or peers, lack of control / autonomy, job insecurity, and poor work-life balance. Signs that someone is in distress include changes in mood (irritability, withdrawal), changes in performance (errors, missed deadlines), changes in attendance (lateness, absences), and physical signs (fatigue, headaches, weight change). Research from the Mental Health Commission of Canada estimates that workplace mental-health problems cost Canadian employers about $50 billion per year in absenteeism, presenteeism, and disability.

Managers cannot — and should not — try to be clinicians. But they can shape the conditions under which stress is generated and respond well when employees struggle. The National Standard of Canada for Psychological Health and Safety in the Workplace (CSA-Z1003) identifies 13 factors managers should attend to, including civility and respect, psychological support, clear leadership, growth and development, recognition, involvement and influence, and protection of physical safety. Practical actions include realistic workloads, clear priorities, regular check-ins, paid mental-health days, employee assistance programs (EAPs), training in psychological first aid, and modelling the behaviour (taking your own vacation, not emailing at midnight). Amy Edmondson's concept of psychological safety — feeling safe to speak up, admit mistakes, and ask questions — has emerged as one of the strongest predictors of both team performance and individual well-being.

  • Eustress vs. distress: short-term/motivating vs. chronic/harmful.
  • Yerkes-Dodson: inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance.
  • Stressors: workload, role ambiguity/conflict, relationships, low control, job insecurity, work-life imbalance.
  • Signs: mood, performance, attendance, physical health changes.
  • CSA-Z1003: Canadian National Standard for Psychological Health and Safety; 13 factors.
  • Psychological safety (Edmondson): the most reliable correlate of team performance.

Case: A high-performing analyst is missing deadlines and snapping at colleagues.

Poor manager response: "Pull yourself together — the work is suffering." Outcome: analyst quits within two months, taking institutional knowledge to a competitor.
Better manager response: schedule a private check-in, lead with concern ("I've noticed you seem stressed — how are you doing?"), listen without judgment, offer concrete options (EAP, temporary workload reduction, paid mental-health day, deadline extension), and follow up in two weeks. Most employees in early distress can recover with relatively small accommodations; the cost of acting early is a small fraction of the cost of replacing them.

  • Confusing being busy with being productive. Chronic overload reduces both performance and health.
  • Assuming high performers don't need check-ins. They often hide distress better and crash harder.
  • Treating mental health as a personal problem outside the manager's responsibility. The CSA standard makes it explicitly a workplace duty.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "describe the causes, signs, and consequences of workplace stress and identify strategies organizations use to support employee well-being and mental health."

3.7 — Managing Change: Lewin and Kotter
Lewin's three-step model, Kotter's eight steps, and why people resist change.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every organization changes — technology shifts, markets move, leadership turns over, regulations evolve. The change-management literature offers two foundational frameworks. Kurt Lewin's three-step model (1947) describes change as unfreeze → change → refreeze. The unfreeze stage creates motivation by surfacing dissatisfaction with the status quo. The change stage introduces the new way — new structures, processes, behaviours. The refreeze stage stabilizes the change by embedding it in routines, incentives, and culture so the organization does not slide back. Lewin paired this with a force-field analysis: diagram the driving forces in favour of change against the restraining forces resisting it; either strengthen drivers or weaken restrainers.

John Kotter's eight-step model, articulated in his 1995 HBR article and 1996 book Leading Change, is the most widely taught practical framework. The steps: (1) create a sense of urgency, (2) build a guiding coalition, (3) form a strategic vision and initiatives, (4) enlist a volunteer army, (5) enable action by removing barriers, (6) generate short-term wins, (7) sustain acceleration, (8) institute change (anchor it in culture). Kotter found that most failed change efforts skip step 1 (declaring urgency before people feel it) or step 6 (running out of energy before short-term wins appear).

People resist change for predictable reasons: fear of the unknown, loss of control, loss of status or competence (especially senior employees whose deep expertise becomes less relevant), concern about increased workload, distrust of leadership, and genuine disagreement about the proposed change. Leaders who treat resistance as obstinacy miss valuable information; resistance often signals legitimate concerns about implementation that should reshape the plan. Effective change leaders communicate the "why" first, involve the affected people in designing the "how," celebrate small wins visibly, and accept that change takes longer than the leader hopes — typically eighteen months to two years to anchor a major change in a mid-sized organization.

  • Lewin's three steps: unfreeze → change → refreeze.
  • Force-field analysis: diagram driving and restraining forces.
  • Kotter's 8 steps: urgency, coalition, vision, army, enable, wins, accelerate, anchor.
  • Sources of resistance: fear, loss of control, loss of status, workload, distrust, disagreement.
  • Time horizon: major change typically requires 18–24 months to anchor.

Case: Migrating an organization to a new ERP system using Kotter's framework.

(1) Urgency — show CFO data: the old system fails twice a year and creates $200K in errors. (2) Coalition — assemble a steering group of CFO, IT director, two operations managers, and a frontline supervisor. (3) Vision — "by Q4 next year, every department uses a single shared system with real-time reporting." (4) Army — recruit one super-user per department. (5) Enable — fund training, replace old hardware, simplify forms. (6) Short-term wins — at month 3 publish "month-end close was 4 days faster." (7) Accelerate — extend to international offices. (8) Anchor — bake the new system into hiring, onboarding, and annual reviews. Skipping any step risks the whole transformation failing.

  • Skipping the "urgency" step. People do not change when comfortable. The leader must surface why now.
  • Stopping before "anchoring." Many transformations slide back when leadership attention moves on.
  • Treating resistance as enemy rather than information. Listening to resistors often improves the plan.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "apply change-management models (Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze; Kotter's eight steps) to a real-world change scenario and explain why employees resist change."
