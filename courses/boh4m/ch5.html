5.1 — The Control Process: Four Steps
Establish standards → measure performance → compare → take corrective action; feed-forward, concurrent, feedback controls.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Controlling is the management function that closes the loop on planning. Without controlling, planning is hopeful guessing. The classic four-step control process: (1) establish standards, (2) measure actual performance, (3) compare performance to standard, (4) take corrective action. The standard is what should happen — a sales target, a quality threshold, a budget. Measurement requires data — sometimes easy (units shipped), sometimes harder (employee engagement). Comparison may be against a numerical standard, a budget, a benchmark, or a prior period. Corrective action might mean changing performance, changing the standard, or recognizing that nothing should be done.

Control happens at three points in time. Feed-forward (or preventive) control happens before the work, anticipating problems — hiring qualified people, calibrating equipment, training employees in advance, preflight checklists in aviation. Concurrent (or steering) control happens during the work — supervisory observation, live dashboards, real-time quality checks on a production line. Feedback control happens after — month-end financial reporting, customer-satisfaction surveys, performance reviews. Effective managers use all three. Feedback control alone is too slow — you find out you missed your target after it is too late to fix it.

Control is most effective when it is future-oriented (improves the next cycle), multidimensional (avoids over-rewarding one metric at the expense of others — Goodhart's Law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"), cost-effective (the cost of the control is less than what it saves), and understandable to those it controls. Excessive control alienates employees and stifles innovation; absent control invites drift and error. The art is in calibrating the tightness.

  • Step 1: establish standards — specific, measurable, time-bound.
  • Step 2: measure performance — gather the right data, at the right frequency.
  • Step 3: compare to standard — variance is interesting, not the absolute.
  • Step 4: take corrective action — change performance, change standard, or do nothing.
  • Feed-forward: before the work; preventive.
  • Concurrent: during the work; steering.
  • Feedback: after the work; learning.
  • Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Case: A coffee shop's quality control.

Standard: every espresso shot pulled in 25–30 seconds, with at least 25 mL of crema-rich liquid.
Feed-forward: every barista trained on the 25/30 standard before serving customers; grinder calibrated every morning.
Concurrent: shift supervisor randomly tests one shot per hour; the espresso machine itself flashes if the shot is outside 22–32 s.
Feedback: weekly secret-shopper report scores espresso quality; monthly customer-satisfaction dashboard.
Corrective action: if shots are running long, recalibrate the grinder (changes performance); if customers consistently want longer shots, consider revising the standard.

  • Relying only on feedback control. You find out about problems too late.
  • Falling into Goodhart's Law: hyper-optimizing one metric (e.g., calls handled per hour) and ignoring quality.
  • Designing controls that cost more than the problem they prevent. Controls should be cost-effective.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "describe the four steps of the control process (establish standards, measure performance, compare to standard, take corrective action)."

5.2 — Financial & Operational Controls
Budgets, variance analysis, ratio analysis, breakeven analysis, TQM, Six Sigma, ISO 9000.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Financial controls use accounting data to ensure the organization is meeting its targets. A budget is the most common control tool — a quantified plan covering revenues, expenses, capital, and cash for a future period (monthly, quarterly, annually). Variance analysis compares budget to actual: a favourable variance means actual is better than budgeted; unfavourable means worse. Variance has two components: volume (sold more or fewer units than planned) and price/rate (charged or paid different unit price than planned). Drilling into variance helps managers identify root causes rather than treating symptoms.

Ratio analysis distills financial statements into a handful of meaningful numbers. Liquidity ratios like the current ratio = current assets / current liabilities (good if > 1.5) measure short-term ability to pay bills. Profitability ratios like gross margin = (revenue − cost of goods sold) / revenue, net margin = net income / revenue, and return on assets (ROA) = net income / total assets measure how efficiently the firm earns from its resources. Leverage ratios like debt-to-equity = total debt / total equity measure risk from borrowing. Breakeven analysis answers "how many units must we sell to cover costs?" with the formula \(\text{Breakeven units} = \dfrac{\text{Fixed costs}}{\text{Price per unit} - \text{Variable cost per unit}}\).

Operational controls focus on processes and quality. Total Quality Management (TQM), popularized by W. Edwards Deming and adopted dramatically in post-war Japan, embeds continuous improvement (kaizen), customer focus, and statistical control of variation into every level of the organization. Six Sigma, originated at Motorola and famously adopted at GE under Jack Welch, uses the DMAIC methodology (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control) and aspires to fewer than 3.4 defects per million opportunities. ISO 9000 is the international family of quality-management standards; certification requires documented procedures and audited compliance. Lean manufacturing (originating at Toyota) attacks waste in seven forms (transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects). All four traditions share an emphasis on data, process discipline, and improvement loops.

  • Budget: the quantified plan; the foundation of financial control.
  • Variance analysis: compare actual to budget; understand the gap.
  • Current ratio: current assets / current liabilities; liquidity.
  • Gross / net margin: profitability per dollar of revenue.
  • Breakeven: Fixed costs / (Price − Variable cost per unit).
  • TQM: continuous improvement, customer focus, statistical thinking.
  • Six Sigma: DMAIC; < 3.4 defects per million opportunities.
  • ISO 9000: international quality-management certification.
  • Lean: attacks waste in seven forms.

Case: Breakeven analysis for a vending-machine business.

Fixed costs (rent on machines, monthly maintenance): $3,000/month.
Variable cost per drink (wholesale + ingredients): $1.20.
Selling price per drink: $2.50.
Contribution margin per drink = \(\$2.50 - \$1.20 = \$1.30\).
Breakeven units \(= \dfrac{\$3{,}000}{\$1.30} \approx 2{,}308\) drinks per month.
If the business currently sells 2,000 drinks per month, it is losing money at $400 below breakeven. Possible actions: raise price (changes contribution margin), reduce variable cost (better wholesale deal), reduce fixed cost (negotiate rent), or grow sales volume (better placement, promotion).

  • Setting budgets so loose they teach nothing. A budget every department comfortably beats is not a control.
  • Treating ratios in isolation. A high current ratio could mean strong liquidity or wasted idle cash. Context matters.
  • Confusing TQM (organization-wide philosophy) with one-time "quality projects." TQM is a culture, not an event.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "compare financial controls (budgets, ratio analysis), operational controls (TQM, Six Sigma, ISO 9000), and strategic controls (balanced scorecard)."

5.3 — Strategic Control: The Balanced Scorecard
Kaplan and Norton's four perspectives — financial, customer, internal process, learning & growth.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Traditional financial controls have a glaring weakness: they are entirely lagging indicators — they tell you about the past, not the future. In 1992 Harvard's Robert Kaplan and David Norton proposed the Balanced Scorecard, a strategic-control framework that tracks performance across four perspectives chosen to balance financial outcomes with the activities that produce them. The four perspectives ask, "to succeed financially, how should we appear to shareholders?" (Financial), "to achieve our vision, how should we appear to customers?" (Customer), "to satisfy shareholders and customers, what business processes must we excel at?" (Internal Process), and "to achieve our vision, how will we sustain our ability to change and improve?" (Learning & Growth).

Each perspective has a small number of objectives, each with specific metrics, targets, and initiatives. The classic example is a quarterly executive review showing 4–6 metrics per perspective, with each metric coloured green/amber/red. Importantly, the scorecard hypothesizes cause-and-effect chains: investments in employee learning (L&G) produce better processes (Internal), which produce happier customers (Customer), which produce stronger financial returns (Financial). Tracking and testing these chains is what makes the scorecard a strategic tool, not just a dashboard.

Balanced Scorecard adoption is widespread among Canadian Crown corporations, hospitals, school boards, and large publicly-traded firms. The Government of Ontario uses scorecard-style reporting in many ministries. Public-sector variants often add a fifth perspective — Community / Stakeholder — recognizing that public bodies serve more than shareholders. Some firms have moved beyond Kaplan & Norton to objectives-and-key-results (OKRs) or "OneStrategy" pages, but the balanced-scorecard philosophy — that strategic control must include leading indicators across multiple dimensions — has become embedded in mainstream management practice.

  • Financial perspective: shareholder returns, profitability, growth.
  • Customer perspective: satisfaction, retention, market share.
  • Internal-process perspective: cycle time, defect rate, innovation cycle.
  • Learning & growth perspective: employee engagement, training hours, leadership pipeline.
  • Cause-and-effect chain: L&G → Process → Customer → Financial.
  • Leading vs. lagging indicators: scorecards mix both deliberately.

Case: Scorecard for an Ontario community college's annual review.

Financial: tuition revenue, endowment growth, cost per credit hour.
Customer (student): graduate employment rate, student satisfaction (NSSE), retention from year 1 to 2.
Internal process: program-renewal cycle time, classroom-utilization rate, percent of accredited programs.
Learning & growth: faculty professional-development hours, percent of faculty with terminal degree, employee engagement score.
Cause-and-effect: investing in faculty development (L&G) leads to better program quality (Process), better student employment outcomes (Customer), and stronger enrolment and tuition revenue (Financial).

  • Treating the scorecard as a passive dashboard. Without management discussion of the cause-effect chains, it loses strategic power.
  • Including too many metrics. Best practice is 4–6 per perspective; over 30 total becomes noise.
  • Forgetting Learning & Growth. Many scorecards skimp on the perspective that drives the future of the other three.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "compare financial controls (budgets, ratio analysis), operational controls (TQM, Six Sigma, ISO 9000), and strategic controls (balanced scorecard)."

5.4 — Business Ethics & Ethical Decision-Making
Ethics vs. legality; utilitarian, rights, justice, virtue frameworks; codes of ethics; whistleblowing.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Business ethics is the discipline of distinguishing right from wrong in business decisions. It is broader than legal compliance — many things are legal but unethical (paying below a living wage in a high-cost city), and a few things are illegal but defensible (whistleblowing in violation of an NDA). The most useful frame for students is to treat law and ethics as overlapping but distinct circles: the most defensible business decisions clearly sit in the overlap; problems arise in the asymmetric zones.

Four classic ethical frameworks help managers reason through hard decisions. Utilitarian ethics (Mill, Bentham) asks which action produces the greatest good for the greatest number; it is the dominant frame in economics and policy analysis but can override individual rights. Rights-based ethics (Kant, modern human-rights theory) asks whether the action respects fundamental rights of all stakeholders; it is the frame behind privacy laws and the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Justice-based ethics (Rawls) asks whether the action distributes benefits and burdens fairly, particularly to the least advantaged; it underlies progressive taxation, employment equity, and accessibility legislation. Virtue ethics (Aristotle) asks "what would a person of good character do?" — focusing on the actor's habits and integrity rather than rules or consequences. Most real ethical decisions become clearer when examined through all four frames; convergent answers carry high confidence, divergent ones flag genuine moral complexity.

Organizations operationalize ethics through codes of ethics (formal written principles), codes of conduct (specific behavioural rules), ethics training, ethics hotlines, and dedicated ethics officers. The federal Public Servants Disclosure Protection Act (PSDPA) and Ontario's whistleblower protections in the Securities Act protect employees who report wrongdoing in good faith. The 2008 Sarbanes–Oxley-equivalent provisions in Ontario require CEOs and CFOs of public companies to certify the accuracy of financial statements personally. Strong ethical cultures share three features: visible leadership commitment (the "tone at the top"), psychological safety to raise concerns (no retaliation), and consistency between stated values and how the organization actually rewards and punishes.

  • Ethics ≠ law: overlapping but distinct; some legal acts are unethical and vice versa.
  • Utilitarian: greatest good for the greatest number.
  • Rights-based (Kant): respect fundamental rights of all stakeholders.
  • Justice-based (Rawls): fair distribution, especially to the least advantaged.
  • Virtue ethics (Aristotle): what would a person of good character do?
  • Code of ethics vs. code of conduct: principles vs. specific behaviours.
  • Whistleblowing: protected under federal PSDPA and provincial securities laws.

Case: A pharmaceutical sales rep is encouraged to push off-label uses of a medication that is approved by Health Canada only for one condition.

Legal lens: Off-label promotion is illegal under the Food and Drugs Act.
Utilitarian: Some patients might benefit, but many will be harmed by uncertain efficacy and side effects → action probably reduces overall welfare.
Rights-based: Patients have a right to evidence-based care and to informed consent → action violates their right to truthful information.
Justice-based: Off-label patients pay the same insurance premiums but receive less-validated care → unfair distribution.
Virtue ethics: Would a person of integrity do this? Almost certainly no — the behaviour is incompatible with honesty and care for vulnerable patients.
All four frames converge; the action is clearly unethical. The rep's options include declining the assignment, raising it with the ethics officer, or — if internal channels fail — filing a complaint with Health Canada under whistleblower protections.

  • Equating "ethical" with "legal." The most damaging corporate scandals (Enron, Volkswagen, Theranos) were ethical failures dressed up as technically legal manoeuvres.
  • Using only utilitarian reasoning. It is powerful but can justify violating individual rights for aggregate benefit.
  • Treating codes of ethics as decorative. Without enforcement and lived example, they erode trust rather than build it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "distinguish between business ethics, legal compliance, and corporate social responsibility (CSR); apply a recognized ethical framework (utilitarian, rights, justice, virtue) to a business dilemma."

5.5 — Corporate Social Responsibility & the Triple Bottom Line
Carroll's pyramid, stakeholder theory, ESG investing, B Corps, sustainability reporting.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) describes a firm's voluntary obligations to society beyond legal compliance. Archie Carroll's CSR pyramid (1991) layers four responsibilities from base to apex: economic (be profitable), legal (obey the law), ethical (do what is right, fair, and just), and philanthropic (be a good corporate citizen). The pyramid claims a firm cannot meet higher responsibilities until lower ones are secure — a money-losing firm cannot fund philanthropy — but it also insists the top layers are real obligations, not afterthoughts.

The competing view in the 1970s, articulated by Nobel laureate Milton Friedman in his famous 1970 New York Times essay, held that "the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits" — within legal and ethical custom. The modern consensus has moved decisively beyond Friedman. Stakeholder theory, articulated by R. Edward Freeman in 1984, argues that firms have obligations to all groups affected by their operations — customers, employees, suppliers, communities, governments, the environment — not just shareholders. The famous 2019 Business Roundtable statement signed by 181 U.S. CEOs explicitly redefined corporate purpose along stakeholder lines, marking the formal end of the shareholder-primacy era.

The Triple Bottom Line (TBL), coined by John Elkington in 1994, captures this expanded accountability in three words: People, Planet, Profit. Modern ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) investing applies the TBL to capital markets — most Canadian pension funds, including CPP Investments and OTPP, now apply ESG screens. Certified B Corporations (B Lab certification, including Canadian B Corps such as Patagonia, Beau's Brewing, Bullfrog Power) commit to balancing all three. Sustainability reporting frameworks like the GRI Standards, SASB, and TCFD are now embedded in many publicly-traded firms' annual reporting. The fundamental shift is that what was once optional "good citizenship" is becoming standard practice — particularly for firms wanting access to ESG-screened capital and Gen-Z talent.

  • Carroll's CSR pyramid: Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic.
  • Friedman doctrine (1970): profit is the social responsibility, within law and ethics.
  • Stakeholder theory (Freeman): obligations to customers, employees, suppliers, community, environment.
  • Triple Bottom Line: People, Planet, Profit.
  • ESG: Environmental, Social, Governance investment screens.
  • B Corporation: certified balance of purpose and profit.
  • Sustainability reporting: GRI, SASB, TCFD frameworks.

Case: A Canadian apparel firm decides between two suppliers.

Supplier A — Bangladesh, $4.00/shirt, no third-party labour audit, weak environmental controls.
Supplier B — Sri Lanka, $5.20/shirt, SA8000 social-accountability certified, mid-tier environmental compliance.
Pure Friedman view: choose A — maximizes short-term margin.
Triple-bottom-line view: choose B — better outcomes for workers (People), modest planet improvement, modest reduction in profit margin (Profit). Risk-adjusted, B may actually outperform: a Rana-Plaza-style supplier incident at A could destroy brand value worth far more than the $1.20/shirt savings. Many Canadian retailers (Lululemon, Roots) have published supplier-audit standards explicitly along these lines after lessons from the 2013 collapse.

  • Treating CSR as charity / philanthropy only. Carroll's pyramid puts philanthropy at the top of a much broader stack.
  • Treating profit and ethics as zero-sum. Substantial evidence shows well-implemented ESG firms outperform peers risk-adjusted over the long run.
  • "Greenwashing" — claiming sustainability commitments not backed by audited action. Reporting frameworks like GRI exist precisely to combat this.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "analyse the triple-bottom-line (people, planet, profit) and stakeholder theory; evaluate a company's CSR report or sustainability initiative."

5.6 — The Culminating Leadership Case Study
Investigation design, evidence gathering, analysis, recommendations, written report, and oral presentation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The culminating activity in BOH4M is a Leadership Case Study Investigation worth 15% of the final mark, in which students choose a real organization or leadership situation and produce a substantial analysis integrating concepts from all four strands. The work is typically completed over 4–6 weeks at the end of the course, replaces several short performance tasks, and is assessed across all four achievement-chart categories.

A complete investigation moves through six stages. (1) Define: choose an organization or leadership situation, formulate a focused research question (e.g., "How did Patagonia's leadership reconcile environmental commitments with international supply-chain complexity?"). (2) Plan: identify the primary sources (interviews, surveys, company filings, annual reports, journalism, industry analyses), the management frameworks you will apply (e.g., Mintzberg's roles, transformational-leadership four I's, Kotter change steps, Carroll's CSR pyramid), and the timeline. (3) Collect: gather evidence systematically; document sources; respect intellectual-property and privacy rules. (4) Analyse: apply the chosen frameworks; identify strengths, weaknesses, and tensions; develop the central argument. (5) Synthesize and recommend: draw conclusions; offer concrete recommendations grounded in the analysis. (6) Communicate: produce a 3,000–4,500-word written report (with proper APA-style citations and a bibliography) and a 12–15-minute oral presentation with visuals to the class.

Common pitfalls and how to avoid them. Choosing a subject too broad ("How do good companies lead?") prevents focus — narrow to one organization and one tension. Listing frameworks without applying them ("Mintzberg's 10 roles include…") is descriptive, not analytical — apply each framework to the specific evidence. Single-source research (one news article, one Wikipedia page) is too thin — aim for at least 8–12 credible sources of varied type. Recommendations untethered from analysis read as opinion — every recommendation should reference specific evidence and frameworks. Treating the oral presentation as a slide reading wastes the format — the talk should add value beyond the written report through storytelling, visuals, and audience engagement.

Assessment of the culminating investigation uses the achievement-chart rubric: K/U (accurate use of management concepts), Thinking & Inquiry (depth of analysis, originality, judgment), Communication (clarity of written and oral expression), and Application (transfer of frameworks to a real-world situation). Strong work shows the ability to step beyond the textbook — to identify genuine tensions in management practice, to argue a thesis, and to support recommendations with evidence and reasoning.

  • Six-stage process: Define → Plan → Collect → Analyse → Synthesize → Communicate.
  • Focused research question: one organization, one tension, one thesis.
  • Frameworks: apply multiple from across the four strands.
  • Sources: 8–12 credible, varied; APA citations.
  • Deliverables: 3,000–4,500-word report + 12–15-minute oral presentation.
  • Assessment: all four achievement-chart categories, equally weighted.

Example research questions that are appropriately scoped:

• "How does Shopify's distributed-by-default working model since 2020 affect the four functions of management, and what tensions has it created for first-line managers?"
• "How did MEC's transition from a member co-operative to private-equity ownership (2020–2026) change its leadership style, organizational structure, and stakeholder relationships?"
• "What change-management approach did Air Canada use to recover from the 2020–2022 pandemic shock, and how can it be analyzed through Kotter's eight-step model?"
Each question names one organization, one specific phenomenon, and a clear analytical lens — ready for 4–6 weeks of evidence-grounded analysis.

  • Choosing a "favourite company" without identifying a tension to analyse. The investigation needs a problem worth investigating, not a fan letter.
  • Relying on the company's own marketing materials as the primary source. Use independent journalism, industry analyses, regulatory filings.
  • Treating the oral presentation as a duplicate of the report. The talk should add narrative, examples, and audience engagement that the written report does not.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D4.1 — "design and carry out a culminating leadership case-study investigation that integrates strands A–D and communicate the findings in a written report and an oral presentation."
