2.1 — Leadership vs. Management & Lewin's Styles
Kotter's distinction, sources of power (French & Raven), and Lewin's autocratic / democratic / laissez-faire continuum.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

"Management is doing things right; leadership is doing the right things." That oft-quoted line, attributed to Peter Drucker, captures a real distinction. Harvard's John Kotter, in his influential 1990 essay What Leaders Really Do, separates the two functions cleanly: management produces order and consistency through planning, budgeting, organizing, and controlling; leadership produces change and movement through vision, alignment, and motivation. Both are essential — pure management without leadership stagnates, pure leadership without management is chaos. Many of the best executives are both managers and leaders, switching modes depending on what the situation requires.

Leadership rests on power. The classic taxonomy is French & Raven's (1959) five bases of power: legitimate (the formal authority of the role), reward (the ability to give bonuses, promotions, recognition), coercive (the ability to punish), expert (recognized knowledge or skill), and referent (personal charisma — others want to identify with you). Effective leaders rely more on expert and referent power and less on coercive power, because the first two endure while the third generates resentment and resistance.

The earliest empirical work on leadership style was done by Kurt Lewin and colleagues at the University of Iowa in 1939 with groups of boys building model airplanes. They identified three styles. Autocratic leaders make decisions alone and tell the group what to do — efficient in a crisis or with inexperienced staff but stifling over time. Democratic leaders share decision-making and seek input — slower but builds buy-in and develops people. Laissez-faire leaders give the group complete freedom — works only with highly skilled, self-motivated experts. Most modern frameworks treat these as a continuum rather than three boxes, and the answer to "which is best?" is contingency: it depends.

  • Management vs. leadership (Kotter): management produces order; leadership produces change.
  • Five bases of power (French & Raven): legitimate, reward, coercive, expert, referent.
  • Autocratic: leader decides; works for crisis, novice staff.
  • Democratic (participative): leader and team decide together; builds engagement.
  • Laissez-faire: team decides; works for highly skilled experts.

Case: A hospital nurse-manager during a Code Blue (cardiac arrest).

During the crisis: she gives sharp, directive orders — "compressions Jamil, airway Sarah, epi 1 mg" — pure autocratic, because seconds matter and trained staff need a clear single voice. After the patient is stabilized she debriefs: "What did we do well? What could improve next time?" — pure democratic, because the team has expertise and learning improves the next response. The same leader uses both styles within thirty minutes — that is contingency leadership in action.

  • Treating "management" and "leadership" as opposites. They are complementary; most managers must do both.
  • Believing democratic is always best. In a fire, an autocratic order saves lives; democratic deliberation kills.
  • Equating laissez-faire with neglect. True laissez-faire is a deliberate choice to empower expert teams, not abdication.

2.2 — Trait, Behavioural & Contingency Theories
The Great Man theory, Ohio State and Michigan studies, Blake & Mouton's Managerial Grid, Fiedler, and Hersey & Blanchard.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The earliest scholarly leadership theory was the Great Man / trait theory of the late nineteenth century: leaders are born, not made, and they share a fixed set of traits — intelligence, dominance, energy, integrity. Decades of research have produced a more modest list (the "Big Five" personality traits, plus emotional intelligence, integrity, and ambition all correlate weakly to moderately with leader emergence), but the central problem stands: trait theory cannot explain why excellent leaders in one context fail spectacularly in another.

The behavioural school shifted attention from who leaders are to what leaders do. The Ohio State Leadership Studies (late 1940s) identified two independent dimensions: initiating structure (task focus — assigning roles, setting deadlines, monitoring progress) and consideration (people focus — respect, support, two-way communication). The University of Michigan studies (Likert and colleagues) found the same two dimensions, naming them production-oriented and employee-oriented. Building on this, Robert Blake and Jane Mouton (1964) drew the Managerial Grid, a 9×9 grid plotting concern for production against concern for people. Five named styles emerged: (1,1) impoverished, (1,9) country club, (9,1) authority-compliance, (5,5) middle of the road, and (9,9) team management. Blake and Mouton argued (9,9) was the universally best style — though contingency theorists later disagreed.

The contingency school said no single style is best — it depends on the situation. Fred Fiedler's contingency model (1967) used a "Least Preferred Co-worker" (LPC) score to classify a leader as task- or relationship-motivated, then matched leader type to three situational variables (leader-member relations, task structure, position power). Hersey & Blanchard's Situational Leadership (SL) model, much more practical and still widely used in training, says the right style depends on follower readiness: directing (S1, low ability/low willingness), coaching (S2, low ability/high willingness), supporting (S3, high ability/variable willingness), delegating (S4, high ability/high willingness). A good leader diagnoses each follower's level and adjusts.

  • Trait theory: leaders are born with stable traits — found to be only weakly predictive.
  • Behavioural theory: the two dimensions of initiating structure (task) and consideration (people).
  • Managerial Grid (Blake & Mouton): 9×9 plot; (9,9) team management ideal.
  • Fiedler's contingency model: match leader type (task vs. relationship) to situational favourability.
  • Situational Leadership (Hersey & Blanchard): directing / coaching / supporting / delegating, based on follower readiness.

Case: Applying Situational Leadership at a software company.

A team of five developers needs different styles from the same manager.
• Priya (junior, eager) — needs coaching (S2): clear direction plus emotional support.
• Jamal (junior, anxious about a new framework) — needs directing (S1): specific instructions, close oversight until confidence builds.
• Mei (senior, but recently disengaged after a project failure) — needs supporting (S3): low direction, high support to rebuild commitment.
• Aaron (senior, autonomous, top performer) — needs delegating (S4): hand off the project and step back.
The same manager must move fluidly among four styles in the same week.

  • Claiming "9,9 team management is always best." It is the Blake & Mouton ideal but contingency theorists reject the "one best" claim.
  • Confusing the two dimensions in the Managerial Grid (concern for production and concern for people are independent, not opposite).
  • Treating Situational Leadership as a personality test for the leader. The diagnosis is of the follower, not the leader.

2.3 — Transformational & Transactional Leadership
Burns and Bass; the four I's of transformational leadership; servant and authentic leadership.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Beginning in the late 1970s, leadership scholarship took another big step. Political scientist James MacGregor Burns, in his 1978 book Leadership, distinguished transactional from transformational leaders. Transactional leaders motivate followers through exchanges — clear goals, contingent rewards, corrective action when performance falls short. They are excellent at executing the existing strategy. Transformational leaders inspire followers to transcend self-interest for a higher purpose: they articulate a vision, energize the organization, and develop people. Bernard Bass operationalized these ideas in his Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ), breaking transformational leadership into the famous four I's: Idealized influence (the leader as role model), Inspirational motivation (an articulated, compelling vision), Intellectual stimulation (challenging assumptions, encouraging creativity), and Individualized consideration (paying attention to each follower's needs and growth).

Research over four decades has consistently linked transformational leadership to higher engagement, lower turnover, and better organizational performance — particularly in volatile environments. Bass insisted, however, that the best leaders combine transformational and transactional behaviours: a CEO must inspire (transformational) and meet quarterly numbers (transactional). Pure transformational leadership without follow-through is rhetoric; pure transactional leadership without vision is bureaucracy.

Two adjacent ideas often appear in BOH4M. Servant leadership, articulated by Robert Greenleaf in 1970, inverts the pyramid: the leader's primary job is to serve followers — listening, healing, building community. Authentic leadership, refined by Bill George and others in the 2000s, emphasizes self-awareness, internalized moral values, balanced processing of information, and relational transparency. Both share an ethical core that pure trait or behavioural theories lack. Many high-performing Canadian leaders — Indigenous chiefs working through consensus, school principals running restorative-justice circles, founders of B Corps — embody elements of all three.

  • Transactional leadership: exchanges, contingent rewards, management by exception.
  • Transformational leadership: the four I's — Idealized influence, Inspirational motivation, Intellectual stimulation, Individualized consideration.
  • Servant leadership (Greenleaf): leader serves first.
  • Authentic leadership: self-aware, values-driven, transparent.
  • Bass's claim: the best leaders are both transformational and transactional.

Case: Two contrasting tech CEOs.

CEO A holds quarterly all-hands meetings, paints a vivid picture of "where the company is heading in five years," personally mentors three high-potential employees, and rewires the engineering culture to challenge sacred cows. This is textbook transformational. CEO B sets aggressive monthly sales quotas, gives spiffs for hitting them, intervenes when teams miss targets, but never articulates why the work matters. This is textbook transactional. The most effective CEOs in research samples do both — Bass's MLQ shows transformational and transactional scores are additive, not substitutes.

  • Romanticizing transformational leadership as inherently good. Charismatic transformational leaders can also lead organizations into ethical disasters (Enron's Ken Lay, Theranos's Elizabeth Holmes).
  • Confusing transactional with autocratic. Transactional leadership is about clear exchanges, not authoritarian control.
  • Treating servant leadership as soft. Robert Greenleaf's original essay is rigorous and demanding: serve-first does not mean please-everyone.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "analyse Lewin's leadership styles (autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire) and identify situations in which each is most effective."

2.4 — Tuckman's Stages of Team Development
Forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning — what teams need from leaders at each stage.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

In 1965, psychologist Bruce Tuckman reviewed dozens of small-group studies and proposed that teams pass through four predictable stages — forming, storming, norming, performing — adding a fifth, adjourning, in 1977. The model is taught everywhere in management because it gives leaders specific behaviours appropriate to each stage.

Forming is polite. The team has just come together; members are tentative, focused on inclusion and acceptance, and unsure of the task. Leaders should clarify the mission, set ground rules, and make introductions warm and structured. Storming is conflict. As personalities and ideas collide, the team experiences disagreement about goals, roles, and process. Leaders should not suppress the storm — they should surface conflict productively, coach the team in active listening, and resist the urge to settle disputes by fiat. Teams that fail to storm rarely produce honest work. Norming follows when the team negotiates explicit and implicit rules — who does what, how decisions are made, how to handle disagreement. The leader's job shifts from active facilitation to occasional refereeing. Performing is the productive stage: cohesion, mutual trust, and high output. The leader can largely delegate. Adjourning arrives when the team's task is finished or membership dissolves: the leader should celebrate accomplishments, capture lessons learned, and help members transition.

A common misunderstanding is that teams move only forward. In reality teams cycle — adding a new member sends a team back to forming, a major strategy shift can re-trigger storming, and a long, stable team can stagnate in a kind of "comfortable norming" that mistakes harmony for performance. Tuckman is most useful as a diagnostic: where are we now, and what do we need from each other to move?

  • Forming: polite, dependent on leader, focused on inclusion.
  • Storming: conflict about roles, goals, process; healthy if managed.
  • Norming: rules and trust emerge; less leader direction needed.
  • Performing: productive, cohesive, autonomous; leader delegates.
  • Adjourning: wrap-up, celebration, lessons learned.

Case: A grade-12 group designing a marketing plan for the school store.

Week 1 (Forming) — Polite introductions; everyone agrees with everything; no real decisions. Leader: schedule a kick-off and clarify roles.
Week 2 (Storming) — Argument over whether to do TikTok content or print posters; one member dominates. Leader: facilitate active listening, do not let conflict become personal.
Week 3 (Norming) — The team agrees: TikTok lead = Priya, posters = Jamal, budget = Mei, presentation = Aaron. Daily 10-minute stand-ups added.
Weeks 4–5 (Performing) — Each member ships their piece; the team integrates the plan; rehearsals run smoothly.
Week 6 (Adjourning) — Presentation to the principal; team debriefs lessons; celebrates with pizza.

  • Treating Tuckman as strictly linear. New members or new mandates reset the cycle.
  • Suppressing storming because conflict feels uncomfortable. Teams that skip storming rarely perform well.
  • Ignoring adjourning. Closure and lessons-learned conversations are how teams (and individuals) carry skill forward.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "describe the stages of group/team development (Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning)."

2.5 — Content Theories of Motivation: Maslow, Alderfer, Herzberg, McClelland
Hierarchy of needs, ERG theory, two-factor (motivators vs. hygienes), and acquired-needs theory.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Content theories of motivation answer the question: what do people want from work? The most famous is Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs, published by Abraham Maslow in 1943. Maslow arranged human needs in a five-level pyramid: physiological (food, water, shelter), safety (security, stable employment, health), belongingness/love (friendship, team, family), esteem (recognition, status, achievement), and self-actualization (realizing one's full potential). Maslow argued lower-level needs must be largely satisfied before higher-level needs become motivating. The implication for managers: an employee who is anxious about being laid off (safety) will not be motivated by an inspirational mission statement (self-actualization).

Clayton Alderfer's ERG theory (1969) compresses Maslow's five levels into three — Existence, Relatedness, Growth — and adds an important refinement: needs can operate simultaneously, and frustration at one level may cause a person to "regress" to a lower level. A worker frustrated in their growth opportunities may compensate by pouring energy into relatedness (the team's social life), which Maslow's strict hierarchy could not predict.

Frederick Herzberg's two-factor theory (1959) is the most directly practical for managers. Based on interviews with Pittsburgh engineers and accountants, Herzberg found that the things that make people satisfied at work are not the opposite of the things that make them dissatisfied. Hygiene factors — pay, working conditions, company policy, supervision, security — cause dissatisfaction if absent but do not motivate when present. Motivators — achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth — actually drive engagement. The management implication is profound: paying more rarely buys motivation; redesigning work to add responsibility and recognition does. Finally, David McClelland's acquired-needs theory proposes that three needs — achievement (nAch), affiliation (nAff), and power (nPow) — are learned through life experience, and their relative strength predicts what kinds of roles and rewards different people respond to.

  • Maslow: Physiological → Safety → Belongingness → Esteem → Self-actualization.
  • Alderfer's ERG: Existence, Relatedness, Growth — non-hierarchical, can regress.
  • Herzberg's two-factor: Hygienes prevent dissatisfaction; Motivators create satisfaction. Different scales.
  • McClelland: three learned needs — Achievement, Affiliation, Power.
  • Practical insight: pay is a hygiene; meaningful work is a motivator.

Case: Designing a retention strategy for a tech firm losing engineers.

The HR director's first instinct: raise salaries 15%. A Herzberg-informed analysis says this will temporarily reduce dissatisfaction (pay is a hygiene factor) but is unlikely to create motivation. The retained employees may stop complaining about pay and still leave for more interesting work elsewhere. A better strategy: keep pay competitive (a hygiene threshold), then invest in motivators — give engineers ownership of full features (responsibility), establish a peer-recognition program (recognition), create internal mobility (growth), and pair them with senior mentors (advancement). Companies that adopt this two-pronged strategy typically reduce voluntary turnover by 20–40%.

  • Reading Maslow as strictly sequential. Real people move up and down the pyramid all day depending on circumstances.
  • Believing pay raises will sustain motivation. Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; they don't create engagement.
  • Confusing McClelland's "power need" with corruption. People high in nPow can use power constructively (e.g., effective managers) or destructively.

2.6 — Process Theories: Equity, Expectancy & Goal-Setting
Adams's equity theory, Vroom's expectancy, Locke & Latham's goal-setting — how, not just what, motivates.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

While content theories tell us what people want, process theories explain how motivation works — the cognitive process by which a person decides how much effort to give. Three are essential.

Adams's equity theory (1965) says people compare their ratio of inputs (effort, skill, experience) to outcomes (pay, recognition, promotion) against the same ratio for a reference person. If the ratios are equal, the person feels fairly treated. If their ratio is worse than the reference person's, they feel under-rewarded and may reduce effort, ask for a raise, or quit. If their ratio is better, they feel over-rewarded and may work harder or feel guilt. Crucially, what matters is perceived equity, not objective equity — and the reference person is often chosen unfavourably. This explains the documented finding that revealing pay information frequently reduces satisfaction even at firms that pay fairly: comparisons are no longer hypothetical.

Victor Vroom's expectancy theory (1964) models motivation as the product of three perceptions: expectancy (will my effort produce performance?), instrumentality (will my performance produce the reward?), and valence (do I actually want the reward?). Motivation = E × I × V. If any one term is zero — the goal seems impossible, the bonus formula is opaque, or the reward doesn't matter to the person — motivation collapses. The implication for managers: to motivate, ensure all three terms are high. Provide training and tools (expectancy), make the link from performance to reward visible and reliable (instrumentality), and tailor rewards to what the person values (valence).

Finally, Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory (refined over forty years) is among the most well-supported theories in industrial-organizational psychology. Five attributes make goals motivating: clarity (specific, not vague), challenge (stretching but possible), commitment (the person buys in), feedback (frequent information on progress), and task complexity (the goal-setting effect weakens for very complex tasks unless sub-goals are used). This research underpins the popular SMART goals framework (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) and the OKR (Objectives and Key Results) practice used at Google and beyond.

  • Equity theory (Adams): compare your input/outcome ratio to a reference person; act to restore fairness.
  • Expectancy theory (Vroom): Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence.
  • Goal-setting (Locke & Latham): specific, challenging, committed-to, feedback-rich goals motivate.
  • SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — practical implementation of goal-setting.

Case: A sales-team bonus plan fails to motivate.

The company offers a $5,000 bonus if quarterly sales rise 25%. Six months in, sales are flat and reps say the bonus "isn't worth chasing." Diagnose with Vroom:
• Expectancy: reps feel a 25% jump is impossible given supply-chain issues → near zero.
• Instrumentality: reps are unsure how individual sales count toward the team target → low.
• Valence: $5,000 sounds nice, but after-tax it is roughly $3,000, which doesn't justify giving up evenings → moderate.
Total motivation \(= 0.1 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.024\) — almost zero. Fix: lower the target to 10% (raises expectancy), make individual contribution to team target transparent on a dashboard (raises instrumentality), and let reps choose the reward (cash, extra vacation, conference attendance) to raise valence.

  • Assuming everyone values the same reward. Valence is highly personal — a free company hoodie excites some employees and insults others.
  • Ignoring expectancy. The most generous bonus is worth nothing if employees believe the target is unreachable.
  • Setting "do your best" goals. Research consistently shows that vague goals lead to lower performance than specific, challenging ones.

2.7 — Job Design, Diversity & Indigenous Perspectives
Job rotation, enlargement, enrichment; the Job Characteristics Model; diversity, generations, Indigenous leadership.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

One of the most direct ways to apply motivation theory is to redesign work itself. Job rotation moves employees through different tasks to build skill variety. Job enlargement broadens a single job by adding more tasks at the same skill level — useful when work is monotonous but not always motivating in itself. Job enrichment, following Herzberg, adds responsibility and decision-making to the job, deliberately building in motivators. Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model (JCM) identifies five core dimensions — skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback — that produce three psychological states (meaningfulness, responsibility, knowledge of results) leading to motivation, satisfaction, and quality. Many modern roles are explicitly designed against the JCM checklist.

Effective leaders also recognize that workplace diversity — of identity, background, generation, and perspective — strengthens decisions but only if it is genuinely included. The Canadian workforce in 2026 includes Baby Boomers (born 1946–1964) still working into their 70s, Generation X (1965–1980), Millennials (1981–1996), Generation Z (1997–2012), and the early edge of Generation Alpha. Each cohort brings different expectations about flexibility, technology, feedback frequency, and meaning at work. The leader's job is not to stereotype generations but to understand the range of preferences and design work and communication that serve them all.

Canadian managers also work with — and increasingly learn from — Indigenous leadership traditions. While Indigenous nations are diverse and no single model applies to all, recurring themes include leadership through consensus rather than command, accountability to the seven generations (a long time-horizon), reciprocity with land and community, and the role of Elders as knowledge keepers. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action (2015), in particular Call 92, ask the Canadian corporate sector to commit to meaningful consultation, equitable employment, and education on Indigenous rights and history. Many Crown corporations, banks, and resource companies have published reconciliation action plans in response. For BOH4M students, the takeaway is that "leadership theory" includes more than Anglo-American academic models, and the best leaders today learn from multiple traditions.

  • Job rotation: move through tasks; builds variety.
  • Job enlargement: add more tasks at the same level (horizontal).
  • Job enrichment: add responsibility and decision-making (vertical) — Herzberg motivators.
  • Job Characteristics Model: skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback.
  • Generational diversity: Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z, Gen Alpha — different expectations.
  • Indigenous leadership: consensus, seven-generations thinking, reciprocity, Elder knowledge; TRC Call to Action 92.

Case: Redesigning a call-centre role using the JCM.

Before: agents handle one narrow product line; calls are scripted; they cannot resolve issues without escalating; they receive feedback only at quarterly reviews. JCM scores: skill variety low, task identity low, autonomy near zero, feedback delayed. Motivating potential score is poor; turnover is 75%/year.
After redesign: agents handle three related products (variety, identity), can authorize refunds up to $500 (autonomy, significance), and have a live dashboard showing customer-satisfaction scores after each call (feedback). Within six months, turnover drops to 35% and customer satisfaction rises by 18 percentage points.

  • Treating diversity as a compliance checkbox. Diverse representation without inclusion (voice, influence) produces little of the predicted benefit.
  • Stereotyping generations. "Millennials want flexibility" is statistically true on average but masks huge individual variation.
  • Treating Indigenous leadership as exotic. Many Canadian organizations now incorporate elements (consensus rounds, land acknowledgments with action, knowledge-keeper roles) as everyday practice.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "identify and describe non-monetary rewards, recognition programs, job design (job rotation, enlargement, enrichment), and their impact on motivation."
