3.1 — Modes of Market Entry
Direct/indirect exporting, licensing, franchising, contract manufacturing, joint venture, FDI; risk-control-capital trade-offs.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Once a firm has decided to enter a foreign market, it must choose how to enter. The choice is a sequence of trade-offs along three axes: capital required, control retained, and risk assumed. Modes are usually ranked from low to high commitment:

  • Indirect exporting — selling through a Canadian-based intermediary (export broker, trading house, EMC). Lowest capital, lowest control, low risk, fast.
  • Direct exporting — the manufacturer ships and bills foreign customers directly. More control, more learning, more administrative cost.
  • Licensing — granting a foreign firm rights to use IP (patent, brand, formula, know-how) for a royalty. No capital from the licensor; risk is loss of IP if it leaks.
  • Franchising — a packaged business model (operations manual, training, brand) operated by a foreign franchisee under ongoing fees. Standard for fast food, hotels, retail (McDonald's, Marriott, Tim Hortons).
  • Contract manufacturing — outsourcing production to a foreign factory while retaining design and marketing (Nike, Apple).
  • Joint venture (JV) — a co-owned local company. Shared risk, shared profits, mandatory in some countries (e.g., past Chinese auto regulations).
  • Wholly-owned subsidiary / FDI — the foreign firm builds or buys a fully owned operation abroad. Highest capital, highest control, highest exit cost.
  • Strategic alliance — collaboration short of an equity stake (e.g., co-marketing, technology sharing).
  • Cross-border e-commerce — direct-to-consumer online sales; collapses traditional channels.

Factors influencing the choice include speed of market entry, knowledge of the local market, intellectual-property protection, host-country regulation, capital availability, and the strategic importance of the market. A small Canadian software firm may start with cross-border e-commerce; once volume justifies investment, it may upgrade to a JV or a wholly-owned sales office.

Example: A Canadian quick-service restaurant chooses Indonesia.

Capital is limited and the chain has no local knowledge. FDI is ruled out for now. Direct exporting doesn't fit a restaurant. The two viable choices are franchising and a joint venture with an Indonesian food-court operator. Franchising offers fast scale (50 outlets within 4 years), full standardization of menu and look, royalties of 5% of sales plus an upfront fee per outlet. JV offers higher per-outlet returns but slower growth and more management bandwidth. The chain chooses franchising, accepting lower per-store profit for faster footprint and lower risk.

  • Choosing FDI for prestige rather than fit. FDI is the most expensive and least reversible commitment.
  • Confusing licensing with franchising. Licensing is narrower (one element of IP); franchising is the full business format.
  • Ignoring rules that mandate local-partner participation. China, Saudi Arabia, and others have or have had such requirements in specific sectors.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "compare modes of entry into international markets (e.g., exporting, licensing, franchising, contract manufacturing, joint venture, foreign direct investment) and assess the factors that influence the choice of entry mode."

3.2 — International Distribution Channels & Intermediaries
Channel structure across countries; agents, brokers, distributors, wholesalers, retailers; role of e-commerce marketplaces (Amazon, Alibaba, Mercado Libre).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A distribution channel is the path a product takes from manufacturer to final consumer. Internationally, channels vary in length (number of intermediaries) and structure (which intermediaries). The shorter the channel, the more control the manufacturer keeps over price, brand, and customer experience — but also the more capability the manufacturer must build itself.

Typical channel members:

  • Manufacturer/exporter — origin of the product.
  • Export trading company / export broker — Canadian-based middleman who finds foreign buyers, paid by commission.
  • Foreign agent — represents the manufacturer abroad on commission; does not take title to the goods.
  • Foreign distributor — buys, warehouses, and resells; takes title and inventory risk; often gives an exclusive territory.
  • Wholesaler — buys in volume and resells to retailers.
  • Retailer — sells to final consumers.
  • E-commerce marketplace — Amazon (global), Alibaba/Tmall (China), Mercado Libre (Latin America), Lazada and Shopee (SE Asia).

Channel length varies sharply by country. Japan is famous for long, multi-layered wholesale networks; the United States is famous for shorter, big-box-dominated channels. Many developing markets retain large numbers of small, family-owned retailers; this fragmented retail structure forces firms to use local distributors who have feet on the street. In contrast, the rise of e-commerce platforms can flatten channels dramatically — a Canadian boutique can sell directly to a Korean consumer through a global marketplace, bypassing several intermediaries.

Distributor selection is one of the most consequential decisions an exporter makes. Criteria: financial stability, market coverage, complementary product lines, sales force size, willingness to invest in marketing the new product, history with competitors, and cultural fit. Once selected, the exporter typically signs a distributor agreement defining territory, exclusivity, minimum purchase volume, marketing obligations, term, and termination rights.

Example: Channel comparison for a Canadian apparel brand.

Option A — wholesaler/distributor channel: Manufacturer (\(C\$30\)) → Foreign distributor (margin 25% = \(C\$37.50\)) → Wholesaler (margin 15% = \(C\$43.13\)) → Retailer (margin 50% gross = \(C\$86.26\)). Final shelf price \(\approx C\$86\).

Option B — direct-to-consumer e-commerce: Manufacturer (\(C\$30\)) ships from Canada, charges \(C\$30 + C\$15\) shipping + 10% platform fee. Final price to consumer \(\approx C\$50\). Higher margin, lower volume, more shipping complexity. The brand may run both: direct online for early-adopter buyers, distributors for mass retail.

  • Choosing a distributor by handshake. A weak agreement leaves the exporter exposed if the partnership sours.
  • Ignoring channel conflict between online and offline channels. A direct e-commerce price below distributor retail breaks the relationship.
  • Underestimating the cost of changing distributors. Many countries have laws that make terminating local agents expensive.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "describe the structure of international distribution channels and the role of intermediaries (e.g., agents, brokers, distributors, wholesalers, retailers, online marketplaces)."

3.3 — Global Supply Chains & Sourcing
Designing a supply network; sourcing decisions; lean vs resilient supply chains; risks (port strikes, pandemics, geopolitics); near-shoring and friend-shoring.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A supply chain is the network of suppliers, factories, warehouses, transport links, and retailers that converts raw materials into a finished product on a consumer's shelf. Global supply chains span multiple countries; an iPhone, for example, contains components from over 40 countries before final assembly in China or India and sale in 100+ countries.

Two competing supply-chain philosophies have dominated the last two decades:

  • Lean supply chain — minimize inventory and cost; rely on just-in-time delivery and a small number of best-in-class suppliers, often offshored to lowest-cost regions. Pioneered by Toyota, copied widely.
  • Resilient supply chain — accept higher cost in exchange for redundancy: multiple suppliers, regional inventory buffers, dual-sourcing of critical components, near-shoring of strategic items. Gained prominence after the COVID-19 pandemic, semiconductor shortages, the Suez blockage, and trade-policy shocks.

Sourcing decisions weigh:

  • Cost — wages, materials, energy, taxes, duties.
  • Quality — supplier capability, certification (ISO 9001, IATF 16949).
  • Lead time — how fast can the supplier respond?
  • Reliability — political stability, port reliability, currency stability, labour relations.
  • Total cost — landed cost including FX risk, tariff, transport, and the value at risk if the supplier fails.

Three recent shifts: near-shoring (moving suppliers closer to home markets, e.g., Mexican factories serving the US); friend-shoring (preferring suppliers in politically aligned countries); and regionalization of global supply chains into Americas-, Europe-, and Asia-centred networks. These changes raise costs but reduce geopolitical risk.

Example: Total-cost sourcing analysis for a Canadian medical-device firm.

Vietnam supplier: unit cost \(C\$22\), lead time 60 days, ocean freight \(C\$2.50\), duty 6%, supplier risk score 0.8 (high). Mexico supplier: unit cost \(C\$26\), lead time 14 days, road freight \(C\$1.20\), duty 0% (CUSMA), supplier risk score 0.3 (low). Comparing landed unit cost alone: Vietnam \(= 22 + 2.50 + (22 \times 6\%) = 25.82\); Mexico \(= 26 + 1.20 + 0 = 27.20\). Vietnam is cheaper per unit by \(C\$1.38\), but Mexico's faster lead time cuts safety stock, and lower risk reduces the expected cost of supply disruption. Including a 15% risk premium on the Vietnamese landed cost (\(25.82 \times 1.15 = 29.69\)) makes Mexico cheaper on a total-cost basis. The firm dual-sources: 60% Mexico, 40% Vietnam.

  • Sourcing on unit price alone. Total cost includes transport, inventory, risk, and FX.
  • Single-source dependency. One disruption can shut a line down.
  • Ignoring regulatory and ESG (environmental, social, governance) screening of suppliers. Customer pressure on labour, environmental, and human-rights standards is rising.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "analyse global supply-chain decisions including sourcing, production location, and the trade-offs between lean and resilient supply chains."

3.4 — Modes of Transport in International Trade
Ocean, air, rail, road, intermodal; containerization (TEU); cost, time, risk trade-offs; choosing transport for product type and destination.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

How goods physically move from origin to destination shapes cost, lead time, and product condition. Five modes dominate international cargo:

  • Ocean — the workhorse of international trade. Carries roughly 80% of world cargo by volume. Cheap per tonne-kilometre, slow (Halifax to Shanghai \(\approx\) 30 days), suited to non-perishable, low-value-density goods (grain, lumber, machinery, consumer electronics in containers).
  • Air — fastest (Halifax to Shanghai \(\approx\) 18 hours), most expensive per tonne-kilometre. Carries about 1% of trade by volume but ~35% by value: pharmaceuticals, electronics, fashion, perishables, mail and parcels.
  • Rail — efficient over land for bulky, long-haul cargo. Canada's rail network (CN, CPKC) is critical for grain, autos, oil, and intermodal containers reaching North American ports. China-Europe rail freight has grown rapidly.
  • Road / trucking — flexible, door-to-door, dominant on shorter cross-border lanes. \(\approx 70\%\) of Canada-US cross-border goods value moves by truck.
  • Pipeline — for oil and gas only; cheap once built, inflexible.

Intermodal transport combines two or more modes — typically with a standardized container. The shipping container, invented by Malcom McLean in 1956, transformed global trade by allowing the same metal box to move from truck to ship to train without unloading. Measured in TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units), container traffic grew from negligible in 1960 to over 850 million TEU per year today.

Choosing the right mode means trading cost against time, against risk of damage, and against the value density of the product. A rule of thumb: if a product is worth less than its weight in chocolate, ship it; if worth more, fly it. For perishables, air or refrigerated sea (reefer container) is mandatory.

Example: Mode choice for a Canadian wine exporter to Japan.

Air freight: 24 hours transit, \(C\$2.50\)/kg. For a 9-litre case weighing 15 kg, freight \(= 15 \times 2.50 = C\$37.50\)/case.

Ocean reefer: 28 days transit, \(C\$0.20\)/kg. For the same case: \(15 \times 0.20 = C\$3.00\)/case.

The wine retails at \(C\$45\). Air freight at \(C\$37.50\)/case eats nearly all the margin; ocean reefer at \(C\$3.00\)/case is the obvious choice. The longer lead time is offset by carrying a 60-day inventory in Japanese warehouses. Air freight stays available for urgent reorders and small premium parcels.

  • Picking transport on time alone. Speed costs disproportionate money.
  • Forgetting Incoterm impact: who pays the freight changes the mode-selection economics.
  • Ignoring product fragility, temperature sensitivity, and customs clearance time.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.3 — "compare modes of international transport (ocean, air, rail, road, intermodal/container) and analyse the trade-offs in cost, time, and risk."

3.5 — Freight Forwarders, Customs Brokers & 3PL Providers
What freight forwarders, customs brokers, and third-party logistics providers do; when to outsource logistics; when to keep it in-house.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Moving a container from a Toronto warehouse to a Bangkok customer is not done by the exporter alone — a small ecosystem of specialists handles each step. The three most important roles:

  • Freight forwarder — coordinates physical transport. Books cargo space with carriers (ocean lines, airlines, rail, trucks), consolidates small shipments into containers (LCL — less-than-container-load), prepares transport documents (bill of lading, air waybill), arranges marine insurance, and tracks the shipment door to door. The forwarder rarely owns the trucks or ships; it is a logistics agent. Major Canadian forwarders include DHL Global Forwarding, Kuehne+Nagel, CEVA, and many domestic specialists.
  • Customs broker — licensed by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) to clear shipments through customs. The broker classifies the goods under the Harmonized System (HS), calculates duties and taxes, prepares customs declarations (e.g., B3 in Canada), and represents the importer to CBSA. Without a broker, every shipment would require the importer to handle paperwork directly. Major brokers: Livingston, A.N. Deringer, GHY International.
  • Third-party logistics provider (3PL) — handles broader logistics functions: warehousing, order picking and packing, returns, distribution, and sometimes a piece of the supply chain itself. 3PLs include FedEx Supply Chain, DSV, and many regional firms. A 4PL manages multiple 3PLs and the entire supply-chain network on behalf of the client.

The make-or-buy decision in logistics is the same as in any business function: outsource when scale, expertise, and capital constraints favour a specialist; keep in-house when the activity is strategic and confers competitive advantage. A small Canadian exporter benefits enormously from a forwarder/broker pair; a multinational like Walmart Canada keeps much logistics in-house because scale and proprietary systems matter.

Example: A Canadian craft-furniture firm exports to the UK.

The firm has six shipments a year, no in-house logistics staff, and no UK customs licence. It contracts a freight forwarder to book ocean LCL shipments from Montreal to Felixstowe, prepare bills of lading, and arrange marine insurance. It contracts a UK-based customs broker to clear shipments through HMRC and pay UK duty/VAT on behalf of the firm. The forwarder charges roughly \(C\$450\) per shipment plus carrier costs; the broker charges around £120 per entry. Total annual logistics spend: under \(C\$8{,}000\), versus the cost of hiring a part-time export coordinator (>\(C\$30{,}000\)) — the outsourced solution wins.

  • Confusing freight forwarder with customs broker. Forwarder moves the cargo; broker clears it through customs. Many firms offer both.
  • Treating the broker as optional. CBSA fines for incorrect HS classification and undeclared value are steep.
  • Going DIY for "savings" without understanding cost of errors. One detained container can cost more than years of forwarder fees.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.4 — "describe the roles of freight forwarders, customs brokers, and third-party logistics providers in moving goods across international borders."

3.6 — Incoterms 2020 — Who Pays, Who Risks, Who Insures
EXW, FCA, FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP; the three points where cost and risk transfer in an international sale.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Incoterms — short for International Commercial Terms — are a set of 11 three-letter codes published by the International Chamber of Commerce. The current version, Incoterms 2020, defines who, between buyer and seller, pays for transport, insurance, and customs clearance — and where physical risk transfers from seller to buyer. Without Incoterms, every international contract would have to re-litigate "who pays the ocean freight?" — Incoterms standardize the answer.

The 11 terms in Incoterms 2020 (with rough escalation of seller obligation):

  • EXW (Ex Works) — seller makes goods available at its premises; buyer does everything else. Minimum seller obligation.
  • FCA (Free Carrier) — seller delivers cleared for export at a named place; buyer takes over from there.
  • FAS (Free Alongside Ship) — seller delivers alongside the vessel at the named port; sea/inland waterway only.
  • FOB (Free On Board) — seller loads on board the vessel; sea/inland only.
  • CFR (Cost and Freight) — seller pays freight to destination port; risk passes at origin port (sea only).
  • CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight) — same as CFR but seller also pays insurance.
  • CPT (Carriage Paid To) — seller pays carriage to named place; any mode.
  • CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To) — CPT plus insurance.
  • DAP (Delivered at Place) — seller pays everything to destination, except import duties; risk transfers at unloading point.
  • DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded) — seller delivers and unloads at destination.
  • DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) — seller delivers, cleared for import, taxes paid. Maximum seller obligation.

Two key insights: each Incoterm specifies three transfers — cost of transport, cost of insurance, and risk of loss/damage. They do not transfer at the same point in every term. Under CIF, for example, the seller pays freight all the way to the destination port, but risk transfers when goods are loaded at the origin port — so if the ship sinks mid-ocean, the buyer (or its insurer) bears the loss, not the seller. Choosing the right Incoterm is therefore not just about who pays what; it determines who insures the cargo.

Example: A Canadian electronics maker exports to Brazil.

Buyer wants DDP (delivered to their warehouse, duty paid, no surprises). Seller refuses — Brazilian customs is complex, duties are 20%, and the seller does not want to act as importer of record. They compromise on CIF Santos: seller pays factory-to-Santos ocean freight (\(C\$3.20/\)unit) and marine insurance (\(C\$0.40/\)unit), risk transfers at Halifax when the container is loaded; buyer arranges Brazilian customs, duty, and inland trucking to São Paulo. Buyer insures from Halifax onward separately. The bill of lading and commercial invoice show "CIF Santos, Incoterms 2020." Both parties know exactly who pays which line.

  • Writing "FOB factory" — wrong. FOB only applies to sea/inland waterway and refers to a port, not a factory. Use FCA for a factory pickup.
  • Confusing cost transfer with risk transfer. CIF transfers cost at destination, risk at origin.
  • Not citing the Incoterms version. Always write "FOB Halifax, Incoterms 2020" to avoid ambiguity with older versions.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "explain Incoterms 2020 and how they allocate cost, risk, and responsibility between exporter and importer in an international sale."
