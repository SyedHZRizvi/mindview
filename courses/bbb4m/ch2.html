2.1 — International Market Research
Why research is essential before entering a foreign market; primary vs secondary sources; the major databases (UN Comtrade, Trade Data Online, World Bank, Statistics Canada, Trade Commissioner Service).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Entering a foreign market without research is like sailing without a chart. International market research is the systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of data about overseas customers, competitors, and conditions. It exists to reduce risk — every dollar spent on early research can save many dollars in failed inventory, broken distribution deals, or trademark disputes downstream.

Research divides into two kinds. Primary research is original data the firm collects itself — surveys, focus groups, interviews, in-country store visits, mystery shopping, pilot launches. It is expensive and slow but gives current, custom answers. Secondary research uses data already collected by someone else — government statistics, industry reports, academic studies, news articles, trade-association databases. It is fast and cheap but may be dated, aggregated at the wrong level, or unavailable for the specific question.

For Canadian exporters, the key secondary sources are:

  • Statistics Canada — Canadian export and import data by HS code and country.
  • Trade Data Online (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada) — trade statistics with custom-built queries.
  • Trade Commissioner Service (Global Affairs Canada) — on-the-ground intelligence from over 160 Canadian missions abroad.
  • UN Comtrade — the world's largest trade-data repository, by country/product/year.
  • World Bank / IMF — country macro data, World Development Indicators.
  • Country-specific agencies — for example, Eurostat for the EU, METI for Japan, the US Census Bureau for the US.

Effective research follows a stepwise process: define the question; identify what data already exist; collect secondary data; identify gaps; design and execute primary research to fill them; interpret; document; revise the entry plan. Skipping the first step — defining the question — is the most common failure.

Example: Sizing a market.

A Canadian e-bike maker wants to estimate the German market for premium e-bikes priced above €2,500. Secondary search of Eurostat and the Confederation of the European Bicycle Industry shows that Germany sold 2.1 million e-bikes in the most recent year, of which roughly 28% were premium. So the segment size is approximately \(2{,}100{,}000 \times 0.28 = 588{,}000\) units. If the firm aspires to a 0.5% share in year three, the target is \(588{,}000 \times 0.005 = 2{,}940\) units per year — a realistic figure to plan inventory, marketing spend, and distribution against.

  • Treating secondary data as up-to-date. Many country databases lag by 12–24 months.
  • Skipping primary research because secondary "looks complete". Local pricing perceptions, taste, and packaging preferences need original input.
  • Comparing apples to oranges across countries. HS codes, demographic brackets, and definitions of "consumer" can differ.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "explain the importance of market research in international business and compare primary and secondary research methods used to assess foreign markets."

2.2 — Segmentation, Targeting & Positioning Across Borders
Demographic, psychographic, geographic, behavioural segmentation in a global context; selecting target segments; positioning the offer against local competitors.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Even within one country, no firm can sell to everyone — and across countries, the variation explodes. Segmentation divides the total market into groups whose members share characteristics; targeting picks which segments to pursue; positioning decides what the brand should stand for in the minds of the chosen segment relative to competitors. This three-step "STP" framework is the bedrock of international marketing.

Four bases of segmentation, all useful internationally:

  • Demographic: age, gender, income, education, occupation, family size. Easy to measure but a coarse predictor of behaviour.
  • Geographic: country, region, urban vs rural, climate zone. Critical for global firms but can mask within-country diversity.
  • Psychographic: lifestyle, values, personality, interests. Often the most powerful predictor — e.g., "health-conscious millennials" cuts across countries.
  • Behavioural: usage rate, brand loyalty, occasion of use, benefits sought. Often used to refine other segments.

Targeting strategies range from undifferentiated (one offer for the whole world — Coca-Cola Classic), through differentiated (multiple offers for multiple segments — Unilever brands), to concentrated/niche (one offer for one narrow segment — Rolex). Positioning is then expressed as a short statement: "For [target] who [need], [brand] is [category] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe]."

Example: Positioning Tim Hortons in India.

Tim Hortons launched in India in 2022. India's coffee market is dominated by Café Coffee Day, Starbucks, and Costa, with a strong tea culture and many vegetarians. Tim Hortons identified a psychographic segment — urban, middle-class, English-speaking professionals 25–40 who value reasonably-priced, fast-service cafés — and positioned itself as "everyday Canadian coffee and snacks at honest prices for the new Indian commuter." It adapted menus (paneer wraps, masala chai), kept its Tim Hortons branding (Canadian provenance) and priced 20% below Starbucks. The position is differentiated, the target is concentrated, and competitors are clearly named.

  • Targeting on country alone. "We sell to Brazil" is not a segment; "young urban Brazilian women interested in athleisure" is.
  • Confusing segmentation and targeting. Segmentation describes; targeting decides.
  • Forgetting positioning. Without it, the brand competes only on price.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "analyse the methods and challenges of segmentation, targeting, and positioning for international markets, including demographic, psychographic, geographic, and behavioural segmentation."

2.3 — The 4 Ps Across Borders: Product, Price, Place, Promotion
Adapting the marketing mix for foreign markets; product modifications; channels and distribution; promotional messaging; how price is set across countries.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every introductory marketing course teaches the 4 Ps — product, price, place, promotion — first proposed by E. Jerome McCarthy in 1960 and popularized by Philip Kotler. In international marketing, the 4 Ps are the same, but every one of them needs to be reconsidered in a new country.

Product: the physical good or service, including features, design, brand name, packaging, label, after-sales service, and warranty. Foreign markets may require changes to electrical voltage (Europe 230V vs North America 120V), language on packaging, units (metric), safety standards (CE, UL, CSA marks), religious requirements (halal, kosher), or even product names that translate badly. McDonald's removed beef from its menu in India because of Hindu dietary norms; Microsoft renamed Vista in some markets where the word had a negative connotation.

Price: the amount the customer pays. Setting price abroad must reflect cost (production, shipping, duties, distribution), demand (what local customers will pay), competition (what Coca-Cola charges in Manila), and currency. Common approaches: cost-plus (cost + markup), market-based (match competitors), penetration pricing (low to gain share), price skimming (high early to harvest from premium buyers).

Place (distribution): the channels through which the product reaches the buyer. Foreign markets have very different channel structures — Japan has long, multi-tiered distribution networks; the US has short, direct channels. The choice of agent, distributor, wholesaler, online platform, and retail format dictates margin and brand experience.

Promotion: all communication that informs or persuades — advertising, sales promotion, public relations, personal selling, digital and social. Promotional copy translated word-for-word often fails (the famous "Pepsi brings your ancestors back from the grave" mistranslation in China). Channels also differ — TV penetration, mobile usage, regulated promotions on alcohol/tobacco/pharma.

Example: A Canadian snack maker enters Mexico.

Product: smaller pack sizes (Mexican retail favours single-serve), labelling in Spanish per NOM standards, no pork to suit broader demand. Price: cost of \(C\$1.20\)/pack, ocean freight + duty + distributor markup raises landed cost to \(C\$2.10\); local competitor sells at MXN 25 (\(\approx C\$1.80\)). Firm chooses market-based pricing at MXN 28 to recover cost while staying close to competitor. Place: agreement with a Mexican distributor that already serves OXXO and Walmart de México. Promotion: in-store displays plus an Instagram campaign featuring Mexican influencers; no glossy English-language print ads.

  • Translating without localizing. A literal translation often misses local idioms.
  • Setting price by cost-plus only. Local willingness-to-pay may be far below.
  • Underestimating channel power. Strong local distributors can hold the brand hostage.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "analyse how each element of the marketing mix (product, price, place, promotion) must be adapted to suit the requirements of foreign markets."

2.4 — Standardization vs Adaptation (the Global/Local Debate)
When to offer the same product globally vs adapt for each market; Theodore Levitt's "Globalization of Markets"; the "glocal" middle ground.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

One of the oldest and sharpest debates in international marketing: should a firm offer the same product worldwide (standardization) to capture scale economies, or adapt the offer to each country (adaptation) to maximize local fit? In a landmark 1983 Harvard Business Review article, "The Globalization of Markets," Theodore Levitt argued that technology was making the world's tastes converge and that firms who standardized would win. The reality — visible in any global supermarket — is messier. Most large firms today practice glocalization: they keep a single global brand and core product, but adapt features, packaging, formulation, and communications locally.

Drivers favouring standardization include scale economies in production and advertising, single brand identity, simpler supply chain, lower complexity. Drivers favouring adaptation include differences in tastes, climate, regulation, language, channel structure, income, and culture. Industrial goods (steel, semiconductors, jet engines) tend to standardize; consumer goods (food, beverages, fashion) tend to adapt.

McDonald's is the textbook glocal: the brand, the golden arches, fast-service format, and a few global menu items (Big Mac, McNuggets) are standardized; the menu is heavily adapted — McAloo Tikki in India, Teriyaki Burger in Japan, McLobster in Maritime Canada, beer in Germany. Coca-Cola similarly standardizes the trademark and red look, but reformulates sweetness (sweeter in Latin America, less sweet in Japan), pack size, and pricing locally.

  • Standardization: one product, one message, worldwide.
  • Adaptation: tailor product/marketing to each country.
  • Glocalization: "think global, act local" — standardized core + local execution.
  • Economies of scale: per-unit costs fall as volume rises.

Example: IKEA's standardization–adaptation balance.

Standardized: flat-pack design, blue-and-yellow stores, low-price positioning, brand name, the catalog. Adapted: bed sizes (Korea Queen 1500×2000; North America 1500×2030), kitchen cabinet sizes, breakfast meals served (Swedish meatballs everywhere, but congee added in China), assembly tools (a wrench in every box; in some markets a screwdriver). Net result: IKEA reaches roughly 50 countries with a remarkably consistent brand experience while still selling things that fit local homes.

  • Treating standardization vs adaptation as binary. Most firms do both, on different attributes.
  • Standardizing without testing. A product that fits Toronto may fail in Mumbai.
  • Over-adapting until scale economies vanish. Every adaptation has a cost.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "compare the strategies of standardization and adaptation in international marketing and explain the trade-offs involved (e.g., economies of scale vs local relevance)."

2.5 — International Pricing & the Hidden Costs of Going Global
Cost-plus, market-based, penetration, skimming pricing; landed cost; tariffs, FX, distributor margins; price escalation and grey markets.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Pricing internationally is rarely as simple as multiplying the Canadian price by an exchange rate. A product made for \(C\$10\) at the factory door rarely reaches a foreign shelf at the same \(C\$10\); a series of costs and markups is added at each step. The cumulative inflation of price as a product moves abroad is called price escalation, and it can double or triple the retail price.

The components of landed cost, the total cost to get goods on the shelf in the destination, typically include: factory cost, export packaging, inland freight in Canada, ocean/air freight, marine insurance, brokerage fee, import duty, taxes (e.g., VAT/GST), terminal handling, distributor margin, retailer margin, and any local advertising allowance. Each is a percent on top of the previous total, so they compound.

Once landed cost is known, the firm chooses a pricing strategy:

  • Cost-plus: price = landed cost × (1 + markup). Simple but ignores demand.
  • Market-based: match or undercut competitor prices already in the market.
  • Penetration: price low to gain share fast (Xiaomi's smartphones in India).
  • Skimming: price high to harvest the premium segment (Apple).
  • Value pricing: price reflects perceived value, not cost.

Two warnings. First, foreign exchange (FX) moves prices invisibly: if the CAD strengthens 10% against the euro, the European list price (in euro) automatically falls 10% in Canadian terms. Second, grey markets arise when a price gap between countries lets opportunists buy where it's cheap and resell where it's expensive — eroding the manufacturer's pricing power.

Example: Landed cost calculation for a backpack exported to Brazil.

Factory cost \(C\$25\). Export packaging \(C\$1\). Inland freight Toronto-port \(C\$2\). Ocean freight \(C\$3\). Marine insurance \(C\$0.50\). Brazilian duty 20% on CIF (cost+insurance+freight = \(25+1+2+3+0.5 = 31.50\), so duty \(= 6.30\)). VAT/IPI 17% on (CIF + duty) = \(17\% \times 37.80 = 6.43\). Brokerage \(C\$1.20\). Total landed cost \(= 31.50 + 6.30 + 6.43 + 1.20 = C\$45.43\).

Distributor adds 25% margin: \(45.43 \times 1.25 = 56.79\). Retailer adds 40% gross margin: \(56.79 / (1 - 0.40) = 94.65\). The shelf price in Brazil is \(\approx C\$95\) — nearly 4× the factory cost. To match a Brazilian competitor selling at the equivalent of \(C\$80\), the firm must either accept thinner margins or simplify the product.

  • Quoting in CAD when the customer needs to plan in their local currency. Quote in EUR or USD if that is what the customer prices in.
  • Forgetting compounding: every margin in the chain is applied to the previous total.
  • Ignoring FX volatility. A 90-day quote in a foreign currency needs a hedge or a price-adjustment clause.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "describe pricing strategies used in international markets (cost-plus, market-based, penetration, skimming) and the factors that affect international pricing decisions, including duties, foreign exchange, and channel margins."

2.6 — Branding, Packaging & Adapting Promotion Across Cultures
Brand-name choices; trademark protection; cultural taboos in colour, symbol, image; high- vs low-context advertising; digital and social media internationally.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A brand is the name, sign, symbol, design, or combination thereof that identifies a seller's goods and differentiates them from competitors. Internationally, brand decisions multiply: should the brand name be the same in every country? Should packaging carry the same colour and image? Should promotion repeat the same story? The wrong answer can be expensive and embarrassing.

Famous translation traps illustrate the stakes. The Chevrolet Nova entered Latin American markets where "no va" means "doesn't go." Pepsi's slogan "Come alive with Pepsi" became, in Mandarin, something close to "Pepsi brings your ancestors back from the grave." KFC's "finger-lickin' good" in China rendered as "eat your fingers off." Most major firms now require a linguistic and cultural audit for every new market before any brand or campaign is launched.

Beyond language, colour and symbol meanings vary widely. White symbolizes purity in the West but mourning in much of East Asia. Red is celebratory in China but signals danger in Western utility contexts. The owl is wisdom in Greek tradition but bad luck in parts of India. Numbers carry meaning too — 4 is unlucky in Japan/Korea/China (sounds like "death"), 7 lucky in the West, 8 lucky in Chinese culture (sounds like "prosperity").

Promotion in high-context cultures (Japan, China, Saudi Arabia) tends to be more visual, suggestive, and relational; promotion in low-context cultures (Germany, Netherlands, US) tends to be more direct, factual, and comparative. Digital platforms also shift: TikTok dominates short-form video in Indonesia and Vietnam, WeChat is the everything-app in China, KakaoTalk in Korea, WhatsApp in India and Brazil. Promotion plans must account for which platform reaches the target.

  • Brand name: the part of a brand that can be spoken.
  • Trademark: the legally registered, protected mark.
  • Linguistic audit: review by native speakers of brand and copy.
  • High- vs low-context promotion: implicit/visual vs explicit/factual.
  • Cultural taboo: a symbol, colour, gesture, or image that offends locally.

Example: Adapting a Canadian craft-beer brand for Japan.

The Canadian brand is "Beaver Tail Ale," using a beaver and a maple-leaf shield. In Japan, the beaver is unfamiliar and the maple leaf reads simply as Canada-imports. The firm keeps the English brand name (Japanese consumers value Western lettering on premium imports), drops the beaver in favour of a stylized leaf, prints calorie info in Japanese, and runs a launch campaign on Instagram and Yahoo Japan featuring Canadian skiing imagery. The localized brand is Beaver Tail Ale — Canadian Premium Lager / カナディアン・プレミアム. Two years in, sales support the standardized name and the adapted visual.

  • Assuming colour and number meaning carry over. They often invert.
  • Skipping a native-language audit. Cheap to do, expensive to skip.
  • Using one global agency without local creatives. Local insight beats imported polish.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "analyse the ways in which branding decisions, packaging, and labelling are influenced by language, regulation, and culture in international markets."
