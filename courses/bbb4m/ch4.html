4.1 — Core Export Documents
Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading (B/L) and air waybill (AWB), certificate of origin, export declaration; what each document does and what it must contain.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An international shipment is followed by a paper trail (today, mostly an electronic one) that proves what was sold, what was shipped, and on what terms. Customs authorities, banks, insurers, and the buyer all rely on this paperwork. A missing or wrong document can hold a container at the port for weeks. The five documents every BBB4M student should know:

  • Commercial invoice — the seller's bill to the buyer. Lists buyer/seller, terms of sale (Incoterm), description and HS code of each item, unit price, total price, currency, payment terms, country of origin. Used by customs to assess duty and by the buyer to pay.
  • Packing list — itemizes each carton in the shipment by weight, dimensions, and contents. Used by carriers to load, and by the buyer to verify on arrival.
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) for sea/road/rail or Air Waybill (AWB) for air — the contract of carriage between shipper and carrier. The B/L is also a document of title: the original B/L is required to claim the goods at the destination port. The AWB is a receipt only, not a document of title.
  • Certificate of Origin — declares the country in which the goods were produced. Critical for claiming preferential tariff rates under FTAs like CUSMA, CETA, or CPTPP. Often certified by a chamber of commerce.
  • Export declaration / B13A in Canada — the exporter's declaration to CBSA describing the shipment. Required above a value threshold (Statistics Canada also uses the data).

Banks involved in payment (especially under a letter of credit) require additional documents — typically a marine insurance certificate, an inspection certificate, and any product-specific certificates (phytosanitary for plants, halal for meat). The principle: every document supports a specific party's role in the transaction.

Example: A Canadian honey exporter ships to a German importer.

The package of documents the exporter prepares for the buyer's bank: commercial invoice (12 cases, 12 × 24 jars × 500 g, FOB Montreal, EUR 4{,}320 total); packing list (with carton numbers and weights); bill of lading showing "Shipper: Honey Inc., Consignee: Honig GmbH, Vessel: MV Hapag-Lloyd Athens, Port of loading: Montreal, Port of discharge: Hamburg"; certificate of origin (Canada, certified by the Toronto Region Board of Trade) so the importer can claim preferential CETA tariff (0% on honey); phytosanitary certificate from the Canadian Food Inspection Agency; export declaration filed electronically through CBSA's CERS. Once the goods arrive and these documents are presented, the German bank releases payment.

  • Treating the commercial invoice and packing list as interchangeable. They serve different purposes and customs requires both.
  • Issuing a "telex release" B/L when the buyer hasn't paid. Until original B/Ls are surrendered, the goods are not legally the buyer's.
  • Omitting the Incoterm on the invoice. Without it, the buyer disputes who pays freight.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.1 — "describe the purpose and content of core export documents, including the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, and export declaration."

4.2 — The Letter of Credit (L/C) — How Banks Make Risky Trade Safe
Parties to an L/C; how the L/C process works step by step; types (sight, usance, confirmed, irrevocable, standby); strengths and limitations.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The letter of credit (L/C) — or documentary credit — is the central instrument of trade finance and has been used in some form since medieval Italian banking. It solves the fundamental problem of international trade: the seller wants to be paid before shipping; the buyer wants to receive goods before paying; and neither trusts the other. The L/C inserts two banks as intermediaries: each side trusts its own bank, the two banks trust each other, and the deal goes through.

Parties to a typical L/C:

  • Applicant — the importer/buyer who applies for the L/C.
  • Issuing bank — the buyer's bank, which issues the L/C and undertakes to pay if documents conform.
  • Beneficiary — the exporter/seller who will be paid.
  • Advising bank — a bank in the seller's country that authenticates the L/C and passes it to the seller.
  • Confirming bank (optional) — adds its own undertaking to pay; protects the seller if the issuing bank fails or its country is risky.

The process (simplified):

  1. Buyer and seller agree on payment by L/C and on the documents required.
  2. Buyer applies; issuing bank issues the L/C and forwards it to advising bank.
  3. Advising bank notifies the seller; the seller checks that the L/C terms match the contract.
  4. Seller ships the goods and assembles the required documents.
  5. Seller presents the documents to the advising/confirming bank, which checks compliance.
  6. If documents comply, the bank pays the seller (sight L/C) or accepts the time draft (usance L/C).
  7. Documents move to the issuing bank, which is reimbursed by the buyer; documents are released to the buyer, who claims the goods.

L/C variants: irrevocable (cannot be changed without all-party consent — the default), confirmed (extra bank guarantee), sight (paid on document presentation) vs usance (paid at a later date), standby (a guarantee that pays only if the buyer defaults — used like a bond). The international rule book is UCP 600, issued by the ICC.

Example: Tracing a US$120,000 L/C transaction.

Canadian seller and Indonesian buyer agree on US\$120,000, FOB Halifax, irrevocable confirmed sight L/C through Indonesian buyer's bank (Bank Mandiri) confirmed by Royal Bank of Canada (RBC). Seller ships, presents documents (invoice, packing list, full set of original B/Ls, certificate of origin, insurance) to RBC, which checks compliance against the L/C and — finding no discrepancies — credits the seller US\$120,000 within 5 banking days. RBC then claims reimbursement from Mandiri, which debits the buyer's account. If the seller had presented documents with a typo on the consignee name, RBC could refuse payment (a discrepancy), the seller would have to amend or fix, and the deal could stall — illustrating why every L/C transaction is partly an exercise in proofreading.

  • Confusing irrevocable (default) with confirmed (added bank guarantee). They are different protections.
  • Ignoring discrepancies. Banks pay only against documents that strictly match the L/C terms.
  • Choosing an L/C for low-risk relationships. L/Cs are expensive — a percent or two of value plus fees. Open account or documentary collection may be cheaper.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "describe the function of a letter of credit (L/C) in international trade, including the parties involved (applicant, beneficiary, issuing bank, advising bank, confirming bank) and the steps in the L/C process."

4.3 — Methods of International Payment
Cash in advance, letter of credit, documentary collection, open account, consignment; cost vs risk trade-off; matching payment method to relationship.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

How does money cross borders to pay for goods? Exporters and importers have five main choices, ranked from most-secure-for-seller to most-secure-for-buyer:

  • Cash in advance — buyer pays before the seller ships. Lowest risk for seller, highest for buyer. Common for small orders, custom-built goods, or buyers with no credit history. Wire transfer (SWIFT) is the typical instrument.
  • Letter of credit (L/C) — bank-mediated promise of payment (see 4.2). Balanced risk; expensive.
  • Documentary collection (D/P, D/A) — banks pass documents through without guaranteeing payment. Under Documents Against Payment (D/P), the buyer pays to get the documents; under Documents Against Acceptance (D/A), the buyer accepts a time draft and pays at maturity. Cheaper than L/C, less secure for the seller.
  • Open account — seller ships and invoices; buyer pays in 30/60/90 days. Most common method for established Canada–US trade and within established relationships. Highest risk for seller absent credit insurance.
  • Consignment — seller ships the goods but retains ownership until the buyer resells them. Common in fashion and consumer goods entering a new market.

The choice is a function of relationship trust, value at risk, country risk, and competitive pressure. Buyers in mature markets routinely demand open account; demanding a confirmed L/C from a long-standing US distributor would be uncompetitive. Conversely, a first sale into a politically risky market typically uses a confirmed L/C.

Canadian exporters also have access to export-credit support: Export Development Canada (EDC) insures accounts receivable from foreign buyers, often allowing exporters to offer open-account terms safely. The Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC) finances working capital. These supports are widely used by SMEs.

Example: Choosing a payment method.

Exporter A: a Canadian organic-cosmetics maker selling \$15,000 of product to a long-time US Whole Foods buyer. Recommended: open account, 60 days. Why: low country risk, established relationship, modest amount. Add EDC insurance for backup.

Exporter B: the same maker selling \$80,000 of product to a new Egyptian distributor. Recommended: confirmed irrevocable sight L/C through a major Canadian bank. Why: new buyer, higher country risk, larger amount. The cost (roughly 1.5% of value) is justified by the protection.

Exporter C: same maker, sample order to a new specialty store in South Korea for \$2,500. Recommended: cash in advance via wire transfer. Why: small amount makes an L/C uneconomic; the buyer is willing to prepay for samples.

  • Insisting on cash in advance with established buyers. Competitive exporters offer open account.
  • Open account without credit checks or insurance. Bad debt can erase a year's profit.
  • Treating L/C as guaranteed payment. Banks pay only against compliant documents.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "compare the main methods of international payment (cash in advance, letter of credit, documentary collection, open account, consignment) and analyse their risk and cost trade-offs."

4.4 — Tariffs, Duties, Taxes & Non-Tariff Barriers
Ad valorem and specific tariffs; the Harmonized System (HS); GST/VAT/duty calculation; quotas, embargoes, standards, technical/regulatory barriers.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Tariffs are taxes governments impose on imported goods. They raise revenue and protect domestic industries — but they also raise consumer prices and invite retaliation. Modern WTO membership has reduced average tariffs sharply, but they remain significant in some sectors (Canada's dairy supply management; US steel; many countries' autos and agriculture).

Two main forms:

  • Ad valorem tariff — a percentage of the customs value (e.g., 6% of CIF value).
  • Specific tariff — a fixed amount per unit (e.g., \(C\$0.50\) per kilogram).
  • Compound tariff — both.

To know which tariff applies, every traded product is classified under the Harmonized System (HS), a globally standardized six-digit code maintained by the World Customs Organization. Countries extend it to 8 or 10 digits (Canada uses 8-digit HS codes, US uses 10-digit). The HS code determines the duty rate, any quota, and any regulatory requirement. Misclassification can mean overpaying or fines.

Beyond tariffs, non-tariff barriers (NTBs) can be just as restrictive:

  • Quotas — quantity limits.
  • Embargoes — outright bans on trade with a country (Cuba, North Korea, currently Russia for some goods).
  • Technical standards — safety, environmental, labelling (CE, FDA, CSA).
  • Sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) measures — disease and pest controls on food and plants.
  • Local content / domestic production requirements.
  • Subsidies to domestic producers, which act like a negative tariff.

Most countries also levy a domestic value-added tax (VAT) or GST/HST on imported goods, calculated on the customs value plus duty. The exporter typically does not pay this — the importer does — but the price the consumer pays reflects it.

Example: Duty and tax on a backpack imported to Canada from Bangladesh.

Customs value (CIF Halifax) \(= C\$25\). Canadian MFN tariff rate on this HS subheading: 18%. Bangladesh enjoys preferential treatment under Canada's General Preferential Tariff (GPT): 0%. With a valid certificate of origin claiming GPT: duty \(= 0\). GST \(= 5\%\) on customs value plus duty \(= 5\% \times 25 = C\$1.25\). If the same backpack came from a country without preferential treatment, duty \(= 0.18 \times 25 = C\$4.50\); GST \(= 5\% \times (25 + 4.50) = C\$1.475\); total Canadian taxes \(= C\$5.98\) versus \(C\$1.25\) for Bangladesh. The certificate of origin is worth \(C\$4.73\) per backpack.

  • Quoting "the tariff" without specifying ad valorem vs specific.
  • Forgetting GST/VAT in landed cost. It can equal or exceed the tariff.
  • Treating misclassification as harmless. Customs audits and re-classifies; back-duties plus penalties can be steep.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.3 — "analyse the impact of tariffs, duties, taxes, and non-tariff barriers (e.g., quotas, embargoes, technical standards) on the cost and competitiveness of international business."

4.5 — Foreign Exchange Markets & Currency Conversion
Spot vs forward rates; direct vs indirect quotes; bid/ask spread; converting between currencies; the FX market structure.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The foreign exchange (FX) market is where currencies are bought and sold. It is the largest market in the world by turnover — over US\$7.5 trillion of trades per day, far larger than all stock markets combined. Major participants are commercial banks, central banks, multinational corporations, and increasingly fintech platforms. Trading happens 24 hours a day across global financial centres (Tokyo → Singapore → London → New York → Sydney).

Three rate concepts every BBB4M student must know:

  • Spot rate — the price for immediate exchange (settlement in 1–2 days).
  • Forward rate — the price for exchange at a specific future date, locked in today.
  • Bid/Ask spread — the difference between the price a dealer will pay (bid) and the price it will sell at (ask). The spread is the dealer's compensation.

Quote conventions matter. A direct quote states home currency per unit of foreign (Canadian \(\$1.36/\)USD means each US dollar costs \(C\$1.36\)). An indirect quote reverses it (US\(\$0.74/\)CAD means each Canadian dollar costs about 74 US cents). The two are reciprocals: \(1/1.36 = 0.735\).

Currency conversion is straightforward arithmetic — but business decisions need both directions. When pricing an export priced in foreign currency, convert at the bid (the rate the firm will receive when selling foreign currency); when paying for an import, convert at the ask (the rate the firm pays to buy foreign currency).

Example: Converting a German invoice.

A Canadian importer must pay EUR 80{,}000 to a German supplier. Bank quote: EUR/CAD bid 1.450 / ask 1.460. To buy euros (importer paying), the importer uses the ask: \(80{,}000 \times 1.460 = C\$116{,}800\). If three months later the firm has earned EUR 50{,}000 from a parallel export deal, the bank now uses the bid to convert that back: \(50{,}000 \times 1.450 = C\$72{,}500\). The 1.0¢ spread on each side is the bank's profit.

If the same importer locks a 90-day forward at \(1.455\), the EUR 80{,}000 cost is fixed today at \(80{,}000 \times 1.455 = C\$116{,}400\), regardless of where the spot moves over 90 days.

  • Mixing up direct and indirect quotes. Always note which currency is on top.
  • Treating bid and ask as equal. Real conversions lose the spread; large transactions can negotiate tighter spreads.
  • Forgetting which side of the spread applies. Buying foreign currency uses the ask; selling foreign currency uses the bid.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.1 — "explain how foreign-exchange markets work, including spot vs forward rates, direct vs indirect quotes, and bid/ask spreads."

4.6 — Managing FX, Political & Trade Risk
Forward contracts, natural hedging, currency clauses; political risk insurance; trade-credit insurance (EDC); using the right Incoterm to shift risk.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

International business carries risks no domestic business faces: currency movements between order and payment, political shocks, transport accidents, buyer default thousands of kilometres away. Managers cannot avoid these risks, but they can transfer, reduce, or accept them deliberately.

FX risk arises whenever revenue or cost is in a currency other than the reporting currency. Three main hedging tools:

  • Forward contract — lock in a future exchange rate today (most common SME tool).
  • Currency option — pay a premium for the right, not the obligation, to convert at a set rate.
  • Natural hedge — match foreign-currency revenue with foreign-currency cost (a Canadian exporter to the US that also imports US inputs has a partial natural hedge).

Contracts can also include currency-adjustment clauses that automatically share an FX gain or loss between buyer and seller if the rate moves beyond a defined band — common in long-term contracts.

Political and country risk includes expropriation, currency convertibility crises, war, civil disturbance, and sudden regulatory shifts. Mitigation: avoid concentration in any one country, buy political-risk insurance (EDC, MIGA, Lloyd's), use confirmed L/Cs from banks in stable jurisdictions, and structure investments under bilateral investment treaties (BITs).

Trade-credit risk is the risk a foreign buyer doesn't pay. Mitigation: customer credit checks (Dun & Bradstreet), trade-credit insurance (e.g., EDC Receivables Insurance covers up to 90% of an uncollectable foreign receivable), shorter payment terms, factoring.

Transport and force-majeure risk: cargo lost, damaged, delayed, or stuck (a strike, a Suez blockage, a typhoon). Mitigation: marine cargo insurance (Institute Cargo Clauses A/B/C), choice of Incoterm (CIF/CIP shifts insurance to the seller), diversification of routes and carriers.

Example: Hedging a EUR 200,000 receivable.

A Canadian software firm signs a EUR 200,000 sale due in 6 months. Spot EUR/CAD = 1.46; 6-month forward = 1.452. If the firm leaves the position unhedged and the euro falls to 1.38 in six months, the firm collects \(200{,}000 \times 1.38 = C\$276{,}000\) — about \(C\$14{,}400\) less than the \(C\$290{,}400\) it would have received at spot. If instead the firm sells EUR 200,000 forward at 1.452, it locks in \(200{,}000 \times 1.452 = C\$290{,}400\) regardless of where the spot moves. The cost of the hedge is the forward discount (0.55% in this example) plus a small bank fee. The CFO can now report a predictable revenue figure.

  • Speculating disguised as hedging. Hedging removes uncertainty; speculation chases gains.
  • Not insuring high-value receivables in risky countries. EDC and private insurers are cheaper than bad-debt write-offs.
  • Choosing an Incoterm without considering insurance. Under EXW, the seller has no insurance obligation — but the buyer must absolutely arrange it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.3 — "analyse non-financial risks in international business (political, transport, credit, force-majeure) and explain the role of trade-credit insurance, hedging, and incoterm choice in mitigating them."
