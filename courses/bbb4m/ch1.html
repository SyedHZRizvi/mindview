1.1 — Why International Business? Importance to Canada
Trade as a share of Canada's GDP, jobs linked to imports/exports, top trading partners, and why almost every Canadian business is touched by global trade.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada is one of the most trade-dependent advanced economies in the world. Exports and imports of goods and services together represent roughly two-thirds of Canada's Gross Domestic Product (GDP), the total market value of all final goods and services produced inside the country in a given year. That ratio — known as the trade-to-GDP ratio — is significantly higher than for large continental economies like the United States or Japan. The practical consequence is that Canadian wages, jobs, prices, and standards of living are tightly coupled to what happens outside our borders.

The United States is Canada's largest trading partner by a wide margin, taking the majority of Canadian merchandise exports, followed by China, the United Kingdom, Japan, and the European Union as a bloc. Energy products, motor vehicles and parts, agriculture, mining, forestry, and increasingly digital services dominate the export list. On the import side, Canadians consume consumer electronics, machinery, vehicles, and clothing largely produced abroad. Approximately one in every five Canadian jobs is directly linked to trade — sales, logistics, customs, manufacturing for export, and tourism — and many more are linked indirectly.

International business covers all commercial transactions between organizations of two or more countries: exporting, importing, licensing, franchising, foreign direct investment, and increasingly cross-border e-commerce. For students, understanding international business is no longer optional; even a Canadian retail clerk works with imported inventory priced in foreign currency, paid for under foreign-exchange contracts, and shipped under international rules.

  • International business: commercial transactions that cross national borders.
  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product): total market value of final goods and services produced inside a country in a year.
  • Trade-to-GDP ratio: (Exports + Imports) / GDP — a measure of openness.
  • Merchandise vs services trade: physical goods vs intangibles (engineering, software, tourism, banking).
  • Trade surplus / deficit: exports minus imports, positive or negative.

Example: Canada's Trade Openness

Suppose in a given year Canada's GDP is \(C\$2{,}800\) billion, total exports are \(C\$880\) billion, and total imports are \(C\$900\) billion.

Trade-to-GDP ratio \( = \dfrac{880 + 900}{2{,}800} = \dfrac{1{,}780}{2{,}800} \approx 0.636 \), or about \(63.6\%\). The trade balance is \(880 - 900 = -C\$20\) billion (a small deficit). Both numbers tell a Canadian business that growth opportunities — and risks — lie heavily outside the country.

  • Confusing trade balance with trade-to-GDP ratio. The first measures surplus/deficit; the second measures openness.
  • Assuming "exports = good, imports = bad". Imports raise consumer welfare and supply inputs for export production.
  • Overlooking services trade. Tourism, fintech, and engineering exports are a fast-growing share.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — "describe the importance of international business to Canada and Canadian companies (e.g., contribution to GDP, employment, standard of living)."

1.2 — Forces Driving Globalization & Forms of International Business
Technology, transport, deregulation, multilateral agreements, and FDI; exporting, importing, licensing, franchising, joint ventures, FDI, and e-commerce as forms of international activity.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Globalization is the increasing interconnection of national economies through trade, investment, technology, and the movement of people. It is not new — silk-road merchants and Hudson Bay traders were globalizers — but the pace and scope have exploded since the 1980s. Five forces explain the surge: dramatic falls in the real cost of transportation (jet aircraft, containerization), the near-zero marginal cost of digital communication (the internet, mobile, cloud), deregulation of finance and capital flows, the spread of multilateral trade agreements (GATT/WTO, regional pacts), and rising foreign direct investment (FDI) by multinational enterprises (MNEs).

Once a firm decides to go international, it must choose how. The most common forms, ordered roughly by commitment and risk:

  • Exporting — selling Canadian-made goods abroad; lowest commitment, fastest to start.
  • Importing — buying foreign goods for resale or input.
  • Licensing — granting a foreign firm the right to use IP (patent, trademark, know-how) for royalties.
  • Franchising — a packaged business model (Tim Hortons, McDonald's) operated abroad under an ongoing fee.
  • Joint venture (JV) — a shared-equity company with a local partner; shares risk and local knowledge.
  • Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) — building or buying productive assets abroad (factory, mine, retail chain). Highest commitment, highest control.
  • Cross-border e-commerce — direct-to-consumer online selling globally; bypasses traditional intermediaries.

Choice depends on capital, control, speed, knowledge of the local market, and the risk tolerance of the home firm. A small Canadian craft brewery may license its recipes in Asia; Bombardier or Magna make large FDI commitments through wholly-owned plants.

Example: Choosing an entry mode for a Canadian maple-syrup producer.

The firm wants to enter Japan. Direct exporting is fastest but provides little market intelligence. Licensing or franchising won't preserve the craft-product premium. A joint venture with a Japanese specialty-food distributor offers shared risk, local distribution access, and Japanese-language marketing in exchange for shared profits. The producer chooses the JV.

  • Treating "globalization" as a single decision. It is many decisions — what to sell, how to enter, under what brand.
  • Confusing licensing with franchising. Franchising bundles the entire business model (training, supply chain, branding); licensing is narrower.
  • Ignoring exit costs of FDI. A factory abroad is hard to close quickly.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "analyse the role of changes in the global economy (e.g., technology, transportation, communication, deregulation, foreign direct investment) on international business."

1.3 — Comparative Advantage & Why Countries Trade
Absolute vs comparative advantage; opportunity cost; gains from specialization and trade; modern factors of competitiveness (resources, labour, capital, technology, entrepreneurship).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Countries do not trade simply because some have apples and others have oranges. They trade because the opportunity cost of producing a good differs across countries. The classical argument is David Ricardo's Theory of Comparative Advantage (1817): even if one country can produce every good more efficiently in absolute terms, both countries gain by specializing in what they produce relatively most efficiently and trading the rest. The result is more total output and lower world prices.

Absolute advantage means producing a good with fewer inputs per unit than another country. Comparative advantage means producing it with a lower opportunity cost. Opportunity cost is what is given up of one good to make one more unit of another. Modern competitiveness adds the Porter Diamond factors: natural resources, labour skill and cost, capital, infrastructure, technology and innovation, and entrepreneurship/management. Canada's lumber, potash, oil, and aerospace strengths emerge from this combination, not from any one factor alone.

  • Absolute advantage: lower inputs per unit of output.
  • Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost of production.
  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next-best alternative given up.
  • Specialization: concentrating resources on goods where comparative advantage is highest.
  • Factor endowments: a country's stock of land, labour, capital, and technology.

Example: Two countries, two goods (Ricardo's classic illustration).

Suppose with one hour of labour, Canada can make 4 barrels of maple syrup OR 2 laptops, while Mexico can make 1 barrel of maple syrup OR 2 laptops. Canada has the absolute advantage in syrup; both are equally productive at laptops. But compare opportunity costs:

Canada: 1 laptop costs \(4/2 = 2\) barrels of syrup; 1 barrel of syrup costs \(2/4 = 0.5\) laptops.
Mexico: 1 laptop costs \(1/2 = 0.5\) barrels of syrup; 1 barrel of syrup costs \(2/1 = 2\) laptops.

Mexico gives up less syrup to make a laptop, so it has the comparative advantage in laptops. Canada gives up fewer laptops per barrel of syrup, so it has the comparative advantage in syrup. Each specializes and trades — both gain.

  • Confusing absolute and comparative advantage. Absolute looks at raw productivity; comparative looks at trade-offs.
  • Assuming a country with absolute advantage in everything should produce everything. Comparative advantage still gives reasons to specialize and trade.
  • Ignoring transport cost and trade barriers, which can erode comparative-advantage gains.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.3 — "compare the major economic systems and indicators (e.g., GDP, GNI, HDI) and explain how economic factors influence international business decisions and comparative advantage."

1.4 — Political, Legal & Economic Systems Around the World
Democratic vs authoritarian states; civil-law, common-law, and theocratic-law systems; market, command, mixed, and traditional economies; political risk and country-risk indices.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every international business decision is shaped by the political, legal, and economic system of the host country. Political systems range along a spectrum from full democracies (Canada, Germany, Japan) — where governments are elected, press is free, and rule of law is strong — to authoritarian systems where political opposition is restricted. Authoritarian systems are not automatically bad for business; some (Singapore, Saudi Arabia) are highly stable. But they raise different risks: sudden policy reversal, expropriation, and limited recourse to independent courts.

Legal systems fall into three broad families. Common law (UK, Canada except Quebec, US, Australia) builds rules from judicial precedent and emphasizes contract freedom. Civil law (France, Germany, Japan, most of Latin America, Quebec) is codified — statutes spell out rules in advance. Theocratic law (Saudi Arabia, Iran in part) derives from religious texts. A firm exporting to Saudi Arabia, for example, must conform to Islamic contract conventions including the avoidance of interest (riba) in some financing structures.

Economic systems answer who owns the means of production and how resources are allocated. A market economy (Canada, US, UK, Singapore) relies on private ownership and prices. A command economy (Cuba, North Korea) uses central planning. A mixed economy (most countries today, including China) blends both. A traditional economy (small pockets of subsistence agriculture) relies on customs. Key indicators a manager checks: GDP per capita, GNI per capita, HDI (Human Development Index), inflation, unemployment, interest rate, and the country's score on indices of corruption (Transparency International) and ease of doing business.

  • Political risk: the chance that political events (expropriation, war, regulatory change) reduce business returns.
  • Rule of law: consistent, predictable, enforceable rules — a key risk indicator.
  • Common, civil, theocratic law: three major legal families.
  • GDP per capita: GDP divided by population — average income per person.
  • HDI: United Nations index combining income, life expectancy, and education.

Example: Assessing political/economic fit.

A Canadian medical-device firm is choosing between Vietnam and Mexico for assembly. Vietnam: command-leaning mixed economy, civil-law system, HDI \(\approx 0.70\), GDP per capita \(\approx \text{US}\$4{,}200\). Mexico: mixed market economy, civil-law, HDI \(\approx 0.78\), GDP per capita \(\approx \text{US}\$11{,}500\), USMCA member providing tariff-free entry to Canada and the US. For a product destined for North American markets, the firm picks Mexico because USMCA removes tariffs and the legal system aligns more closely with Canadian contract norms. Vietnam remains attractive for the Asia-Pacific market.

  • Equating "developed = democratic" or "authoritarian = high risk". Each country must be assessed on its own indicators.
  • Using GDP without dividing by population. A large total GDP can hide low per-capita income.
  • Treating legal systems as interchangeable. A common-law contract drafted for the UK may not enforce in a civil-law jurisdiction without changes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "analyse how political and legal factors (e.g., political stability, rule of law, intellectual-property protection, taxation) influence international business decisions and risk."

1.5 — Socio-Cultural Factors & Hofstede's Dimensions
Language, religion, customs, values, demographics, and Hofstede's framework (power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation, indulgence).
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Culture — defined as the learned, shared values and behaviours of a group — is invisible to insiders and decisive for international business. Language, religion, customs, food habits, family structure, attitudes toward time, and demographic patterns each affect what consumers buy, how negotiations unfold, and how staff are managed. The single most cited framework for analysing these differences is the work of Dutch researcher Geert Hofstede, who originally surveyed IBM employees in 50+ countries.

Hofstede's six dimensions:

  • Power Distance (PDI): the extent to which less-powerful members accept unequal distribution of power. Higher in China, Mexico, Saudi Arabia; lower in Canada, Denmark, Israel.
  • Individualism vs Collectivism (IDV): the strength of ties between individual and group. Higher individualism in Canada, US, UK; collectivism in Japan, South Korea, much of Latin America.
  • Masculinity vs Femininity (MAS): emphasis on achievement and assertiveness vs cooperation and quality of life. Higher in Japan; lower in Sweden, Norway.
  • Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI): tolerance for ambiguity. Higher in Greece, Portugal; lower in Singapore, UK.
  • Long-Term vs Short-Term Orientation (LTO): focus on future rewards. Higher in China, South Korea; lower in the US, Australia.
  • Indulgence vs Restraint (IVR): attitudes toward gratification of desires. Higher in Mexico, Canada; lower in Russia, Egypt.

A second useful framework is Edward T. Hall's high-context vs low-context communication. In high-context cultures (Japan, China, Arab world) much meaning is implied — through silence, hierarchy, body language. In low-context cultures (Canada, Germany, Scandinavia) meaning is spelled out explicitly in words and contracts. Mismatched expectations here cause more failed deals than language alone.

Example: Designing a sales presentation.

A Canadian sales rep visits potential clients in Tokyo (high PDI, high LTO, high context) and Stockholm (low PDI, short-term, low context). In Tokyo, the rep prepares a senior delegation, exchanges business cards with two hands, allows extended silence, never names the most senior buyer in the proposal, and emphasizes a long-term relationship. In Stockholm, the same rep arrives alone, gets to the agenda immediately, uses informal first names, and emphasizes measurable ROI in the first quarter. Same product, two presentations.

  • Treating culture as a stereotype. Hofstede scores describe national averages, not individuals.
  • Assuming everyone speaks English well. Misunderstanding rises sharply when one side translates in real time.
  • Ignoring religious and dietary norms. Pork or alcohol in a corporate gift can offend Muslim, Jewish, or Hindu clients.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.4 — "analyse the effects of socio-cultural factors (e.g., language, religion, values, customs, demographics) on international business and on the strategies that businesses use abroad."

1.6 — Trade Agreements & Trading Blocs (WTO, CUSMA, CETA, CPTPP, EU)
The role of the WTO, IMF, and World Bank; bilateral vs multilateral agreements; CUSMA, CETA, CPTPP for Canada; the EU, ASEAN, and Mercosur as trading blocs.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Trade does not happen in a rule-less space. Three Bretton-Woods era institutions and a network of treaties shape the global trading system. The World Trade Organization (WTO), headquartered in Geneva and successor to the 1947 GATT, sets rules that lower tariffs, ban discriminatory taxes, and resolve disputes among its 160+ members. Its core principle is most-favoured-nation (MFN) treatment — a tariff cut for one member must be extended to all. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) stabilizes the global financial system, lends to countries in balance-of-payments crisis, and surveils macroeconomic policies. The World Bank finances long-term development projects in poorer countries.

On top of WTO multilateralism, countries sign preferential trade agreements with selected partners. Canada's most important are: CUSMA (Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement, replacing NAFTA in 2020) covering goods, services, autos, agriculture, IP, and labour; CETA (Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement) with the European Union, removing tariffs on ~98% of goods; and the CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership), an 11-country pact spanning the Pacific Rim including Japan, Australia, Vietnam, and Singapore.

Trading blocs deepen the integration beyond a single agreement. They come in degrees: a free-trade area removes internal tariffs (CUSMA); a customs union adds a common external tariff (Mercosur of South America); a common market adds free movement of labour and capital; an economic union adds harmonized policy and often a shared currency (the European Union, with the euro for most members). For a Canadian exporter, knowing whether the destination is inside one of these blocs determines duties, rules of origin, and competitive position against bloc insiders.

  • WTO: the rule-maker and dispute-settler of multilateral trade.
  • Most-Favoured Nation (MFN): non-discriminatory tariff treatment.
  • Free-trade agreement (FTA): removes tariffs between signatories.
  • Customs union: FTA plus a common external tariff.
  • Common market / economic union: add free movement of factors / harmonized policy.
  • Rules of origin: requirements that a good be sufficiently transformed within a member country to qualify for preferential rates.

Example: Why a car bumper is "Canadian".

Under CUSMA, a passenger vehicle must contain at least 75% North American content (rising from 62.5% under NAFTA) and 40–45% must be made by workers earning at least US\$16/hour to qualify for zero tariff. A Canadian-owned plant near Windsor produces bumpers using 60% Canadian steel, 25% Mexican plastic, 15% Japanese fasteners. Total North American content: \(60 + 25 = 85\%\), above the 75% threshold — so the bumper qualifies for tariff-free entry to the US and Mexico. Replacing the Japanese fasteners with US-made ones would also satisfy the rule even if the percent edged closer to the line.

  • Confusing "FTA" with "the EU". An FTA removes tariffs; the EU is a deeper economic and political union.
  • Ignoring rules of origin. An exporter that fails to document Canadian content cannot claim preferential rates and ends up paying full MFN tariff.
  • Forgetting that some sectors (dairy, supply-managed) remain protected even under modern FTAs.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A3.1 — "describe the roles of major international trade organizations (e.g., WTO, IMF, World Bank) and trade agreements (e.g., CUSMA, CETA, CPTPP) in supporting and regulating global business."
