🔍 6.1 The Six Historical Thinking Concepts
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Historical thinking is the set of disciplined intellectual moves historians use to make defensible claims about the past. Peter Seixas and Tom Morton, working at the University of British Columbia's Historical Thinking Project, identified six concepts that structure those moves; their framework underpins Ontario's history curriculum. Mastering these six concepts is what graduates a Grade 12 student from "knows history" to "thinks historically." The CHY4U research essay (topic 6.6) cannot be written without them.

The six concepts are: (1) historical significance, (2) cause and consequence, (3) continuity and change, (4) historical perspective (sometimes called perspective-taking or empathy), (5) the ethical dimension, and (6) evidence. Each is a question the historian asks of any topic.

Historical significance asks: why is this event, person, or development worth telling, given that the past contains effectively infinite events? Seixas and Morton's criteria — importance to people at the time; depth and durability of consequences; quantity of people affected; revealing of broader trends — provide a defensible procedure. The criterion can be reversed for "small" things that turn out to matter: a single decision by Maximilien Robespierre, a single execution warrant signed by Ferdinand and Isabella expelling Jews in 1492, a single letter by Albert Einstein urging research into atomic weapons. Significance is not given; it is argued.

Cause and consequence asks: what made this happen, what followed from it? The historian distinguishes long-term and short-term causes, necessary and sufficient causes, intended and unintended consequences, and the role of contingency. The First World War's causes (Chapter 4.1) is the textbook case: long-term militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism converged with short-term July Crisis triggers. Multi-causal analysis is the default in modern historiography.

Continuity and change asks: what changed across a period, and what stayed the same? The temptation is to focus on change because change is dramatic; the discipline is to notice continuity too. The "transformation of women's lives" between 1914 and 1945 involves both massive change (suffrage, factory work, professional employment) and significant continuity (the gender pay gap, primary caregiving expectations, restrictions on bodily autonomy). A defensible argument holds both.

Historical perspective — sometimes called perspective-taking — asks: how did people at the time understand their world? This is the discipline against presentism (judging the past by today's standards). It does not require excusing past wrongs; it requires understanding them in context. Medieval Christians thought about religious toleration through frameworks that did not include modern secular pluralism; understanding that does not require approving it.

The ethical dimension asks: what moral judgements can we defensibly make about past actions, and what responsibilities do they generate for the present? Strand A makes the ethical dimension explicit: the Atlantic slave trade, the Holocaust, residential schools, colonial atrocities require not only documentation but ethical reckoning. Holocaust education in Ontario (now mandated from Grade 6 since 2025) is built around this concept.

Evidence asks: how do we know what we know? Primary sources (documents created during the period studied) and secondary sources (later interpretations) require different kinds of scrutiny. Sourcing (provenance, perspective, purpose), contextualisation, corroboration with other sources, and recognition of what is missing are the basic moves. Topics 6.2 and 6.3 work through them in detail.

  • Historical significance — defensible judgement of which past events, people, or developments deserve attention.
  • Cause and consequence — multi-causal analysis of why things happen and what follows.
  • Continuity and change — recognising both what persists and what transforms across periods.
  • Historical perspective — understanding past worldviews on their own terms without presentism.
  • Ethical dimension — making evidence-based moral judgements about past actions and their present implications.
  • Evidence — disciplined source analysis (provenance, contextualisation, corroboration).
  • Presentism — anachronistic imposition of present-day values onto past actors.

Worked Example — Applying All Six Concepts to a Single Event

Consider the 1492 expulsion of Jews from Spain by Ferdinand and Isabella.

(1) Significance: highly significant — ended a thousand-year Jewish presence in Sepharad; created the Sephardic diaspora; foreshadowed religious persecution patterns; affected hundreds of thousands directly and the Jewish-Christian relationship for centuries. (2) Cause: long-term Christian-Muslim-Jewish tensions in Iberia; the Reconquista's completion; the Spanish Inquisition's logic of religious purification; royal interest in confiscated property. (3) Consequence: Sephardic communities established in Ottoman territories (Salonika, Istanbul), the Netherlands, Italy; Spanish economic decline as commercial expertise was lost; future Spanish persecution of "New Christians" descendants. (4) Historical perspective: the monarchs and their advisors framed the expulsion as a religious necessity; contemporaries (Jewish, Christian, Muslim) understood it within early-modern religious categories, not modern human-rights ones. (5) Ethical dimension: by any defensible ethical standard — including those articulated by 15th-century Jewish leaders like Don Isaac Abravanel — the expulsion was wrong; it caused mass suffering and was driven by religious hatred. (6) Evidence: we know about the expulsion from royal edicts (the Alhambra Decree of 31 March 1492), tax records, Inquisition trial records, Jewish chronicles, Genoese banking documents tracking refugee finance. Each source has biases; corroborating them is the historian's task. Practising this kind of multi-concept analysis on a single event is the discipline of historical thinking in action.

  • Treating "historical thinking" as an add-on rather than the core of history education.
  • Using "significance" without applying criteria — vague claims that "this changed everything."
  • Confusing perspective-taking with relativism — taking past worldviews seriously does not mean refusing ethical judgement.
  • Calling something "evidence" without sourcing it — even direct quotations require provenance.
  • Reducing causation to a single factor — most historical events have multiple converging causes.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.1 — formulate different types of questions to guide investigations into issues, events, and/or developments in world history since the fifteenth century.

📜 6.2 Working with Primary Sources
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A primary source is any document, image, object, or testimony created at or close to the time of the event studied. Letters, diaries, treaties, photographs, news reports, oral testimonies, government records, paintings, archaeological artefacts, songs, and posters are all primary sources. They are the raw evidence of history. A secondary source is a later interpretation — a textbook, a scholarly article, a documentary, a biography written long after the events. Both kinds of source are useful; both require critical reading; neither is "more true" than the other in the abstract.

The historian's basic procedure for any primary source has been formalised in the acronym SOURCES or in similar mnemonics. Whatever the mnemonic, the questions are: (1) Who created this? — author, role, position, biases, audience. (2) When? — temporal proximity to the events; what the author could and could not know yet. (3) Where? — geographical location; access to information; physical vantage point. (4) Why? — purpose; persuasion, record, accountability, propaganda, private. (5) For whom? — intended audience; how that shapes content. (6) What does it say directly? — explicit content. (7) What does it imply but not say? — assumptions, omissions, framing. (8) What is missing? — voices, perspectives, evidence not in the document. (9) What other sources corroborate or contradict? — the network of evidence.

The same primary source can support very different conclusions depending on the question asked. Hitler's 1924 Mein Kampf is evidence about Hitler's stated intentions in 1924; about Nazi ideology before mass support; about the genre of political autobiography; about the literary techniques of demagogic rhetoric; about what was publicly available to Western diplomats in the 1930s. A single source is not a "true" or "false" thing; it is a witness with a specific perspective, useful for some questions and limited for others.

Several specific pitfalls trap inexperienced students. The unreliable narrator: a memoir written 30 years after an event mixes memory, retrospective justification, and contemporary politics. The translation problem: a 15th-century Italian text translated into Victorian English carries the translator's choices. The selection problem: even an honest archive preserves what survives — letters of the literate, edicts of the powerful, while peasant or enslaved voices are systematically underrepresented. The genre problem: a treaty is a political document, a diary is private, a sermon is performative — all primary, all needing different reading.

Sources from oppressed groups require particular care. The voices of enslaved Africans in the 18th-century Atlantic world reach us mainly through three channels: (1) court depositions and legal documents (filtered through European legal categories); (2) the small number of slave narratives published by literate ex-slaves (Olaudah Equiano 1789, Mary Prince 1831, Frederick Douglass 1845, Harriet Jacobs 1861); (3) post-emancipation oral-history projects (the WPA Slave Narratives of the 1930s collected from formerly enslaved Americans then in their 80s and 90s). Each source has limitations, but their existence forces the historian to read European records "against the grain" — looking for what enslaved people did rather than what their captors said about them.

Modern primary sources have their own difficulties. A tweet, a social-media post, a digital photograph carry metadata that documents-on-paper did not. They are also easier to falsify, ephemeral (URLs disappear), and so abundant that selection becomes the historian's central problem. The Internet Archive, the Wayback Machine, and emerging archival standards address these issues, but the contemporary historian has source problems unlike those of the early-modern historian. The basic discipline — provenance, purpose, perspective — is unchanged.

  • Primary source — document, image, object, or testimony from the period studied.
  • Secondary source — later interpretation by a historian, journalist, or other writer.
  • Provenance — origin and ownership history of a source.
  • Corroboration — confirmation of a source's claims by independent other sources.
  • Reading against the grain — analysing a source for what it inadvertently reveals beyond its author's purpose.
  • Archive bias — systematic over- or under-representation of certain voices in surviving records.
  • Anachronism — projection of later concepts or technologies into a past period (e.g., calling 16th-century Europeans "racist" by 21st-century definitions).

Worked Example — Two Sources on the Same Event

The Boston Massacre of 5 March 1770: nine British soldiers under Captain Thomas Preston fired on a Boston crowd, killing five. Two famous engravings followed.

Paul Revere's engraving (March 1770) depicts the redcoats in disciplined firing line, the captain ordering the shooting, the colonists peaceful with no weapons, a small dog standing calmly between the parties. The title at the bottom reads "The Bloody Massacre." Revere was a Boston silversmith and Sons-of-Liberty activist. The engraving was sold to fund anti-British propaganda; it was distributed widely and helped shape American colonial opinion.

Henry Pelham's engraving (March 1770, on which Revere's was likely based) shows similar composition but is less ideologically heightened. A British soldier's later deposition — the loyalist printer published the soldiers' statements — describes a crowd that had been throwing snowballs, oyster shells, and clubs at the soldiers, with shouts of "fire," before any shot was discharged. A modern historian using both kinds of source would conclude that the colonists were not as peaceful as Revere depicted, that the soldiers were not as coolly murderous as Revere depicted, that the truth was something more complicated involving an inflamed crowd, panicked soldiers, ambiguous orders, and a propaganda apparatus that immediately turned the event into political mobilisation. None of the primary sources is "wrong"; each is partial. The historian's job is to triangulate.

  • Treating a single source as conclusive — always corroborate.
  • Confusing the source's claim about reality with the historian's claim — the historian uses sources, not just quotes them.
  • Ignoring what is not in the source (silenced voices, blanked-out passages, absent contexts).
  • Treating primary as always more reliable than secondary — a contemporary partisan account may be less accurate than a careful later study.
  • Underestimating the labour required: serious primary-source analysis is time-consuming; budget for it.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.3 — assess the credibility of sources relevant to their investigations (e.g., evaluate authors' purpose, perspective, expertise).

⚖️ 6.3 Bias, Corroboration, and Historical Argument
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

"Bias" is one of the most-misunderstood concepts in high-school history. Students sometimes write that a source is "biased" as if that disqualified it; in fact, every source is biased — has a perspective, an audience, a purpose, a position — and that bias is part of what makes it informative. The historian's job is not to find an "unbiased" source (there is none) but to identify the bias, account for it, and triangulate with other sources whose biases pull in different directions. A pamphlet by Thomas Paine and a memoir by Lord North are both biased about the American Revolution; reading them against each other and against neutral records (port-of-Boston customs returns, voter lists, casualty counts) produces a more defensible picture than reading either alone.

Corroboration is the discipline of confirming a claim by checking it against independent sources. A claim becomes more credible when multiple sources, with different biases and origins, converge on it. The Holocaust is the most-corroborated event in modern history precisely because Allied military records, German bureaucratic records, perpetrator testimony, survivor testimony, archaeological evidence, photographs, films, demographic statistics, and post-war legal proceedings all converge. Holocaust denial requires dismissing all of these — a tell that denial is not historical scepticism but ideological commitment.

Several common biases recur and have technical names. Confirmation bias: looking for evidence that supports what you already believe and discounting evidence that does not — a researcher problem as much as a source problem. Survivorship bias: only studying the records that survived (the famous example is statistician Abraham Wald's WW2 analysis of returning bombers, where the missing damage spots indicated where lost bombers had been hit). Selection bias: when the sample studied is not representative of the broader population — most early-modern letters that survive are from elites. Hindsight bias: knowing what happened next leads us to read prior events as more inevitable than they were. Eurocentric bias: framing world history as "the rise of the West," which we discussed in Chapters 1 and 2.

The historian's argument is the synthesis the work produces. A strong historical argument has three features. (1) A defensible thesis: a claim that someone could disagree with, formulated specifically (not "the French Revolution was important" but "the radical phase of the French Revolution between 1792 and 1794 produced the political model of mass mobilisation that has shaped modern revolutionary movements from Russia 1917 to Iran 1979"). (2) Evidence that supports the thesis: primary-source quotation, statistical data, demographic patterns, narrative cause-and-effect. (3) Engagement with counter-evidence: acknowledging what does not fit, refuting or qualifying it, conceding what should be conceded. A thesis that ignores counter-evidence is propaganda; a thesis that acknowledges and addresses counter-evidence is scholarship.

Historiographical schools — the major interpretive traditions historians have developed — provide context for any thesis. The Marxist tradition (Eric Hobsbawm, Christopher Hill) emphasises class struggle and economic structures. The Annales school (Lucien Febvre, Marc Bloch, Fernand Braudel) emphasises long-duration ("longue durée") structures of geography, climate, and demography. Social history (E. P. Thompson, Eugene Genovese) focuses on the experience of ordinary people. Cultural and intellectual history (Robert Darnton, Lynn Hunt) traces ideas and mentalities. Postcolonial history (Edward Said, Dipesh Chakrabarty) interrogates Eurocentric assumptions. Microhistory (Carlo Ginzburg) zooms in on a single case to illuminate broader patterns. A Grade 12 essay does not need to master all of these, but should be aware that historical argument exists within these traditions and that contemporary historiography is plural, not monolithic.

  • Bias — perspective, purpose, and position of a source or historian; ubiquitous and informative, not disqualifying.
  • Corroboration — confirmation of a claim by multiple independent sources.
  • Confirmation bias — tendency to seek evidence supporting prior beliefs and dismiss disconfirming evidence.
  • Survivorship / selection bias — non-representativeness of surviving or chosen evidence.
  • Hindsight bias — overestimating the inevitability of past events because we know how they ended.
  • Historiography — the study of how history has been written; major schools and debates.
  • Counter-evidence — evidence that contradicts a thesis; engaging with it is the mark of scholarship.

Worked Example — Building a Historical Argument

Thesis: "The Cold War in Europe ended in 1989 not because the West 'won' but because the Soviet leadership under Gorbachev chose to allow it to end."

Evidence to support: Gorbachev's decisions — not to intervene against Polish Solidarity (1988-89), not to send tanks to East Germany (October 1989), to accept German reunification (1990) — were the proximate causes of communist collapse in Eastern Europe; Soviet economic weakness alone did not require these political choices (Khrushchev and Brezhnev had managed similar weakness with repression); declassified documents (the National Security Archive's Cold War International History Project, the Hoover Institution Gorbachev collection) show Gorbachev rejecting the use of force consistently. Counter-evidence: Western military and economic pressure (the Reagan defence build-up, sanctions, Strategic Defence Initiative) constrained Soviet options; Eastern European movements had their own agency (Solidarity, Charter 77, environmental movements in East Germany); Polish, Hungarian, and Czechoslovak leaders made their own decisions. Synthesis: Gorbachev's choices were necessary but not sufficient; without Western pressure and Eastern European agency, the outcome would have been different. The thesis stands, in revised form: "the Cold War ended through a combination of Soviet leadership choice, Eastern European agency, and Western pressure — but Gorbachev's restraint was the decisive permissive factor without which the others would not have produced peaceful change." This more careful, evidence-anchored argument is what a strong Grade 12 essay does.

  • Using "biased" as a disqualifier — instead use "biased in the following direction, which we account for as follows."
  • Confusing absence of evidence with evidence of absence — many things happened that left no record.
  • Reasoning from a single source — always corroborate; one document is never a thesis.
  • Ignoring counter-evidence — strong essays engage and refute it; weak essays pretend it does not exist.
  • Treating historiography as background — it is foreground; every claim is in conversation with prior interpretations.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.4 — interpret and analyse evidence and information relevant to their investigations using various tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for historical inquiry.

🗓️ 6.4 Periodisation and the Shape of the Past
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Periodisation is the division of the past into intervals — eras, periods, ages. It looks innocent. It is not. The choices a historian makes about where one period ends and another begins shape the questions she can ask and the conclusions she can reach. CHY4U takes "the fifteenth century" as a starting point; that choice is conventional but defensible (printing press, voyages of discovery, Renaissance) and not the only possible one. World-historian Patrick Manning has argued for a starting date of 1300 to capture the Black Death's reshaping of Eurasia; the Marxist tradition often begins with the medieval-to-modern transition (the "long 16th century" of Immanuel Wallerstein); Indigenous historians of the Americas note that 1492 is meaningful in the colonising story but not in the Indigenous millennium that preceded it.

Several periodisations structure how the CHY4U curriculum is organised. The "early modern" period (c.1450–c.1750) is bounded by the printing press and the Industrial Revolution. The "long 19th century" (1789–1914, Eric Hobsbawm's coinage) extends from the French Revolution to the outbreak of the First World War. The "short 20th century" (1914–1991, also Hobsbawm) runs from the First World War to the collapse of the USSR. These boundaries are not arbitrary, but they are also not fixed by nature; they are historiographical choices. Other defensible periodisations (e.g., Christian Smith's "long" 20th century from 1870 to 2008) would tell different stories.

Three pitfalls of periodisation. (1) Eurocentrism: dividing world history by European events (the "fall of Rome," the "Reformation," the "French Revolution") presumes European events are the structuring ones, which marginalises Asia, Africa, and the Americas. The standard remedy is to use multiple periodisation schemes in parallel — one for European political history, one for Atlantic world history, one for the Indian Ocean world, one for East Asia. (2) Reifying period names: "the Renaissance," "the Enlightenment," "the Industrial Revolution," "modernity" are historians' constructs, not entities that existed in the heads of people at the time. No 15th-century Florentine called herself a Renaissance person. (3) Sharp boundaries: real change is gradual, contested, geographically uneven; a 1789 boundary makes a tidy story but obscures the fact that French rural life looked similar in 1810 to 1750.

The same problem applies to geographical periodisation — what counts as a "region." Western Europe, the Atlantic World, the Mediterranean, the Indian Ocean, "the West," "the Global South" — each is a unit that includes some places and excludes others, and the choice matters. Fernand Braudel's The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949) made the Mediterranean a unit; world historians like Janet Abu-Lughod have made the medieval Eurasian trade network a unit; Atlantic-world historians make the Atlantic basin one. Each choice illuminates some questions and shadows others.

For the research essay (topic 6.6), good periodisation discipline means: (1) name your chronological boundaries explicitly ("This essay covers the period 1789–1815"); (2) justify them ("These dates correspond to the outbreak of the French Revolution and the end of the Napoleonic Wars at Waterloo"); (3) acknowledge their limits ("Some processes — the long aftermath of the Code Civil, the colonial repercussions in Latin America — extend beyond this period"); (4) align your evidence with your boundaries — sources from outside your chosen range should be flagged as such; (5) reflect on your geographical choices similarly. This is not academic pedantry — vague boundaries produce vague arguments, and clear boundaries produce assessable ones.

  • Periodisation — division of the past into intervals; shapes the questions and conclusions of historical inquiry.
  • Early modern — period roughly 1450–1750, marked by global expansion, religious reformation, and scientific revolution.
  • Long 19th century — Hobsbawm's term for 1789–1914.
  • Short 20th century — Hobsbawm's term for 1914–1991.
  • Longue durée — Braudel's term for long-duration structural patterns (geography, climate, demography) that move slowly.
  • Anthropocene — proposed geological epoch in which human activity dominates Earth-system change; emerging periodisation.
  • Eurocentrism — habit of treating European events and timelines as the structuring frame of world history.

Worked Example — Defending a Periodisation Choice

Suppose you are writing a research essay on women's economic activity from 1500 to 1900. What periodisation should structure it?

A first-pass periodisation might use European political markers: 1500–1648 (Wars of Religion), 1648–1789 (early modern), 1789–1815 (Napoleonic), 1815–1900 (industrial). But women's economic activity does not necessarily fit these periods. A more sensitive periodisation might use: (1) c.1500–c.1700, when household-based production gave women (especially widows) substantial economic roles in guilds, brewing, and small commerce; (2) c.1700–c.1830, when the separation of home and work in industrialising regions reshaped women's labour; (3) c.1830–c.1880, when factory and domestic-service labour expanded in industrial economies; (4) c.1880–c.1914, when the "New Woman," professional careers, and the suffrage movement emerged. This periodisation is grounded in the historical phenomenon being studied, not in unrelated political events. Acknowledging the choice and defending it in your introduction is the discipline. The Strand A skill at work: choosing scales (chronological and geographical) that fit the question, and explaining the choice.

  • Treating period names as self-evident — "the Reformation" is a historiographical construct, not a thing in the world.
  • Using one periodisation for all questions — different topics need different boundaries.
  • Reifying "the West" without specifying which parts and timeframes — it covers very different societies.
  • Ignoring the costs of any periodisation choice — selecting boundaries excludes some questions while enabling others.
  • Treating "everything since 1900" as one period — the 20th century includes multiple distinct phases.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.6 — evaluate evidence and draw conclusions about issues, events, and/or developments in world history since the fifteenth century.

🤔 6.5 Continuity, Change, and Causation in Historical Argument
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Two of the most powerful historical-thinking concepts — continuity and change and cause and consequence — show up in nearly every exam question and essay prompt. Strong students understand them as analytical tools, not vocabulary words. Weak students see "continuity and change" as a checkbox to tick. This topic concentrates on how to deploy both concepts in extended argument.

Continuity and change is not a binary. Real historical reality is layered: some things change rapidly while others stay similar, and the historian's job is to disaggregate. Consider Britain across the 19th century. Substantial change: the franchise widened from about 2 percent of adult men in 1832 to about 60 percent in 1885; the population urbanised from about 30 percent to about 75 percent; industrial output multiplied roughly 15-fold; education became compulsory and free; the legal status of married women shifted dramatically (Married Women's Property Acts 1870 and 1882). Significant continuity: the monarchy survived; the empire expanded; the House of Lords retained veto power; women's suffrage was not won until after 1918; gender pay gaps and class inequality endured; the established Church of England remained. A nuanced answer holds both threads in tension and is specific about what changed and what persisted.

A useful classroom tool is the "change-continuity matrix" — a four-cell grid that helps locate phenomena: rapidly changing / institutionally continuous (e.g., 19th-century industrial technology within a continuous British monarchy); slowly changing / institutionally discontinuous (e.g., gradual changes in women's rights through abrupt legislative moments); and so on. The matrix forces the historian to specify the timescale and the institutional location of the change being claimed.

Causation has its own architecture. A historian distinguishes necessary causes (without which the event would not have happened) from sufficient causes (whose presence is enough to produce the event). For the French Revolution, necessary causes include the fiscal crisis, the Enlightenment vocabulary, and the calling of the Estates-General; no single one is sufficient. The historian also distinguishes long-term, medium-term, short-term, and triggering causes. For the First World War: long-term militarism and alliances; medium-term Balkans instability; short-term July Crisis decisions; triggering assassination at Sarajevo. Layering causes this way is a clear-thinking discipline that produces stronger essays than vague "many factors" answers.

Two cognitive biases distort causal reasoning. (1) Hindsight bias: once we know an event happened, we exaggerate its inevitability. The German conquest of France in May–June 1940 looks inevitable in retrospect, but contemporary French commanders, German generals, and most outside observers did not expect that outcome; the campaign turned on contingent decisions about armoured concentration and timing. (2) Single-factor explanations: students are tempted to identify "the cause" of the Reformation (Luther's theology) or "the cause" of the Cold War (Stalin's expansion). Real causation is multi-factor and converging. A defensible essay specifies which factor it foregrounds and why, while acknowledging others.

One more analytical move: counterfactual reasoning, the disciplined "what if?" question. What if Lenin had not arrived in Petrograd in April 1917? What if Hitler had been killed in the Beer Hall Putsch in 1923? What if the Black Death had not reached Europe? Niall Ferguson, Richard Evans, and others have debated whether counterfactuals are legitimate historical analysis or misleading speculation. The defensible position is that counterfactuals are useful as a way of testing causal claims: if you believe X caused Y, then asking "what if X had not happened?" is a clarifying exercise. But the counterfactual world is unknowable in detail, and confident assertions about it are speculation, not history.

  • Continuity and change — recognising both what persists and what transforms across periods; not a binary.
  • Necessary / sufficient cause — necessary: without it, no event; sufficient: enough on its own to produce the event.
  • Multi-causal explanation — most historical events have multiple converging causes.
  • Hindsight bias — exaggerating the inevitability of past events because we know how they ended.
  • Contingency — the openness of historical outcomes to small decisions; the opposite of determinism.
  • Counterfactual — a "what if?" exercise used to test causal claims (used cautiously).
  • Path dependence — the tendency for earlier choices to constrain later options (e.g., QWERTY keyboard, US two-party system).

Worked Example — Both Continuity and Change in One Paragraph

Topic: Indigenous-settler relations in Canada 1763–2025.

A strong paragraph: "Between the Royal Proclamation of 1763 — which recognised Indigenous title to unceded lands west of the Appalachians — and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (S.C. 2021, c. 14), the legal status of Indigenous peoples in Canada underwent dramatic change. The Indian Act of 1876, residential schools (1831–1996), the pass system, and the Sixties Scoop made the 19th and 20th centuries a period of intensified settler-colonial control. The political renaissance of Indigenous movements — the 1969 White Paper resistance, James Bay 1975, R v. Sparrow 1990, the 1996 Royal Commission, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission 2008–2015, UNDRIP 2021 — represents change toward recognition. But significant continuities persist: 96 percent of Indigenous reserve lands remain unceded by treaty in British Columbia; the 1876 Indian Act remains in force; the funding gap for on-reserve services persists; missing and murdered Indigenous women remain a national crisis. Any honest account of Indigenous-settler relations across this period must hold the trajectory of legal change and the persistence of structural inequality together; treating either alone produces a misleading picture." This kind of integrated handling of continuity, change, and causation across a long period is what Strand A's expectations specifically reward.

  • Treating "continuity and change" as a checkbox — strong essays integrate both threads in every paragraph.
  • Claiming a single "cause" of a major event — most have multiple converging causes.
  • Inferring necessity from sequence — that A preceded B does not prove A caused B (correlation vs causation).
  • Overusing counterfactuals — they are sharpening exercises, not standalone arguments.
  • Ignoring contingency — historical outcomes were not predetermined; people made choices.

📝 6.6 The Research Essay
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The CHY4U Research Essay is the course's major piece of independent historical inquiry — typically 1500–2500 words, on a student-chosen topic within world history since 1450, with at least 6–10 sources (a balance of primary and secondary) and proper citation (the course will use either MLA 9 or Chicago Notes-Bibliography). The essay is worth 15 percent of the course (the Final Exam is the other 15 percent of the 30-percent Final Evaluation). Strong essays are not just longer versions of in-class paragraphs; they require a sustained argument, structural sophistication, and engagement with secondary scholarship that an in-class paragraph cannot.

The research process, well-managed, breaks down into stages. (1) Topic selection: choose something narrow enough to handle in 2,000 words but rich enough to require argument. "The Reformation" is too broad; "Luther's use of the printing press in the propaganda campaign of 1517-1522" is workable. (2) Initial reading: a survey textbook chapter and a Britannica entry to map the terrain; two scholarly articles to identify debates. (3) Question formulation: turn the topic into a question the essay will answer. Open questions ("In what ways did Luther's pamphlets exploit print technology?") work better than yes/no questions. (4) Primary-source identification: which contemporary documents (Luther's pamphlets, the Edict of Worms, correspondence between Frederick the Wise and the Pope) bear on the question? (5) Secondary-source survey: which scholars have written on this topic, and what are their interpretations? (6) Working thesis: a preliminary answer to your question, which the rest of the research and writing will test. (7) Outline: structure the argument before drafting. (8) Drafting: write the body before the introduction; introductions improve when you know what you have actually argued. (9) Revision: read the draft as if it were not yours; cut what is irrelevant; sharpen what is unclear. (10) Citation and presentation: footnotes/in-text citations, Works Cited, formatting.

Thesis statements for history essays do three things: identify the subject; make a defensible claim; signal the structure of the argument that follows. Weak thesis: "The French Revolution was important and changed many things." Stronger thesis: "Between 1789 and 1799, the French Revolution produced not one revolution but four — a constitutional moment (1789-1791), a republican turn (1792-1793), a radical terror (1793-1794), and a conservative consolidation (1794-1799) — and each phase resolved problems left by the previous one." The second thesis is debatable, specific, and tells the reader what the essay will demonstrate.

Paragraph structure for a research essay follows the basic PEEL or TEXAS form discussed in topic 6.5 but on a larger scale. Each body paragraph: a topic sentence asserting one piece of the larger argument; 2-4 sentences of historical evidence (with specific dates, names, and direct quotations from primary sources where appropriate); 2-3 sentences of analysis explaining how the evidence supports the topic sentence; a transition or link sentence connecting to the next paragraph. Avoid the "evidence dump" paragraph — a list of facts without analysis is not a paragraph.

Citation is non-negotiable. Failure to credit sources is plagiarism — academically and ethically serious. The two systems most common in history at the university level are Chicago Notes-Bibliography (footnotes/endnotes plus a bibliography; preferred in most history journals) and MLA 9 (in-text parenthetical citations plus Works Cited; common at high school). Your teacher will specify. Both systems require: full citation for each work used; consistent format; quotation marks around any directly quoted material; clear indication of paraphrase. Use Zotero or another citation manager if you have access; even a simple spreadsheet tracking sources, page numbers, and notes will save you from late-night attribution panic. Note that artificial-intelligence assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) are not citable scholarly sources and are notorious for inventing citations — never rely on them for source-listing; verify every reference independently. AI use is also typically restricted by Ontario boards and your teacher's syllabus; check before using.

The research essay is the course's culmination not because of its length but because it requires all the historical-thinking concepts at once: significance (choosing a worthy topic), causation (explaining why things happened), continuity and change (situating in time), perspective (taking historical actors seriously), ethics (where relevant), and evidence (using sources well). Done well, the essay is an example of what historical inquiry can produce. Done poorly, it is a missed opportunity for the most important skill the course teaches — independent, rigorous engagement with the past.

  • Research essay — extended, source-based argument on a chosen topic; typically 1500–2500 words.
  • Thesis statement — single sentence stating the essay's main argument; debatable and specific.
  • Primary vs secondary sources — period documents vs later interpretations; the essay should use both.
  • Citation style — Chicago Notes-Bibliography or MLA 9 (check with your teacher).
  • Plagiarism — passing off another's words or ideas as one's own; serious academic and ethical offence.
  • Footnote / endnote — citation positioned at bottom of page (footnote) or end of essay (endnote).
  • Bibliography / Works Cited — alphabetised list of sources used, at the end of the essay.
  • Counter-argument — a strong essay engages with the strongest objection to its thesis, refutes or qualifies, and returns to the argument.

Worked Example — Outlining a Research Essay

Topic: "How did the printing press contribute to the Protestant Reformation?"

Working thesis: "The printing press was a necessary but not sufficient cause of the Protestant Reformation; without Gutenberg's technology, Luther's theology would have remained a local academic dispute, but without Luther's specific theology, the printing press would not by itself have produced the religious revolution of the 16th century."

Outline:

  • I. Introduction: state thesis; preview structure; introduce sources.
  • II. The technological context: Gutenberg's press c.1450; the scale of European book production by 1500 (the Incunabula); economic feasibility.
  • III. Luther's specific media strategy: his vernacular pamphlets, especially To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation (1520) — printed in 4,000 copies in 18 days, exemplifying pamphlet-warfare.
  • IV. Counter-evidence to printing-alone argument: Jan Hus (1372–1415) had similar theology and was burned without successfully launching a Reformation; printing alone is not sufficient.
  • V. Counter-evidence to Luther-alone argument: Luther's earlier works in Latin manuscripts had circulated only in academic circles; the press created the scale required.
  • VI. The Catholic response and its print-strategy (Council of Trent's role; the Index of Prohibited Books 1559).
  • VII. Conclusion: thesis confirmed; multi-factor causation; broader implications for media history (parallel to 21st-century discussions of social media and political change).

This kind of outline is the architectural foundation of a strong research essay. Note that it engages both directions of counter-evidence, makes the thesis a tested claim rather than a flat assertion, and ends with a broader implication that connects historical analysis to contemporary discussion — a standard hallmark of Grade 12 historical writing.

  • Starting to write before you have a working thesis — you will rewrite the introduction four times.
  • Citing only secondary sources (or only primary) — most strong essays use both.
  • Plagiarism, including unintentional plagiarism through poor note-taking — always record the source of every paraphrase.
  • Using AI to generate citations — they are routinely fabricated; always verify.
  • Trying to cover too much — narrow your topic until you can support every claim with specific evidence.
