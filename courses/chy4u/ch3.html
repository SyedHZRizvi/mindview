🇫🇷 3.1 The French Revolution (1789–1799)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The French Revolution is the political event from which the modern Western political vocabulary descends. Left and right (from where deputies sat in the National Assembly), citizen, constitution, terror, dictatorship, declaration of rights — all entered widespread use during the years 1789–1799. The Revolution destroyed a thousand-year-old monarchy, executed its king and queen, fought off coalitions of European powers, abolished slavery (then partly restored it), proclaimed the universal rights of man, briefly attempted to remake the calendar and the decimal system, and ended in the military dictatorship of Napoleon Bonaparte. It is the case study in how social pressure, fiscal failure, and political theory can converge in revolutionary transformation.

The fiscal crisis of the French monarchy was the immediate trigger. After supporting the American Revolution (a war that France could not afford), the French treasury defaulted in 1788. Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General — the medieval representative body, not called since 1614 — to authorise new taxes. It met in May 1789. The Third Estate (commoners) refused to accept voting by order (which would give the privileged First and Second Estates joint veto) and on 17 June declared themselves the National Assembly; three days later, locked out of their meeting hall, they took the Tennis Court Oath not to dissolve until a constitution was written. The storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 — a fortress that held seven prisoners but was the symbol of royal arbitrary power — marked the moment Parisian crowds entered politics. In August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges in a single night session and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights."

The constitutional monarchy of 1789–1791 failed because Louis XVI never accepted it. He attempted to flee France in June 1791 (the Flight to Varennes), was caught, and brought back as a prisoner-king. War broke out in April 1792 between France and Austria/Prussia, who wanted to restore the monarchy. Defeats brought down the constitutional regime; in August 1792 the Paris crowd overthrew the king, and in September a new convention proclaimed France a republic. Louis XVI was tried for treason and guillotined in January 1793; Marie Antoinette followed in October.

The Reign of Terror (June 1793 – July 1794) was the Revolution's radical phase under the Committee of Public Safety dominated by Maximilien Robespierre. Surrounded by foreign war, internal counter-revolution (the Vendée), and economic crisis, the revolutionary government adopted emergency measures: price controls (the Maximum), mass conscription (the levée en masse of August 1793, the first modern citizen-army), revolutionary tribunals, and de-Christianisation. Perhaps 17,000 were officially executed; another 25,000 died in the Vendée and other internal conflicts. Robespierre's overthrow (9 Thermidor, 27 July 1794) and execution ended the Terror but began a more conservative phase (the Thermidorian Reaction, the Directory).

The Revolution's legacy is multi-vocal. It produced the language of universal rights, citizenship, and popular sovereignty that has shaped modern democracy. It also produced the model of revolutionary terror, the levée-en-masse citizen-army that would feed 19th-century total wars, and the conviction (developed by Sieyès and others) that political legitimacy requires a written constitution. The legal codification of 1804 — Napoleon's Civil Code — spread Revolutionary legal principles (legal equality, private property, secular marriage and divorce) across Europe. The Revolution failed at many of its aims: women never received the rights Olympe de Gouges had demanded in her Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791, for which she was guillotined in 1793); colonial slavery was abolished in 1794 only to be restored by Napoleon in 1802. But the precedent — that a sovereign people may remake its own political order — could not be unmade.

  • Estates-General — French representative body of three estates (clergy, nobility, commoners), called by Louis XVI in 1789.
  • National Assembly / National Convention — 1789 / 1792 revolutionary legislatures.
  • Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) — foundational statement of revolutionary principles.
  • Sans-culottes — urban workers and shopkeepers, the radical popular base of the Revolution.
  • Reign of Terror (1793–1794) — emergency rule by the Committee of Public Safety; mass executions.
  • Jacobin / Girondin — radical / moderate factions of the revolutionary period.
  • Olympe de Gouges — wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791); guillotined 1793.

Worked Example — Causes of the French Revolution

A document-based question asks students to evaluate multiple causes.

A strong response distinguishes long-term and short-term causes. Long-term: Enlightenment political theory (Locke, Rousseau, Montesquieu); inequalities of the three estates (clergy and nobility paid almost no tax); the example of the American Revolution. Medium-term: fiscal exhaustion from the American war (1778–1783) and bad harvests in 1788; ministerial paralysis under Louis XVI; the calling of the Estates-General. Short-term/triggers: the Estates-General's procedural deadlock in May 1789; the dismissal of the popular finance minister Necker on 11 July; the storming of the Bastille on 14 July. Notice that none of these alone caused the Revolution — Enlightenment ideas had circulated for fifty years without revolution; fiscal crises had happened before. What was new was the convergence: an ideologically prepared bourgeoisie, an exhausted treasury, popular hunger, and an indecisive monarch. This is exactly the multi-causal historical-thinking move Strand A asks for.

  • Calling the Revolution "the French Revolution of 1789" — the period 1789–1799 contains several distinct revolutions, plus Napoleon.
  • Treating the Bastille's storming as freeing thousands of prisoners — there were seven.
  • Equating the Terror with the entire Revolution — the radical phase ran one year out of ten.
  • Forgetting Olympe de Gouges, Théroigne de Méricourt, and the Society of Revolutionary Republican Women — women were active in revolutionary politics.
  • Confusing the National Assembly (1789–1791), Legislative Assembly (1791–1792), and National Convention (1792–1795) — they are different bodies.

🌴 3.2 The Haitian Revolution and Napoleon
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

While the French Revolution dominates textbook attention, the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) is its most radical extension and the single most important revolt in the history of slavery. In what was then the French colony of Saint-Domingue — the wealthiest plantation colony in the world, producing 40 percent of global sugar and 60 percent of global coffee on the labour of half a million enslaved Africans — a slave uprising that began in August 1791 succeeded after 13 years of war against French, Spanish, and British forces in producing the only successful slave revolt to create an independent state: Haiti, proclaimed by Jean-Jacques Dessalines on 1 January 1804.

The uprising began in the northern plain on the night of 22–23 August 1791 with a ritual at Bois-Caïman led by the houngan Dutty Boukman. Within weeks, plantations across the north were burning. The early years saw a complex four-cornered war involving enslaved insurgents, free people of colour (gens de couleur libres, many of them prosperous), white planters, and Spanish and British invaders. Toussaint Louverture, a literate former slave and brilliant strategist, emerged as the most important leader by 1793; his army defeated invading British forces (1798), defeated his Spanish-allied rivals, and ruled the colony with effective independence by 1801. The National Convention had already abolished slavery in all French colonies in February 1794 — the first European abolition.

Napoleon Bonaparte, who had seized power in France in the coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799) and become First Consul, decided to reverse abolition. In 1802 he sent a 30,000-strong expedition under his brother-in-law General Leclerc to retake Saint-Domingue and reimpose slavery. Toussaint was tricked into a meeting, kidnapped, and shipped to France, where he died in a Jura prison in April 1803. But Leclerc and his successor Rochambeau lost two-thirds of their men to yellow fever and to the relentless guerrilla warfare led by Dessalines and Henri Christophe. On 18 November 1803 the French were defeated at the Battle of Vertières. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines proclaimed the independent state of Haiti — the name a deliberate restoration of the Taíno Indigenous name for the island.

The Haitian Revolution's consequences were global. The financial cost convinced Napoleon to sell the Louisiana Territory to the United States in 1803, doubling US territory. The example terrified slaveholders across the Americas; the US, France, and other powers refused to recognise Haiti for decades, and an indemnity France extorted from Haiti (1825) crippled the new state's finances into the 20th century. But for enslaved peoples across the Atlantic world, the Haitian Revolution proved revolution was possible. Frederick Douglass, decades later, called Haiti "the original pioneer emancipator of the nineteenth century."

Napoleon himself, meanwhile, transformed Europe. Crowned Emperor in 1804, he conquered most of continental Europe by 1810; the Napoleonic Wars cost perhaps 3.5 to 6 million lives. His Civil Code (1804) codified Revolutionary legal principles — legal equality of citizens, secularism, civil marriage, divorce, property rights — across most of the territories he ruled. The Code outlasted the empire and shaped French, Belgian, Dutch, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Latin American, and Louisiana legal systems. His invasion of Russia in 1812 was the disaster from which his empire never recovered; defeated at Leipzig (1813) and Waterloo (1815), Napoleon ended his life on the British prison-island of Saint Helena, where he died in 1821.

  • Saint-Domingue / Haiti — French Caribbean plantation colony / first independent post-colonial Black state (1804).
  • Toussaint Louverture — formerly enslaved, military and political leader of the Haitian Revolution.
  • Jean-Jacques Dessalines — proclaimed Haitian independence 1 January 1804.
  • Civil Code (1804) — Napoleon's codification of French civil law; legal equality, secular marriage, property rights.
  • Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815) — European conflicts triggered by French expansion.
  • Continental System — Napoleon's attempt to economically isolate Britain by closing European ports to British trade.
  • Haitian indemnity (1825) — debt France imposed on Haiti, equivalent to roughly 21 billion modern dollars; paid until 1947.

Worked Example — Why Did the Haitian Revolution Succeed?

This is one of the great causal puzzles of modern history: across the Americas, hundreds of slave revolts failed; one succeeded. Why?

Multiple causes converged. (1) Demography: Saint-Domingue's enslaved population vastly outnumbered the white population (around 17:1), unlike the US (where it was roughly 1:3). (2) Leadership: Toussaint's literacy, military skill, and political vision were exceptional. (3) Disease: yellow fever and malaria killed European troops at devastating rates — perhaps 50,000 French soldiers died in 1802–1803. (4) Timing: the French Revolution's abolition decree (1794) gave the revolt legal cover; Napoleon's preoccupation with European wars limited his Caribbean reinforcements; the Anglo-French rivalry created openings. (5) Solidarity: the uprising mobilised both formerly enslaved and free people of colour, with religious traditions (Vodou) providing organisational networks. None of these alone is sufficient; together, they produced an outcome no other slave revolt achieved. This kind of multi-factor causal explanation is what Strand A's "evaluate evidence and draw conclusions" expectation rewards.

  • Treating the Haitian Revolution as a footnote to the French — Haitians fought eight years for an independence the French Republic never granted them.
  • Underestimating the casualties — the Saint-Domingue wars killed perhaps 150,000 of the colony's 500,000 enslaved population.
  • Ignoring the indemnity of 1825 — it is essential to understanding Haiti's subsequent poverty.
  • Calling Napoleon merely a tyrant — the Civil Code did spread genuine legal reforms; he also reimposed slavery, attacked free Black communities, and conscripted millions into his wars.
  • Forgetting that Haiti was a slave colony of the Spanish before the French — the 1697 Treaty of Ryswick transferred the western third of Hispaniola to France.

⚙️ 3.3 The Industrial Revolution
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Industrial Revolution is the transformation in production, transport, energy, and social organisation that began in 18th-century Britain and spread, unevenly, across the world over the next two centuries. It is the largest change in human material life since the agricultural revolution 10,000 years earlier. Before 1800, output per worker had grown only slowly, by a few tenths of a percent per year; after 1800, in industrialising economies, it grew at rates that doubled output every generation. The compound effects — for population, life expectancy, climate, inequality — are still working themselves out.

Why Britain, and why then? Historians have argued at length. Likely factors: abundant coal in convenient locations near rivers and population centres; high wages that incentivised labour-saving machinery (Robert Allen's argument); a constitutional regime since 1689 that protected property and patents; integrated capital markets (the Bank of England 1694, the London Stock Exchange); empire and slavery generating commercial demand and capital; agricultural improvement (the Norfolk four-course rotation, enclosure of common lands) that fed expanding cities; and dense networks of craftsmen and "tinkerers" who incrementally improved spinning, weaving, and steam-engine designs. None of these alone explains industrialisation; together they made Britain the first industrial economy.

The key technologies arrived in stages. James Watt's improved steam engine (patented 1769, commercialised from the 1770s) made coal-driven power portable and efficient — first for pumping mines, then for spinning factories, then for transport. Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) mechanised cotton-thread production. The power loom (Edmund Cartwright, 1785) mechanised weaving. By the 1820s and 1830s, steam railways (the Stockton and Darlington 1825, the Liverpool and Manchester 1830) and steamships (Brunel's SS Great Western, 1838) connected the new industrial cities to ports and markets. Manchester's population grew from 25,000 in 1770 to 350,000 in 1850; Britain became urban by mid-century (the first society in human history to be so).

The human cost was high and unevenly distributed. Working hours in early factories were 12–16 per day, including for children as young as 6, who could fit under spinning frames. Friedrich Engels, observing Manchester in 1842, described the workers' districts in his The Condition of the Working Class in England: streets unpaved, sewers absent, infant mortality 35 percent, life expectancy in the worst districts around 17 years. The textile workers' protest movements — the Luddites (1811–1816, who smashed power-looms not from technophobia but from anger at wage cuts), the Chartists (1838–1848, who demanded universal male suffrage), the early trade unions — emerged in response. British Parliament reformed slowly: the 1832 Reform Act expanded the male franchise; the Factory Act of 1833 limited child labour and required half-time schooling; the Mines Act of 1842 banned women and children from underground work.

Industrialisation spread. Belgium and parts of Germany and France industrialised in the 1830s–1860s; the United States after the Civil War; Russia from the 1890s; Japan after the Meiji Restoration of 1868. The global consequences were profound. The Great Divergence — the term Kenneth Pomeranz coined for the gap that opened between industrialising Europe/North America and the rest of the world after 1800 — was the basis of European imperial dominance examined in topic 3.6. The carbon-fuelled industrial economy also began the long process of global warming whose consequences fall on our 21st century.

  • Industrial Revolution — transformation of production, energy, and society beginning in 18th-century Britain.
  • Steam engine — Watt's 1769 patent of a separate-condenser steam engine, drastically more fuel-efficient.
  • Factory system — concentration of mechanised production under one roof, with wage-labour discipline.
  • Enclosure — legal conversion of English common land into privately owned fenced fields; cleared peasants for factory labour.
  • Luddites — early-19th-century English textile workers who attacked machinery in protest of wage cuts.
  • Chartism — 1838–1848 British movement for universal male suffrage and political reform.
  • Great Divergence — historian Kenneth Pomeranz's term for the post-1800 economic gap between Europe and Asia.

Worked Example — Continuity and Change in Industrial Life

What changed and what stayed the same for a typical English worker between 1780 and 1850?

Changes: location (rural village → urban factory town); work pattern (seasonal agriculture → 6-day, 12-hour factory shifts); employer (often a family farm → an impersonal factory owner); diet (more sugar, tea, and bread; less variety); life expectancy (declined in urban areas in the 1830s–1840s before improving). Continuities: poverty (the wage-share of national income did not improve until after 1850); patriarchal family structure (women worked, but their wages were typically half a man's and legally controlled by husbands); religion (the Methodist revival was a 19th-century phenomenon, not a decline of religious practice); deference (the property qualification for voting still excluded most workers after 1832). A strong Grade 12 essay holds both threads — industrial life transformed material conditions, but several social structures (class, gender, deference) persisted for at least two more generations. This is the kind of continuity-and-change historical reasoning Strand A explicitly tests.

  • Calling the Industrial Revolution "the rise of capitalism" — capitalism existed for centuries before 1750 (commercial Florence, Dutch joint-stock companies).
  • Romanticising "factory progress" — wages stagnated for many workers until at least 1850.
  • Treating it as exclusively British — Belgian, French, and Northern US industrialisation began within a generation.
  • Forgetting women: the textile workforce was majority female in early Lancashire and Massachusetts mills.
  • Ignoring environmental consequences — the Great Smog, river pollution, and the start of CO₂ accumulation date to this period.

🏛️ 3.4 Nationalism, Reform, and the Revolutions of 1848
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The 1815 Congress of Vienna, dominated by Klemens von Metternich of Austria, attempted to restore the Europe of 1789 — legitimate monarchs, territorial balance, and suppression of revolutionary ideas. For thirty-three years the Concert of Europe largely succeeded at containing major war. But it could not suppress two forces unleashed by 1789: liberalism (constitutional government, civil liberties, free markets) and nationalism (the political claim that ethno-cultural nations should be sovereign states). The revolutions of 1848 — the "Springtime of Peoples" — exploded across Europe in February (France), March (German states, Austria, Hungary, Italy), and the following months. Most failed within a year. But the issues they raised — constitutional government, national unification, social reform — dominated European politics for the rest of the century.

1848's pattern was strikingly repeated. In each major city — Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Budapest — crowds and university students built barricades, royal armies were initially withdrawn, monarchs offered constitutions or abdicated, parliaments were convened, and within months the army returned, the king (or his successor) regained control, and constitutions were withdrawn or weakened. France produced the most lasting change: Louis-Philippe abdicated in February, the Second Republic was proclaimed, and (under universal male suffrage for the first time in European history) Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte was elected president in December — only to make himself Emperor Napoleon III in a coup in 1851. In the German states, the Frankfurt Parliament drafted a liberal constitution and offered the imperial crown of a united Germany to Frederick William IV of Prussia, who refused to "pick up a crown from the gutter." German national unification was postponed until Bismarck's "blood and iron" did it from above.

Italian unification (the Risorgimento, "resurgence") was achieved between 1859 and 1870 under the pragmatic leadership of Count Camillo di Cavour of Piedmont-Sardinia, the popular military genius Giuseppe Garibaldi (whose volunteer Red Shirts seized Sicily and Naples in 1860), and King Victor Emmanuel II. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861; Venetia was added after the 1866 war; Rome (held by the Pope under French protection) was finally taken in 1870 when French troops were withdrawn for the Franco-Prussian War.

German unification followed under the conservative leadership of Otto von Bismarck, Prussian Chancellor from 1862. Bismarck combined Prussian military reform (financed by overriding the Landtag), three "wars of unification" (against Denmark 1864, Austria 1866, France 1870), and skilful diplomacy. The Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871, ending with the siege of Paris and the capture of Napoleon III at Sedan, completed the process; on 18 January 1871 the German Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles — a deliberate humiliation of France. France's loss of Alsace-Lorraine in the peace would poison Franco-German relations for two generations and contribute to the outbreak of war in 1914.

Reform movements that operated within liberal parliamentary frameworks also remade 19th-century societies. The British 1832, 1867, and 1884 Reform Acts gradually expanded the male franchise; Catholic Emancipation (1829) and the abolition of slavery in the British Empire (1833) preceded them. Female suffrage took longer. The American Civil War (1861–1865), provoked by the South's defence of slavery against the federal government's containment of its expansion, killed about 750,000 — proportionally the deadliest war in US history — and ended slavery in the United States. Reconstruction (1865–1877) gave African-American men citizenship and the vote in principle; in practice, after federal troops withdrew, white-supremacist regimes in the South systematically disenfranchised Black voters for the next 90 years.

  • Congress of Vienna (1815) — settlement of post-Napoleonic Europe; restored legitimate monarchies, established Concert of Europe.
  • Nationalism — political claim that ethno-cultural nations should be sovereign states; civic and ethnic forms.
  • Liberalism — political ideology of constitutional government, civil rights, and (typically) free-market economics.
  • 1848 revolutions — Europe-wide wave of liberal and national uprisings, mostly defeated within a year.
  • Risorgimento — Italian unification, completed 1861–1870 under Cavour, Garibaldi, and Victor Emmanuel II.
  • Bismarck's "Blood and Iron" — 1862 speech defining his realpolitik strategy for German unification.
  • American Civil War (1861–1865) — ended slavery in the United States; the Reconstruction era 1865–1877.

Worked Example — Why Did 1848 Fail?

A standard exam question: "Account for the failure of the 1848 revolutions in continental Europe."

A strong response identifies converging causes: (1) Coalition breakdown — liberals and nationalists allied with workers in February-March, then split when workers demanded social reforms (the June Days in Paris saw 4,000 workers killed by the Republican army). (2) Conservative resistance — royal armies remained largely loyal; once monarchs recovered nerve, they used them. (3) Divided nationalisms — Czech, Hungarian, German, and Italian nationalists wanted overlapping territories; the Frankfurt Parliament's struggle to define "Germany" illustrated this. (4) Rural conservatism — peasants, satisfied by abolition of feudal obligations early in 1848, withdrew from urban radicalism. (5) International intervention — Russia helped Habsburg Austria crush the Hungarian rebellion in 1849. A Level 4 paper holds all five together and argues that the underlying issue was the absence of a stable cross-class alliance for constitutional reform. The lasting legacies — national consciousness, abolition of serfdom in the Habsburg lands, the lessons learned by Bismarck and others — were nonetheless real. This kind of complex assessment of "failure" is exactly what Strand A asks of Grade 12 historians.

  • Calling 1848 a "complete failure" — abolition of serfdom in Austria-Hungary, universal male suffrage in France, and lasting nationalist projects emerged from it.
  • Treating German and Italian unification as inevitable — neither was; both took skilful (and bloody) leadership.
  • Forgetting that liberalism and nationalism were not always allied — by 1900 nationalism was often illiberal.
  • Ignoring women — Flora Tristan, Louise Otto-Peters, Anneke Mathilde-Franziska led 1840s women's movements.
  • Conflating the American Civil War with European nationalism — the US war was over slavery and federal authority, with different dynamics.

🗾 3.5 Asia in the Long Nineteenth Century: Meiji Japan and Late Qing
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The 19th century reversed the relative position of Asia and Europe. In 1800, Qing China was the largest economy in the world; by 1900, it was a sovereign-but-humiliated empire on the verge of collapse, its coastal cities under foreign "concessions," its government effectively bankrupted by indemnities from successive defeats. Japan, by contrast, after a century of cautious engagement, undertook the most successful crash modernisation in modern history and became — by 1905 — a recognised great power capable of defeating Russia. The divergent trajectories of the two empires test, more starkly than any other comparison, how political institutions and choices interact with external pressure.

The Opium Wars were the public humiliation of the Qing. British merchants, anxious to balance the trade deficit they accumulated buying tea from China, had been smuggling Indian opium into Chinese ports since the late 18th century. By 1830 several million Chinese were addicted, and outflows of silver to pay for opium were destabilising the Chinese economy. The Daoguang Emperor appointed Commissioner Lin Zexu, who in 1839 seized and destroyed 20,000 chests of British opium in Canton. Britain declared war. The First Opium War (1839–1842) ended with the Treaty of Nanjing: Britain took Hong Kong, opened five "treaty ports," extracted a $21 million indemnity, and won the principle of extraterritoriality (British subjects on Chinese soil tried under British law). The Second Opium War (1856–1860) opened more ports and legalised the opium trade; the Anglo-French expedition burned the Summer Palace in Beijing. The "Unequal Treaties" of these decades stripped the Qing of tariff autonomy and judicial sovereignty.

Internal rebellions compounded the dynasty's troubles. The Taiping Rebellion (1851–1864), led by Hong Xiuquan — a failed exam candidate who claimed to be the younger brother of Jesus Christ — captured Nanjing as its capital and ruled much of southern China for over a decade. It cost perhaps 20–30 million lives, the deadliest war of the 19th century anywhere. The Qing finally suppressed it in 1864 with the help of provincial Chinese armies (Zeng Guofan's Hunan Army) and Western-officered forces (the "Ever-Victorious Army" under Frederick Townsend Ward and later Charles Gordon). The dynasty survived; the relative empowerment of provincial governors would later contribute to the 1911 collapse.

The Meiji Restoration in Japan (1868) was the opposite response. After Commodore Matthew Perry's American squadron forced Japan to open in 1853–1854, Japanese reformers concluded that resistance required modernisation. A coalition of southwestern domains (Satsuma, Chōshū) overthrew the Tokugawa Shogunate in 1868 and "restored" the emperor (a fifteen-year-old, Mutsuhito, who took the regnal name Meiji, "enlightened rule"). Within twenty-five years, Japan abolished the samurai class (1873), introduced universal conscription and primary education (both 1873), promulgated a constitution modelled on Prussia's (1889), built modern textile and heavy industry (Tokyo, Osaka), constructed railways and a navy, and modernised legal codes. The leadership coalition explicitly studied Western models — sending the Iwakura Mission of 1871–1873 to inspect Europe and the United States — but selected and adapted, not copied. Japan defeated China in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895), seizing Taiwan and Korea's effective independence from China; defeated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), the first major non-European victory over a European power in modern times; and annexed Korea outright in 1910.

Japan's success and China's failure are the most-studied comparative question in modern Asian history. Plausible factors include: Japan's smaller population (around 30 million vs China's 400 million) and more easily reformable institutions; the legitimacy of the emperor as a symbolic focus of unity; Japan's relatively defensible island geography; the late-Tokugawa intellectual ferment (rangaku) that had already imported European medicine and science; the strategic clarity of the Meiji oligarchs; and luck — Japan was modernising during the brief window before European colonialism had locked Asia in. China's reformers (Self-Strengthening, the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898, the late Qing's New Policies of 1901–1911) attempted similar measures, but the empire was too large, the foreign pressures too constant, and the political cohesion at the top too brittle. The dynasty fell in 1911.

  • Opium Wars (1839–1842, 1856–1860) — British (and Anglo-French) wars to force Chinese acceptance of opium imports and free trade.
  • Treaty of Nanjing (1842) — first of the "unequal treaties"; ceded Hong Kong, opened treaty ports, extraterritoriality.
  • Taiping Rebellion (1851–1864) — religious-millenarian uprising in southern China; perhaps 20–30 million dead.
  • Meiji Restoration (1868) — overthrow of Tokugawa Shogunate and "restoration" of imperial rule; rapid modernisation programme.
  • Iwakura Mission (1871–1873) — Japanese diplomatic-fact-finding mission to Europe and US.
  • Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) — Japanese victory over Russia at Tsushima and Mukden.
  • Treaty ports — Chinese ports legally opened to foreign trade and residence by unequal treaties.

Worked Example — Document-Based Analysis: The Treaty of Nanjing

Article 2 of the Treaty of Nanjing (1842) reads (in translation):

"His Majesty the Emperor of China agrees, that British Subjects, with their families and establishments, shall be allowed to reside, for the purpose of carrying on their Mercantile pursuits, without molestation or restraint at the Cities and Towns of Canton, Amoy, Foochowfoo, Ningpo, and Shanghai…"

A Grade 12 historian asks four questions. (1) What kind of source? Treaty signed under duress after British naval defeat of Qing forces; a primary legal document. (2) What is the explicit claim? Five named ports are opened to British residence and commerce. (3) What is implicit? The Chinese imperial system, which had restricted foreign trade to Canton and worked through the Cohong merchant monopoly, is being forced to abandon its sovereign control over its own ports. (4) How would this look to different audiences? To Palmerston in London, a triumph for free trade; to Qing officials, a humiliating concession of sovereignty; to coastal Chinese merchants, both an opening and a threat. This four-question source-analysis pattern (provenance, explicit content, implicit content, perspective) is the basic move Strand A's expectation A1.3 ("assess the credibility of sources") asks for.

  • Calling the Opium Wars "trade disputes" — they involved British military attacks to coerce Chinese acceptance of a narcotic.
  • Crediting the Meiji Restoration solely to imitation of the West — the leadership was selective and creative; Japanese institutional traditions were heavily adapted, not abandoned.
  • Forgetting Korea, Vietnam, and other Asian states reshaped by the same century.
  • Treating "China and Japan" as a sufficient summary of Asia — South Asia (the British Raj), Southeast Asia (Dutch Indies, French Indochina), the Ottoman Empire, all matter.
  • Ignoring the role of women in Meiji modernisation (Tsuda Umeko's pioneering girls' education) and in the Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901, where women fighters were prominent).

🌍 3.6 The New Imperialism (1870–1914)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Between 1870 and 1914, a small handful of European powers (and from 1898 the United States and Japan) extended formal political control over most of Africa and large parts of Asia. The "new imperialism" was qualitatively different from earlier European empires: faster (the African continent was carved up in under thirty years), justified by pseudo-scientific racial theory, motivated by industrial-capitalist economic interests, and made possible by new technologies (the breech-loading rifle, the Maxim gun, quinine prophylaxis against malaria, the steamship, the telegraph). By 1914, Britain controlled a quarter of the world's land surface and population; France's empire was the second largest; Germany, Italy, Belgium, Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, the US, and Japan held substantial possessions. Almost all of these empires would collapse within 60 years.

The Scramble for Africa ran from roughly 1881 (the French takeover of Tunisia, British of Egypt) to 1914. At the start, Africa was about 10 percent European-controlled (coastal trading posts and French Algeria, Cape Colony); by 1914, every African state except Ethiopia and Liberia was a European colony. The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, convened by Bismarck at the request of King Leopold II of Belgium, laid down the rules: a European power could claim a coastal territory by occupying it and notifying others; the interior could be claimed by negotiating "treaties" with local rulers, often through duress or deception; "effective occupation" was the test. No African ruler was invited. Leopold's "Congo Free State" (1885–1908) — a personal possession, not Belgian state property — exemplified the worst: rubber-quotas enforced by mutilation, perhaps 10 million dead through atrocity, disease, and starvation, exposed by the Belgian socialist E. D. Morel and the British consul Roger Casement. Even by the standards of the era, the Congo scandal was so extreme that Belgium took over administration in 1908.

The British in India built the largest single colony in the new empire. After the 1857 Indian Rebellion (the "Indian Mutiny" in older British texts, the "First War of Independence" in Indian nationalist historiography), the British Crown took over administration from the East India Company. The Raj (1858–1947) built railways (45,000 km by 1900), telegraph lines, irrigation works, English-medium universities — and, in the same period, presided over recurrent famines (the 1876–1878 famine killed perhaps 5.5 million; the 1896–1902 famine 6–10 million more) while exporting grain. Indian historians from Dadabhai Naoroji onward documented the "drain theory" — that India transferred net wealth to Britain throughout the colonial period.

The justifications Europeans offered for empire ranged from outright racial supremacism (Carl Peters, Cecil Rhodes) through the "civilising mission" (Joseph Chamberlain, Jules Ferry) to Kipling's "White Man's Burden" (1899). Social-Darwinist theories — applying Darwin's biological evolution to societies, the "survival of the fittest" — were widely cited, with the colonised characterised as belonging to "lower stages" of civilisation. These ideas were not marginal; they were taught at Oxford and Cambridge, at the Sorbonne, in Berlin and Vienna, and they justified the legal, political, and economic structures of empire.

African and Asian resistance was constant. Some was military: the Zulu defeat of a British force at Isandlwana (1879); the Mahdist victory at Khartoum (1885); Ethiopia's stunning defeat of an Italian invasion at Adwa (1896), the only large-scale African military victory in the era that preserved independence; the Boxer Rebellion in China (1899–1901); the Maji Maji uprising against German East Africa (1905–1907); the Vietnamese Cần Vương ("aid the king") movement against the French. Other resistance was political and intellectual — the Indian National Congress (founded 1885), the All-India Muslim League (1906), early African nationalist newspapers, Pan-African Congresses (London 1900 onward, organised by W. E. B. Du Bois and Henry Sylvester Williams). By 1914 anti-colonial movements existed across the empire, even if independence would take another two generations to win.

  • New imperialism — late 19th-century rapid colonisation of Africa and Asia by European powers.
  • Berlin Conference (1884–1885) — European agreement on rules for partitioning Africa; no African delegates.
  • Scramble for Africa — partition of Africa by European powers between c.1881 and 1914.
  • British Raj — direct British rule of India 1858–1947, following the suppression of the 1857 rebellion.
  • Social Darwinism — late-19th-century pseudo-scientific ideology applying biological evolution to societies, used to justify imperialism and racism.
  • Battle of Adwa (1896) — Ethiopian victory under Menelik II over Italian invasion, preserving Ethiopian independence.
  • Indian National Congress — founded 1885; principal organisation of Indian nationalism.

Worked Example — Causes of the New Imperialism

Why did European powers rapidly partition Africa between 1881 and 1914?

Historians have argued multiple causes; a strong answer cites several. (1) Industrial economics: European industry needed raw materials (rubber from Congo, cotton from Egypt and India, palm oil from West Africa, gold and diamonds from South Africa) and protected markets. (2) Strategic rivalry: once one power began to acquire territory, others feared exclusion; the "preventive imperialism" argument (Robinson and Gallagher). (3) Technological enablement: the Maxim gun, quinine prophylaxis against malaria, and steamship transport made conquest militarily feasible at a tolerable cost. (4) Domestic politics: mass politics in Europe demanded "prestige," and imperial conquests played well in newspapers (Lord Salisbury once called colonies "useful for occupying the British public"). (5) Ideology: Social-Darwinism and missionary Christianity gave moral cover. (6) The "man on the spot": ambitious colonial officers (Lugard, Stanley) often outran instructions from London. A Level 4 paper integrates several causes and avoids monocausal answers. Strand A explicitly tests this multi-factor causal reasoning.

  • Calling the new imperialism merely economic — racial ideology, strategic competition, and missionary motives also drove it.
  • Treating African and Asian peoples as passive victims — resistance is documented at every stage; some of it was strikingly successful (Ethiopia, Japan).
  • Calling European empire "civilising" — the death tolls of Congo, Indian famines, and the Herero genocide (1904–1908) belong in the same accounting.
  • Forgetting the United States and Japan as imperial powers — both joined the imperial club after 1895 (US in Cuba, Philippines, Puerto Rico; Japan in Korea, Taiwan).
  • Mis-dating the Indian rebellion — 1857, sometimes wrongly recalled as 1858.
