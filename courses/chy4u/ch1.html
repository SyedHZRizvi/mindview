🎨 1.1 The Italian Renaissance (c.1400–1550)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Italian Renaissance was a cultural and intellectual transformation centred on the wealthy city-states of northern and central Italy — Florence, Venice, Milan, Rome, Ferrara, Urbino — between roughly 1400 and 1550. It is not the "rebirth of civilisation" that nineteenth-century historians once claimed; medieval Europe was sophisticated and learned. What did distinctively re-emerge was a self-conscious turn toward the secular, urban, and classical: a generation of scholars and artists deliberately modelled themselves on ancient Greece and Rome, while painters, sculptors, and architects invented new techniques (linear perspective, anatomical accuracy, classical proportion) that gave their work the look modern viewers associate with "Renaissance."

The economic engine of this transformation was Italian commerce. Genoese, Venetian, and Florentine merchants had grown rich in long-distance Mediterranean trade since the Crusades. By 1400, banking families like the Medici of Florence — who provided four popes and effectively ruled the city for most of the 15th century — were channelling commercial wealth into patronage of artists and humanist scholars. Patronage was not simply generous; it bought political legitimacy, divine favour, and family glory. When Cosimo de' Medici funded the rebuilding of San Lorenzo, he was also legitimating Medici rule.

Humanism — the scholarly movement at the Renaissance's intellectual core — emphasised the studia humanitatis: grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry, and moral philosophy, all studied in their original Latin and Greek. Humanists like Petrarch (1304–1374), Lorenzo Valla (1407–1457), and Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494) argued that engagement with the classical past would make Christians wiser and more virtuous. Valla famously used philological analysis to prove that the Donation of Constantine — a document the papacy had cited for centuries to justify temporal authority — was a forgery. This was scholarship as power.

Renaissance art transformed Western visual culture. Giotto (1267–1337) had already begun replacing the flat, otherworldly figures of Byzantine icons with weighted, three-dimensional bodies. Filippo Brunelleschi codified linear perspective in the 1410s; Masaccio applied it to fresco in the Brancacci Chapel. Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) combined scientific dissection with painting; Michelangelo (1475–1564) showed the human body as the central revelation of God's design (Sistine Chapel ceiling, 1508–1512); Raphael (1483–1520) synthesised the classical balance and the new religious feeling in the School of Athens. Painting moved from anonymous craft to celebrated individual achievement — a shift signalled by Giorgio Vasari's Lives of the Artists (1550).

Politically, the Italian Renaissance flourished within the unstable balance of five major states — Florence, Milan, Venice, the Papal States, and the Kingdom of Naples — plus smaller courts. Niccolò Machiavelli's The Prince (1513) is best read in this context: a Florentine secretary out of office, watching Italy fall under successive French and Spanish invasions, asks how a prince might preserve a state in a world where moral idealism failed. Italy's brilliant culture was preserved even as Italy's political independence was lost.

  • Humanism — Renaissance educational and intellectual programme based on the studia humanitatis.
  • Patronage — financial support of artists and scholars by wealthy individuals, families, or institutions.
  • Linear perspective — mathematical technique for representing three-dimensional space on a flat surface, codified by Brunelleschi.
  • City-state — sovereign urban polity (Florence, Venice, Milan) typical of Renaissance Italy.
  • Civic humanism — Florentine ideal that the active, public life of the citizen is the highest form of virtue.
  • Vernacular — the spoken language of a place (Italian, French) as distinct from Latin.
  • Quattrocento / Cinquecento — the 1400s / the 1500s, terms used in Italian art history.

Worked Example — Reading Machiavelli's "the ends justify the means"

Machiavelli's most famous line, often paraphrased as "the ends justify the means," actually reads (Chapter 18 of The Prince):

"In the actions of all men, and especially of princes, where there is no court of appeal, one judges by the result."

A Grade 12 historian asks three questions. (1) What kind of source is this? A treatise of political advice written 1513 by an out-of-office Florentine official, addressed to Lorenzo de' Medici. (2) What is its context? Italy is being repeatedly invaded by France and Spain; the Italian city-states cannot defend themselves. (3) What is the author's purpose? Machiavelli is arguing that princes who cling to conventional Christian virtue will be destroyed by princes who do not. He is therefore not endorsing cruelty in the abstract; he is making the empirical claim that political survival requires moral flexibility. Reading Machiavelli as simple cynicism misses the genre of advice-literature and the crisis of 1494–1513 it responds to.

  • Treating "the Renaissance" as the sudden invention of reason — medieval Europe had universities, scholastic philosophy, and Aristotle in Latin.
  • Calling humanism "secular" without qualification — most humanists, including Erasmus, were devout Christians.
  • Forgetting that Renaissance Italy was politically fragmented and frequently at war.
  • Ignoring women: Christine de Pizan, Isabella d'Este, Sofonisba Anguissola, Lavinia Fontana, Veronica Franco — all part of the period but routinely omitted.
  • Generalising from Florence to "the West" — Renaissance Italy was a particular set of urban societies, not a global model.

📜 1.2 The Protestant Reformation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

On 31 October 1517, a 33-year-old Augustinian friar and professor of theology named Martin Luther (1483–1546) sent his Ninety-five Theses to the Archbishop of Mainz. Whether or not he also posted them on the door of the castle church at Wittenberg (later legend probably embellished the act), the document — together with the cheap printing presses that copied it — touched off the most consequential schism in European history. Within a generation, much of northern Europe had broken with Rome; within a century, Europe was redrawn along confessional lines and had fought religious wars that killed perhaps a third of the German-speaking population.

The trigger was the sale of indulgences — papally authorised documents that, in popular understanding, reduced the time souls spent in purgatory. In 1517 Johann Tetzel was selling indulgences in Saxony to finance the rebuilding of St Peter's Basilica in Rome and to repay the debts of Archbishop Albrecht of Brandenburg. Luther's theses attacked the theological assumption that salvation could be bought. His deeper claim, developed over the next four years, was that salvation came through faith alone (sola fide), grounded in scripture alone (sola scriptura), by grace alone (sola gratia) — three theological propositions that, if accepted, made much of medieval Catholic practice (penance, indulgences, the cult of saints, the mediation of priests) unnecessary.

The Reformation succeeded for reasons that were never solely theological. The printing press, invented by Johann Gutenberg around 1450, had by 1517 produced perhaps twenty million books. Luther's pamphlets sold in tens of thousands; his German translation of the New Testament (1522) made scripture accessible to anyone literate in German. Princes in northern Germany, Scandinavia, and parts of Switzerland had political and financial reasons to seize church lands and reject Rome's authority — Henry VIII's break with Rome (1534), unlike Luther's, was almost purely political. Urban artisans found in priesthood-of-all-believers a doctrine that flattened ecclesiastical hierarchy. And Catholic responses (the Council of Trent, 1545–1563) hardened doctrinal positions on the Reformation side as much as on the Catholic.

Other reformations followed Luther. Huldrych Zwingli led the Zurich reformation from 1519. John Calvin (1509–1564) systematised reformed theology in his Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536, revised 1559); his Geneva became a model city for reformed Protestantism. The Anabaptists — radical reformers who rejected infant baptism and, in some cases, civil authority — were persecuted by Catholics, Lutherans, and Calvinists alike. The Catholic Church's own reform, the Counter-Reformation, produced new religious orders (the Jesuits, founded 1540), tightened doctrine (Trent), and launched missions to Asia and the Americas. The result by 1650 was confessional Europe: a continent permanently divided into Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed (and within Britain, Anglican and Puritan) zones, with religious dissent suppressed in each.

The wars of religion — most catastrophically the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) in the Holy Roman Empire — devastated central Europe. The 1648 Peace of Westphalia ended that war by recognising the principle cuius regio, eius religio ("whose realm, his religion") and effectively the sovereign-state system that would govern European international relations into the 20th century.

  • Indulgence — papally authorised reduction in the temporal punishment due for sin.
  • Sola fide / sola scriptura / sola gratia — by faith alone / by scripture alone / by grace alone — the Protestant theological pillars.
  • Anabaptist — radical reformer rejecting infant baptism and (often) civil authority.
  • Counter-Reformation — Catholic Church's internal reform and response to Protestantism, c.1540–1650.
  • Council of Trent — Catholic ecumenical council 1545–1563 that defined Counter-Reformation doctrine.
  • Cuius regio, eius religio — "whose realm, his religion" — principle of the 1555 Peace of Augsburg, reaffirmed in 1648.
  • Peace of Westphalia (1648) — treaties ending the Thirty Years' War; foundational moment for the sovereign-state system.

Worked Example — Cause and Consequence in the Reformation

A document-based question asks: "Was the Protestant Reformation caused primarily by Luther's theology, the printing press, or political ambition?"

A strong Grade 12 response refuses the false choice. It argues that Luther's theology supplied the content, but theology alone had been suppressed before — Jan Hus (1372–1415) had taught similar things and was burned at Constance. The printing press supplied the scale: Luther's To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation (1520) sold 4,000 copies in 18 days. Political ambition supplied protection: Elector Frederick the Wise hid Luther after the Diet of Worms (1521), and the German princes' interest in claiming church lands gave the Reformation territorial bases. The conclusion is multi-causal: Luther's theology was a necessary cause, but neither sufficient on its own. This is exactly the cause-and-consequence move historians want to see.

  • Calling Luther's theses "ninety-five reasons to leave the Catholic Church" — they were academic disputation points, not a manifesto for schism.
  • Conflating Lutheran and Calvinist theology (they differ on the Eucharist, predestination, and church governance).
  • Treating Henry VIII as a Protestant reformer — his break was political and his theology remained largely Catholic.
  • Forgetting Catholic reform: the Counter-Reformation was a renewal, not merely a reaction.
  • Ignoring women: Marguerite of Navarre, Argula von Grumbach, Katharina von Bora wrote, preached, or sheltered reformers.

⚓ 1.3 The Voyages of Exploration and the Columbian Exchange
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Between 1415 (the Portuguese capture of Ceuta) and 1522 (the return of the surviving Magellan-Elcano expedition), western Europeans crossed the Atlantic, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, reached India and East Asia by sea, and circumnavigated the globe. The "Age of Exploration" is a phrase that flatters Europeans by suggesting curiosity; the historian must read these voyages as commercial, religious, and geopolitical projects, undertaken by states (Portugal, Castile, England, the Netherlands, France) that were competing for trade routes, converts, and territory. Asian, African, and American peoples had complex maritime traditions of their own; what made the European voyages historically distinctive was their scale, the violence they brought, and the global trade system they founded.

The Portuguese, under Prince Henry "the Navigator," began systematically charting West Africa from 1419. Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488; Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498 and returned with spices that sold for 60 times their cost. The Spanish reach took a different route: Christopher Columbus, sailing west for Castile in 1492 in search of Asia, made landfall in the Bahamas and opened a permanent European presence in the Americas. Ferdinand Magellan's Spanish expedition (1519–1522) crossed the Pacific; one of its five ships made it back to Spain. The Dutch displaced the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean spice trade in the 17th century; the English and French followed.

The encounter with the Americas was demographically catastrophic for Indigenous peoples. Estimates of pre-1492 populations vary widely (35–100 million), but a consensus exists that within 150 years of contact, populations had fallen by 70–90 percent across most of the Americas. The main cause was epidemic disease — smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus — to which Indigenous peoples had no inherited immunity. Conquest accelerated the catastrophe. Hernán Cortés and 600 Spaniards, exploiting Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous allies who hated the Mexica empire, took Tenochtitlán in 1521; Francisco Pizarro captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532 and the Inca empire fell soon after.

The historian Alfred Crosby coined the term Columbian Exchange (1972) for the bidirectional transfer of plants, animals, microbes, and people across the Atlantic. The Americas sent the Old World maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cassava, peanuts, chili peppers, vanilla, cacao, and tobacco — a calorific revolution that, by 1750, was feeding population growth in China, Ireland, Italy, and West Africa. The Old World sent the Americas wheat, rice, sugar cane, citrus, coffee, horses, cattle, sheep, pigs, chickens — and smallpox, measles, and influenza. The Exchange also moved peoples: between 1500 and 1866, roughly 12.5 million enslaved Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas (about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage), the largest forced migration in human history. The plantation labour system — sugar in Brazil and the Caribbean, silver in Potosí, tobacco in Virginia — depended on coerced African and Indigenous labour and produced the wealth that financed Europe's commercial revolution.

The first global economy was therefore structured by Atlantic slavery and Pacific silver. Spanish silver mined at Potosí (Bolivia) by Indigenous mita labour was shipped to Manila and exchanged for Chinese silks and porcelain that ended up in European homes. Globalisation in 1600 already linked four continents — but the linkage was deeply unequal, with most of its costs paid by Indigenous Americans and West Africans.

  • Columbian Exchange — transfer of plants, animals, microbes, and people across the Atlantic after 1492.
  • Conquistador — Spanish conqueror in the Americas (Cortés, Pizarro).
  • Encomienda — Spanish royal grant entitling colonists to Indigenous labour and tribute, in theory in exchange for Christianisation.
  • Middle Passage — forced transatlantic voyage of enslaved Africans to the Americas.
  • Mita — Andean forced-labour rotation, especially in the silver mines of Potosí under Spanish rule.
  • Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) — Spanish-Portuguese treaty dividing the non-European world along a meridian.
  • Mercantilism — economic theory that national wealth = accumulated bullion; colonies exist to supply the metropole.

Worked Example — Reasoning with Demographic Numbers

Suppose pre-contact central Mexico had a population of approximately 25 million in 1519, and 1.5 million in 1620. What proportion is that?

The calculation: 1.5 / 25 = 0.06, or 6 percent of the 1519 population remaining a century later. The loss is 94 percent. This makes the Columbian Exchange — when one includes the demographic collapse from disease and violence — proportionally larger than any other recorded mortality event in human history, including the Black Death (which killed perhaps 50 percent of Europe in 1347–1351). The historian's task is to hold those numbers in mind when judging the "first global age" — global, yes; the costs, terribly unequal.

  • Calling 1492 "the discovery of America" — the Americas had been continuously inhabited for at least 14,000 years.
  • Attributing the conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires solely to Spanish technology — disease, Indigenous alliances, and political opportunism mattered as much.
  • Treating the Columbian Exchange as neutral biology — it was driven by, and reinforced, an unequal colonial system.
  • Forgetting that Africa was a participant, not just a victim — African states sold captives to Europeans, but the volume and brutality of the trade was unprecedented.
  • Equating "globalisation" with shared prosperity — early modern globalisation was structured by coercion.

⛓️ 1.4 The Atlantic Slave Trade and African Societies
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Atlantic slave trade is one of the most documented atrocities in human history. Between 1501 and 1866, the most authoritative database — the SlaveVoyages project at Emory University — records 36,000 transatlantic voyages that embarked approximately 12.5 million enslaved Africans, of whom roughly 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage to be sold in the Americas. The trade was unprecedented in scale, duration, and racial codification, and it shaped Atlantic economies, African polities, and global racial categories for centuries afterward.

Slavery itself was not a European invention. Domestic slavery existed in many West African societies, in the Ottoman Empire, and in Arabic-speaking North Africa; the Mediterranean had a continuous slave trade since antiquity. What changed after 1500 was the volume, the racial colour-coding ("African = slave," "European = free" became a legal and ideological norm only over the 16th and 17th centuries), and the location of bondage — large numbers of Africans were now removed permanently from Africa to plantations 5,000 km away. The first transatlantic shipment recorded landed in Hispaniola in 1501; the trade ended only when Britain banned its own ships from carrying slaves (1807), the United States banned imports (1808), and the trade was finally suppressed at sea over the next half-century.

The economics were brutal and simple. Sugar — labour-intensive, calorie-rich, and addictive — became the first mass-market consumer commodity of the European economy. To grow sugar in Brazil (Portuguese from c.1530) and the Caribbean (English, French, and Dutch from the 1640s), planters needed labour. Indigenous Caribbean populations had collapsed; European indentured servants were too few. African captives, purchased on the West African coast from African intermediaries who had taken them in war or raid, became the labour force. The triangular trade — European manufactures to Africa, enslaved Africans to the Americas, sugar and tobacco back to Europe — produced extraordinary profits for European merchants and planters, financed by joint-stock companies (the Royal African Company, the Dutch West India Company), insured by London marine underwriters, and protected by the Royal Navy and its Dutch and French equivalents.

African societies were not passive. Kingdoms like Kongo, Benin, Dahomey, Asante, and the Oyo Empire participated in the trade — usually selling captives taken in warfare with rival African polities. The trade altered African political structures: the demand for captives encouraged predatory raiding states, while gun imports gave armed states an advantage over unarmed neighbours. The historian Walter Rodney's influential How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972) argued that the trade extracted demographic and economic potential from Africa for four centuries; more recent historians, while challenging Rodney's specifics, broadly accept that the regions most engaged in the Atlantic trade experienced long-term demographic and political damage.

Enslaved Africans resisted continuously. Shipboard revolts (the most famous: the Amistad, 1839) were common enough that ship insurance accounted for them. Maroon communities of escaped enslaved people — Palmares in Brazil (1605–1694) and the Maroons of Jamaica — sustained sovereign African-descended polities for decades. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), discussed in Chapter 3, was the only successful slave revolt that produced an independent state. African-descended people in the Americas also created new cultures — Yoruba-derived religions like Candomblé and Santería, Akan-derived legal traditions in Suriname, Creole languages, gospel music, jazz — that re-made the Atlantic world's culture.

  • Triangular trade — three-leg Atlantic trade pattern: European manufactures to Africa, enslaved Africans to the Americas, plantation goods back to Europe.
  • Middle Passage — the transatlantic leg, marked by mortality of 12–25% per voyage in the 17th–18th centuries.
  • Chattel slavery — slavery in which the enslaved person is legally property, heritable, and racially coded — a New World innovation in scale.
  • Maroon — community of escaped enslaved people, often in remote terrain (Suriname, Jamaica, Brazil's Palmares).
  • Asiento — Spanish monopoly contract for supplying enslaved Africans to Spanish America.
  • Abolitionism — political movement to end the slave trade and slavery itself; mass-movement phase 1780s–1860s.

Worked Example — Reading a Slave-Ship Document

The Brookes slave ship diagram, published 1788 by British abolitionists, showed 482 enslaved people packed into a Liverpool ship that was actually licensed to carry 454 under the Regulated Slave Trade Act of 1788 — meaning it had been carrying around 600 before regulation. A historian asks: what kind of source is this?

The diagram is a secondary visualisation produced by the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade and the engraver William Elford, drawing on parliamentary testimony and Royal Navy measurements. It is therefore not a neutral record — it was designed to shock British voters into supporting abolition. But that does not make it inaccurate; cross-referenced with the captain's logs and parliamentary commissions, its dimensions are correct. The image's power as persuasion (it sold 8,000 copies) is separate from its power as evidence (the cargo space and the testimony stand up to corroboration). Holding the two together — analysing both how a source persuades and what it independently shows — is the historical-thinking skill at work.

  • Calling the slave trade "European" without naming the African states that participated — both did, though under deeply asymmetric power.
  • Treating chattel slavery as identical to other forms — its racial codification and scale were historically distinctive.
  • Saying "slavery ended in 1865" — chattel slavery in the United States, yes; the global suppression of slavery extended into the 20th century (Mauritania 1981 officially, 2007 criminalised).
  • Erasing African agency — Olaudah Equiano, Toussaint Louverture, Harriet Tubman, Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth are part of this history, not footnotes.
  • Ignoring that the legal end of trade did not end racism: the racial ideologies developed to justify slavery long outlived it.

🕌 1.5 The Ottoman, Mughal, and Ming Worlds (c.1450–1650)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A history that ends at Europe's western edge misses where most of the world's people, wealth, and political power lay in 1500. The three "gunpowder empires" of the early modern Islamic and East Asian world — the Ottoman Empire centred on Istanbul, the Mughal Empire in India, and the Ming dynasty (and from 1644, Qing dynasty) in China — together ruled a majority of the world's population and produced the greater part of its manufactured goods. CHY4U's curriculum explicitly requires that students study societies in the West and in other regions of the world. A balanced view of the 1450–1650 period therefore foregrounds these powers.

The Ottoman Empire took Constantinople in 1453 — an event so significant that European historians long used it to mark the end of the Middle Ages. Under Mehmed II "the Conqueror," Sultan Selim I, and most spectacularly Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent (r.1520–1566), the Ottomans extended from the Hungarian plain to Egypt, the Hejaz, and parts of Iraq and the Caucasus. The empire's military edge rested on the Janissary corps — initially Christian boys taken via the devshirme levy, converted to Islam, and trained as elite infantry. Suleiman's law code (kanun) and the architecture of his architect Mimar Sinan made Istanbul the largest city in Europe. The Ottoman millet system granted Christians and Jews communal self-government in exchange for the jizya tax, producing a multi-confessional empire that — although structured by Muslim primacy — sheltered Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain (1492) and offered greater de facto toleration than confessional Europe.

The Mughal Empire in India was founded in 1526 when Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia, defeated the Lodi sultan at Panipat. Under his grandson Akbar (r.1556–1605), the empire stretched from Kabul to Bengal and from Kashmir to the Deccan. Akbar abolished the jizya, married Rajput princesses, included Hindus in his administration, sponsored translations of the Mahabharata and the Ramayana into Persian, and held interfaith debates at his Fatehpur Sikri court — a religious policy known as sulh-i kull ("universal peace"). His great-grandson Aurangzeb (r.1658–1707) reversed many of these accommodations. Mughal cotton textiles dominated world markets; in 1700, India produced around a quarter of global manufactured goods.

The Ming dynasty (1368–1644) ruled the largest economy in the world. Under the Yongle Emperor (r.1402–1424), the Ming sponsored the famous voyages of Admiral Zheng He: between 1405 and 1433 he led seven expeditions of treasure ships — some over 120 metres long — to South Asia, the Persian Gulf, and the East African coast. The Ming abruptly halted the voyages after 1433 for reasons that still divide historians (cost, Confucian conservatism, refocus on northern defence against Mongol incursions). The Ming wall-building programme produced the version of the Great Wall most tourists see today. By the early 17th century, however, fiscal crisis, peasant rebellion, and Manchu pressure from the north destabilised the dynasty; in 1644 the Manchu Qing dynasty took Beijing and ruled China until 1912.

Reading these empires alongside the European Renaissance shows that 1500 was a world of multiple centres of wealth and innovation. Astronomy in Samarkand under Ulugh Beg, miniature painting in Tabriz, the philosophical poetry of Hafez and Rumi, the algebra of al-Khwarizmi (still influencing Europe's mathematics), Chinese movable type (centuries before Gutenberg though less efficient for ideographic scripts), Korean rain-gauge meteorology under Sejong — none of these can be folded into a "rise of the West" narrative. The integration of Asia into world history is not optional; it is part of the curriculum and a basic correction to Eurocentric story-telling.

  • Janissary — Ottoman elite infantry recruited via the devshirme child-levy from Christian populations.
  • Millet system — Ottoman governance giving non-Muslim communities autonomy under their own religious authorities.
  • Sulh-i kull — Akbar's policy of "universal peace" and religious toleration.
  • Mansabdar — Mughal administrative rank, tied to a numerical rank and salary in tax-revenue assignment (jagir).
  • Zheng He — Ming admiral whose seven voyages 1405–1433 reached the East African coast.
  • Mandate of Heaven — Chinese political concept that legitimate rule depends on the ruler's virtue and is forfeited by misrule.
  • Suleiman the Magnificent — Ottoman sultan (r.1520–1566), under whom the empire reached its political and cultural peak.

Worked Example — Comparing Imperial Tolerance

How did religious diversity work under Akbar (Mughal, 1556–1605) compared to Philip II of Spain (1556–1598)?

Akbar abolished the discriminatory jizya tax on non-Muslims in 1564, integrated Hindu Rajputs into senior military command, sponsored translations of Sanskrit epics, and held interfaith debates including with Christians and Zoroastrians. Philip II expelled the Moriscos (Spanish Muslims) in 1609 (continuing earlier expulsions of Jews in 1492), funded the Inquisition's prosecution of suspected Protestants and crypto-Jews, and led Catholic armies in the Dutch Revolt and the Spanish Armada (1588). Both rulers governed religiously diverse populations; their answers diverged sharply. A Grade 12 historian uses this comparison not to praise Akbar at Europe's expense but to challenge the unexamined assumption that toleration was a European invention. It was not, and the comparison's purpose is to make that visible.

  • Calling the Ottoman, Mughal, and Ming empires "non-Western" as though they were exotic exceptions — they ruled most of the world's population.
  • Treating Islamic empires as monolithic — Shi'i Safavid Iran fought Sunni Ottoman Turkey throughout the 16th–17th centuries.
  • Forgetting the 1644 Qing conquest of China — a major political transition often skipped in survey courses.
  • Mis-dating Zheng He's voyages by a century (they ended in 1433, well before Columbus).
  • Using terms like "Mohammedan" or "Hindoo" — anachronistic and pejorative; use "Muslim," "Hindu."

🔭 1.6 The Beginnings of the Scientific Revolution
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Scientific Revolution names the transformation in European understanding of nature between roughly 1543 (Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium) and 1687 (Newton's Principia). Within these dates the earth lost its place at the centre of the cosmos, planetary motion was first described mathematically, the human body was mapped anatomically, microscopes and telescopes opened the very small and very large to inspection, and the modern category "science" (then called natural philosophy) began to crystallise as a method-based, communal, falsifiable enterprise. The revolution did not destroy religion — many of its leading figures were devoutly Christian — but it slowly shifted authority for claims about nature from textual tradition (Aristotle, Galen, the Bible) toward observation and mathematical reasoning.

Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543), a Polish canon and astronomer, proposed in De revolutionibus (1543) that the earth orbits the sun rather than vice versa. The heliocentric model fit some observations better than the elaborate Ptolemaic system but had problems of its own; Copernicus's diagrams still used circular orbits. Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) replaced the circles with ellipses in his three laws (1609, 1619), based on Tycho Brahe's extraordinarily precise observations of Mars. Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) turned the new telescope on the sky in 1609 and saw mountains on the moon, four moons around Jupiter, phases of Venus — observations incompatible with Aristotelian cosmology. He was tried by the Roman Inquisition in 1633 for defending heliocentrism and spent his last years under house arrest. The Catholic Church only formally exonerated him in 1992.

The new sciences extended beyond astronomy. Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543, the same year as Copernicus) replaced Galen's textual anatomy with detailed dissection-based illustrations and corrected hundreds of Galen's errors. William Harvey demonstrated the circulation of the blood (1628). Francis Bacon's Novum Organum (1620) argued for an inductive, experimental method and against deference to ancient authority. René Descartes's Discourse on Method (1637) made systematic doubt and analytical geometry foundational moves of the new philosophy. The Royal Society (London, 1660) and the French Académie des Sciences (1666) institutionalised the new natural philosophy: papers were read, experiments demonstrated, claims peer-reviewed.

Why did the Scientific Revolution happen when and where it did? Historians have offered many answers: the printing press enabled rapid circulation of results; the religious upheavals of the Reformation broke the Catholic Church's intellectual monopoly; rich commercial cities (Florence, Padua, Leiden, London) supported instrument-makers and patronised scholars; the recovery of Greek texts (especially Archimedes) gave mathematicians new tools; the new astronomical and navigational data generated by the voyages of exploration demanded better theory. None of these is alone sufficient. What is certain is that "science" as a self-conscious practice with a method, journals, and communal verification did not exist in 1500 and did exist by 1700. Newton's Principia Mathematica (1687) crowned that transformation by uniting terrestrial and celestial mechanics — and Newton himself, characteristically, also wrote more on theology and alchemy than on the physics for which we remember him.

The Scientific Revolution had cost. Aristotelian and Galenic frameworks held together humans, animals, and cosmos in a single qualitative scheme; the new science separated quantitative measurement from qualitative meaning, and produced disciplinary specialisation that the modern world has inherited. Women, who had practised midwifery and herbal medicine for centuries, were progressively excluded from the new male, university-based, instrument-rich enterprise. And, while many practitioners remained devout, the long-term effect of the Scientific Revolution — augmented by the Enlightenment in Chapter 2 — was the slow secularisation of European elite culture.

  • Heliocentrism — model in which the earth orbits the sun (Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo).
  • Geocentrism — model in which the earth is the centre of the cosmos (Ptolemy, Aristotle).
  • Natural philosophy — pre-1800 term for what we now call science.
  • Inductive method — reasoning from particular observations to general laws (Bacon).
  • Deductive method — reasoning from general principles to particular conclusions (Descartes).
  • Royal Society — founded London 1660; published the first scientific journal, Philosophical Transactions, in 1665.
  • Vesalius — Flemish anatomist whose 1543 dissection-based Fabrica revolutionised medicine.

Worked Example — Continuity and Change in the Scientific Revolution

Question: To what extent did the Scientific Revolution represent a break with medieval thought, and to what extent did it continue medieval traditions?

A strong response holds both threads in tension. Break: the rejection of Aristotelian physics (earth at rest, four elements, four causes); the replacement of textual authority with observation; the use of mathematics as the language of nature ("the book of nature is written in the language of mathematics" — Galileo); the establishment of independent learned societies. Continuity: medieval universities had taught Aristotle, Euclid, and Ptolemy, providing the technical training Copernicus relied on; Christian theology framed many practitioners' motives (Kepler saw planetary harmony as evidence of God's design); alchemy continued to interest Newton and Boyle; Aristotelian frameworks survived in biology for another two centuries. The most defensible answer is that the Scientific Revolution was a slow, partial, contested transformation — not a sudden replacement — and that historians who emphasise either rupture or continuity exclusively are oversimplifying. This is exactly the kind of continuity-and-change reasoning Strand A's historical-thinking concepts demand.

  • Calling the trial of Galileo "religion vs science" — most leading scientists were devout; the conflict was about textual authority and politics.
  • Treating Copernicus's heliocentrism as immediately accepted — it was disputed for a century.
  • Dating the "Scientific Revolution" too sharply — historians disagree on whether to begin in 1543, 1610, 1620, or even later.
  • Ignoring contributions from outside Europe — Islamic astronomy (al-Tusi's lemma) and Indian mathematics fed European science.
  • Forgetting that "science" as a profession is a 19th-century coinage (William Whewell, 1833).
