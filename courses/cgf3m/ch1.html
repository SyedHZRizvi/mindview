🌍 1.1 The Four Spheres of Earth
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Physical geographers approach the Earth as four overlapping systems — the lithosphere (rocks and soils), the atmosphere (air), the hydrosphere (all water, including the cryosphere of ice), and the biosphere (living things). The boundaries between these systems are useful fictions rather than firm lines; in practice the four spheres exchange matter and energy continuously, and almost every interesting geographic question — from the timing of monsoons to the location of agricultural belts — sits at one of the interfaces. A pine tree, for example, has roots in the lithosphere, exchanges gases with the atmosphere, transpires water from the hydrosphere, and is itself part of the biosphere. None of those statements is wrong, and that is precisely the point: the spheres are descriptive layers laid over a single integrated planet.

Energy drives the system. The Sun supplies roughly 174,000 terawatts of incoming shortwave radiation to the top of the atmosphere, a value remarkably stable on human timescales (the so-called solar constant, about 1361 W/m² at Earth's mean orbital distance). About 30% is reflected back to space (the planetary albedo), 23% is absorbed by the atmosphere, and 47% is absorbed by Earth's surface. The surface re-emits this energy as longwave infrared radiation; greenhouse gases — water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone — absorb some of that infrared and re-radiate it back downward, producing the natural greenhouse effect that keeps mean surface temperature near +15 °C instead of −18 °C. A second, much smaller energy source comes from inside the Earth — radioactive decay of uranium, thorium, and potassium-40 in the mantle and crust, totalling about 44 terawatts — which drives plate tectonics, volcanism, and the geothermal gradient.

The four spheres interact through specific couplings that recur throughout the course. Lithosphere–atmosphere: mountain ranges deflect prevailing winds, producing rainshadows (the Rocky Mountains' lee side is dry). Atmosphere–hydrosphere: evaporation from oceans transports heat poleward and supplies precipitation. Hydrosphere–biosphere: water availability sets biome distribution; without water there is no life. Biosphere–atmosphere: photosynthesising organisms produced the oxygen atmosphere we breathe over roughly two billion years (the Great Oxidation Event). Geographers refer to these reciprocal influences as feedbacks: positive feedbacks amplify change (melting Arctic ice lowers albedo, absorbing more sunlight, melting more ice), while negative feedbacks dampen it (a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, which forms more cloud, which can increase albedo).

  • Lithosphere — the solid rock and soil surface of Earth, roughly the top 100 km.
  • Atmosphere — the gaseous envelope: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere.
  • Hydrosphere — all water — oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater — and the cryosphere (ice).
  • Biosphere — the global community of all living organisms.
  • Albedo — fraction of incoming sunlight that a surface reflects (0 = total absorption, 1 = total reflection).
  • Solar constant — incoming solar irradiance at top of atmosphere, ≈ 1361 W/m².
  • Greenhouse effect — absorption and re-radiation of longwave infrared by atmospheric gases.
  • Feedback — process by which an output influences future inputs; positive amplifies, negative dampens.

Worked Example — Estimating Surface Energy Absorbed

Earth's cross-sectional area is \(A = \pi r^2\), where \(r = 6{,}371\) km. The solar irradiance reaching the top of the atmosphere is \(S_0 = 1361\) W/m². Total power intercepted: \(P = S_0 \cdot \pi r^2 = 1361 \times \pi \times (6.371 \times 10^6)^2 \approx 1.74 \times 10^{17}\) W (174 PW). Averaging this across Earth's full surface area \(4\pi r^2\) gives a mean of \(S_0/4 \approx 340\) W/m². If planetary albedo is 0.30, then 30% is reflected, so the average absorbed is \((1 - 0.30) \times 340 \approx 238\) W/m². The Stefan–Boltzmann blackbody temperature is \(T = (238/\sigma)^{1/4}\) where \(\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\); this gives \(T \approx 255\) K = −18 °C. The fact that Earth's actual mean temperature is +15 °C, a 33 °C difference, is the strength of the natural greenhouse effect. This calculation is the foundation of all energy-balance climate models.

  • Treating the four spheres as sealed compartments rather than overlapping continua.
  • Confusing the natural greenhouse effect (essential for life) with anthropogenic enhancement (a problem).
  • Forgetting that albedo varies spatially and seasonally (fresh snow ~0.9, dark ocean ~0.06).
  • Treating Earth's internal heat flux as negligible — it dominates plate tectonics even though it is tiny compared with solar input.
  • Using "climate" and "weather" interchangeably (weather is short-term, climate is the multi-decadal average).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "explain the major characteristics of and interrelationships among Earth's four physical systems (lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere)."

🌋 1.2 Plate Tectonics & Earth's Interior
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Earth is layered like a hard-boiled egg with an unusually thin shell. The crust is 5–10 km thick under the oceans and 30–70 km thick under continents — a vanishingly small fraction of the planet's 6,371 km radius. Beneath the crust lies the mantle, which despite being solid rock flows like extremely viscous toffee on geological timescales. Inside the mantle, an outer liquid iron core (the source of Earth's magnetic field through dynamo action) surrounds a solid inner core kept solid by tremendous pressure despite temperatures near 5500 °C. The boundary between cool brittle crust + uppermost mantle (the lithosphere) and the warmer, more ductile mantle (the asthenosphere) is mechanically important: the lithosphere is broken into roughly 15 large plates that float and slide over the asthenosphere.

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, citing matching coastlines (Africa and South America), identical fossils (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris), and continuous rock formations across modern oceans. He was widely ridiculed because he had no mechanism. The mechanism — convection currents in the mantle plus seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges — was supplied between the 1950s and 1960s by Harry Hess (seafloor spreading), Vine and Matthews (magnetic stripes on the seafloor), and J. Tuzo Wilson (the Hawaiian hotspot trail and the concept of plate boundaries). By 1968, plate tectonics had replaced older "geosyncline" theories and become the unifying paradigm of geology.

Plate boundaries come in three types and produce most of the world's earthquakes, volcanoes, and major landforms. Divergent boundaries (e.g., the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the East African Rift) split plates apart as magma rises and solidifies, widening the ocean basin by 2–10 cm/year. Convergent boundaries bring plates together: ocean-continent convergence (Nazca-South America) produces a deep trench, a subduction zone, andesitic volcanoes (the Andes), and great earthquakes; ocean-ocean convergence produces island arcs (Japan, Aleutians); continent-continent collision (India-Eurasia) produces mountain ranges (Himalayas, still rising 5 mm/year). Transform boundaries grind plates past each other horizontally (San Andreas fault, Anatolian fault) and produce earthquakes without volcanism. Hotspots (Hawaii, Yellowstone) are mantle plumes that pierce moving plates, producing chains of volcanoes that get older with distance from the active vent.

  • Lithosphere vs asthenosphere — rigid outer rock layer vs ductile underlying mantle.
  • Continental drift — Wegener's 1912 idea that continents move; precursor to plate tectonics.
  • Seafloor spreading — new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and spreads laterally.
  • Subduction zone — dense oceanic plate sinks beneath a less dense plate, producing trenches and volcanoes.
  • Convergent / divergent / transform boundary — three plate-margin types.
  • Hotspot — fixed mantle plume producing volcanic activity (Hawaii, Iceland, Yellowstone).
  • Pacific Ring of Fire — the belt of subduction zones encircling the Pacific where ~75% of Earth's volcanoes and ~90% of earthquakes occur.

Worked Example — Calculating the Age of a Hawaiian Volcano

The Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain is produced by the Pacific Plate moving over a stationary mantle hotspot. The Big Island of Hawaii (still active) is at the current vent. Kauai, 540 km to the northwest, is dated by radiometric methods to ≈ 5.1 million years old. Calculate the rate of Pacific Plate motion. Rate = distance / time = \(540\ \text{km} / 5.1 \times 10^6\ \text{yr} = 1.06 \times 10^{-4}\) km/yr = 10.6 cm/year, in the direction NW. This matches independent GPS-measured Pacific Plate velocity. The same logic, applied to older volcanoes like Suiko Seamount (≈ 65 Ma), reveals a bend in the chain where the Pacific Plate's direction of motion changed about 47 Ma — a major geological event detectable from a wall of underwater volcanoes.

  • Saying the Earth's interior is "molten lava" — most of the mantle is solid rock that flows slowly.
  • Confusing continental drift (Wegener, 1912) with plate tectonics (1960s synthesis).
  • Thinking plate boundaries are always at coastlines (the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is entirely underwater).
  • Believing hotspots themselves move — the plate moves over a relatively stationary plume.
  • Treating earthquakes as random — they cluster at plate boundaries, with magnitude tied to plate type and depth.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.2 — "explain plate-tectonic theory and the processes that occur at convergent, divergent, and transform plate boundaries."

⛰️ 1.3 Weathering, Erosion, and Landforms
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Plate tectonics builds elevation; weathering and erosion tear it down. The shape of any landscape is the net result of these uplift-and-removal processes operating over time, and Ontario's own gentle, glaciated topography is as much a product of erosion as the soaring peaks of British Columbia are products of uplift. Three classes of process matter. Weathering breaks rock apart in place. Erosion transports the broken pieces. Deposition lays them down somewhere else, where they may eventually compact into new sedimentary rock — closing the rock cycle. Mountains rise at perhaps 1–10 mm/year while erosion lowers them at a comparable rate; over millions of years, the balance determines whether a range grows or shrinks.

Weathering is subdivided into physical, chemical, and biological categories. Physical weathering disintegrates rock without changing its composition: frost wedging (water freezes in cracks, expands by 9% in volume, levers the rock apart), exfoliation (pressure release as overlying rock erodes, causing concentric sheets to peel off), salt-crystal growth (in coastal and desert environments), thermal expansion and contraction. Chemical weathering decomposes rock by altering its minerals: carbonation (CO₂ dissolved in rainwater forms weak carbonic acid that dissolves limestone, producing karst topography with caves and sinkholes), hydrolysis (water reacts with feldspars to produce clay), oxidation ("rusting" of iron-rich minerals). Biological weathering combines both: tree roots wedge rocks apart, lichens secrete acids that dissolve mineral surfaces, burrowing animals expose fresh rock.

Erosion is dominated by four agents: running water (rivers, the most volumetrically important agent globally), moving ice (glaciers, which sculpted Ontario), wind (most effective in deserts), and waves (along coastlines). Glacial erosion is particularly important for Canada. During the Quaternary Ice Age, continental ice sheets up to 3 km thick covered nearly all of Canada multiple times; the most recent glaciation (the Wisconsinan) reached its peak ~22,000 years ago and retreated from southern Ontario only about 12,000 years ago. The ice sheets scraped soil and rock from the Canadian Shield, gouged out the Great Lakes basins, deposited moraines and drumlins, and left behind the till plains and erratic boulders that dominate southern Ontario's surface geology. Toronto sits on glacial-lake clays. The cliffs of the Niagara Escarpment expose the resistant dolostone caprock that has retreated about 11 km since glacial melting first exposed it.

  • Weathering — in-situ breakdown of rock; physical, chemical, biological.
  • Erosion — transport of weathered material by water, ice, wind, or waves.
  • Deposition — laying down of transported material, often as new sediment.
  • Rock cycle — continuous transformation among igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock.
  • Frost wedging — physical weathering by repeated freeze-thaw cycles.
  • Karst — landscape of caves, sinkholes, and underground drainage formed by carbonation of limestone.
  • Moraine — ridge of debris deposited at the edge of a glacier (e.g., Oak Ridges Moraine, GTA).
  • Drumlin — elongated whaleback hill streamlined by glacial flow (e.g., Peterborough drumlin field).
  • Erratic — boulder transported by ice far from its source bedrock.

Worked Example — Estimating Niagara Falls Retreat Rate

Glacial Lake Iroquois drained 12,500 years ago, exposing the Niagara Escarpment caprock to erosion. The falls have since retreated about 11 km upstream from their original position at Queenston Heights. Average retreat rate = \(11{,}000\ \text{m} / 12{,}500\ \text{years} = 0.88\) m/year. Historical observations (1842 to present) record higher rates of 1–1.2 m/year before flow diversion for hydroelectric power, and roughly 0.3 m/year today with reduced flow. The numbers reveal three lessons: (a) average rates hide variation; (b) human engineering can substantially slow geological processes; (c) the absolute retreat distance (11 km) confirms that the present falls are geologically young and will eventually erode upstream to reach Lake Erie, draining the falls altogether — but on a timescale of about \(40\) km / 0.3 m/yr ≈ 130,000 years.

  • Confusing weathering (breakdown in place) with erosion (transport).
  • Forgetting that Ontario's topography is glacial — the soil under Toronto was carried here by ice.
  • Treating freeze-thaw as a temperature problem rather than a volume problem (water expands 9% when it freezes).
  • Equating "rock cycle" with "water cycle" — different timescales, different drivers.
  • Assuming caves require running water — many are dissolved out by groundwater long before any river appears.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "explain processes of weathering, erosion, and deposition and the landforms they produce in a variety of physical environments."

🌬️ 1.4 The Atmosphere & Global Circulation
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The atmosphere is a thin shell of gases retained by Earth's gravity. By volume, dry air at sea level is 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.93% argon, and 0.04% carbon dioxide — a composition stable on geological timescales (though CO₂ has risen from 280 ppm before industrialization to over 420 ppm today). Water vapour is highly variable (0–4%) and is by far the most important greenhouse gas in absolute terms, though it is a feedback rather than a forcing because its abundance is set by temperature. The atmosphere is layered by temperature: the troposphere (0–12 km) where weather happens; the stratosphere (12–50 km) housing the ozone layer; the mesosphere (50–85 km); and the thermosphere (85–600 km) where the aurora forms. Pressure decreases roughly exponentially with height, halving every 5.5 km — which is why aircraft must pressurise their cabins.

Global circulation arises because Earth receives more solar energy in the tropics than at the poles, and the atmosphere acts to even out the imbalance. The simplest model is the three-cell model. In each hemisphere, a Hadley cell rises near the equator (the Intertropical Convergence Zone, or ITCZ, with year-round heavy rain — the wet rainforest belt) and descends near 30° latitude (the subtropical high-pressure belt, producing the world's great deserts: Sahara, Arabian, Atacama, Australian outback). A Ferrel cell circulates from 30° to 60°, producing the mid-latitude westerlies that bring most of Canada's weather. A Polar cell descends at the pole and flows outward as polar easterlies, meeting the Ferrel cell at the polar front (around 60°), which is where the powerful jet streams meander.

Earth's rotation deflects winds — the Coriolis effect — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. This converts simple north-south pressure-driven flow into the trade winds (easterlies at low latitudes), the prevailing westerlies (mid-latitudes), and the polar easterlies. Hurricanes spin counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern because of Coriolis deflection of converging air. Ocean currents follow similar patterns, and they redistribute enormous quantities of heat: the Gulf Stream carries warmth from the Caribbean to western Europe, which is why Halifax (44° N) has colder winters than Lisbon (38° N) despite being further south. Understanding global circulation is the prerequisite for explaining nearly every climate-zone, biome, and precipitation pattern.

  • Troposphere — lowest atmospheric layer where weather occurs.
  • Stratosphere — second layer, contains the ozone layer that absorbs UV.
  • ITCZ (Intertropical Convergence Zone) — equatorial belt of converging trade winds and heavy precipitation.
  • Hadley / Ferrel / Polar cells — three meridional circulation cells per hemisphere.
  • Coriolis effect — apparent deflection of moving objects (including air) caused by Earth's rotation.
  • Jet stream — fast (≥100 km/h) westerly winds near the tropopause; storm tracks follow them.
  • Trade winds — easterly surface winds between the equator and 30°.
  • Westerlies — prevailing mid-latitude winds from west to east.

Worked Example — Why is the Sahara a desert?

Apply the Hadley-cell model. (1) Strong heating at the equator produces rising air that cools adiabatically; water vapour condenses and falls as the heavy equatorial rains of the ITCZ. (2) By the time the air reaches the upper troposphere it has lost most of its moisture. (3) It moves poleward and descends near 30° N (and 30° S). (4) Descending air warms adiabatically at about 10 °C per km, which increases its capacity to hold water — so the descending air is dry and the local climate is arid. The world's major hot deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Australian outback, Atacama, Mojave-Sonoran) all cluster around 30° N or 30° S precisely because they lie under the Hadley descending limb. The same logic explains the equatorial rainforests on the other end of the cell. The Hadley model is the single most powerful tool a Grade 11 geographer has for predicting where Earth's biome boundaries will lie.

  • Claiming Coriolis "deflects toilet water" — Coriolis is negligible at the scale of a sink.
  • Treating the ITCZ as fixed at 0° — it migrates seasonally north and south, driving monsoons.
  • Confusing the ozone layer (stratosphere, beneficial) with ozone smog (troposphere, harmful).
  • Saying winds "blow toward low pressure" without acknowledging Coriolis turns them into rotating systems.
  • Forgetting that adiabatic warming (descending air) produces dry conditions just as adiabatic cooling (rising air) produces rain.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.2 — "describe global atmospheric circulation patterns and the factors that influence them (e.g., the three-cell model, the Coriolis effect, jet streams, the ITCZ)."

💧 1.5 The Hydrologic Cycle & Freshwater Systems
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Water on Earth is a closed system — its total mass has been essentially constant for billions of years — but the location and phase of water are in constant motion. The hydrologic cycle describes this movement: water evaporates from oceans, lakes, and soil; transpires from plant leaves (combined the two are called evapotranspiration); condenses as it rises and cools, forming clouds; precipitates as rain, snow, sleet, or hail; runs off into rivers or infiltrates the ground to recharge groundwater; and eventually returns to the oceans. Solar energy powers the entire cycle, lifting roughly 500,000 km³ of water annually from Earth's surface against gravity. The cycle is the bridge between the atmosphere and the lithosphere/biosphere and the most important physical-geography concept students should master.

Earth's water is unequally distributed among reservoirs. Of the 1.39 billion km³ of total water, oceans hold 97.5%; only 2.5% is fresh, and most of that (1.74%) is locked in glaciers and ice caps. Liquid fresh water — what humans actually use — is less than 1% of total water, with the bulk in groundwater (0.76%), only 0.013% in lakes and rivers, and a trace in soil moisture and the atmosphere. Despite this scarcity, the cycle's rapid turnover means liquid fresh water is being replenished continuously: atmospheric water cycles every 8 days on average, river water in 16 days, soil moisture in months, and groundwater in years to millennia depending on aquifer depth. The mismatch between rapid surface turnover and slow groundwater recharge is at the heart of every modern water-management problem.

Canada is unusually water-rich. The country holds about 7% of the world's renewable fresh water and 9% of its accessible surface fresh water, yet only 0.5% of world population. The Great Lakes alone contain about 22,700 km³ — roughly 21% of the planet's surface fresh water. But this abundance is misleading. Most Canadian water flows north to the Arctic and Hudson Bay, while most Canadians live within 200 km of the southern border. Toronto draws its drinking water from Lake Ontario, the smallest of the Great Lakes and the most downstream — so any pollution upstream eventually reaches Toronto. The 2000 Walkerton, Ontario, water tragedy (May 2000) — in which seven people died and 2,300 became ill from E. coli contamination of municipal wells — became the defining Canadian case study in source-water protection and remains required reading in this course.

  • Evaporation / transpiration / evapotranspiration — water moving from surface or plants into the atmosphere.
  • Precipitation — rain, snow, sleet, hail; the only input to most terrestrial systems.
  • Runoff — water flowing over the surface to streams and rivers.
  • Infiltration — water soaking into the soil.
  • Groundwater & aquifer — water below the water table, stored in porous rock or sediment.
  • Watershed (drainage basin) — area of land draining to a common outlet; basic unit of water management.
  • Residence time — average time a water molecule spends in a reservoir before moving on.

Worked Example — Calculating a Reservoir's Residence Time

Residence time \(\tau\) is given by \(\tau = V / Q\), where \(V\) is reservoir volume and \(Q\) is the inflow (or outflow) rate. For Lake Superior: volume \(V \approx 12{,}100\) km³; mean outflow \(Q \approx 2{,}100\) m³/s. Convert outflow to km³/yr: \(Q = 2{,}100\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} \times 3.156 \times 10^7\ \text{s/yr} \approx 6.6 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}^3/\text{yr} = 66\) km³/yr. Then \(\tau = 12{,}100 / 66 \approx 183\) years. By contrast, Lake Erie (volume 484 km³, outflow ≈ 175 km³/yr) has \(\tau \approx 2.8\) years. The contrast matters: pollutants persist much longer in Lake Superior, while Lake Erie can recover more quickly (which is why Lake Erie's recovery from 1960s eutrophication was relatively rapid once phosphorus controls were imposed). Residence time is the right number to use when designing pollution-control policy.

  • Calling all water "renewable" — fossil groundwater (e.g., the Ogallala Aquifer) is effectively non-renewable on human timescales.
  • Forgetting transpiration — terrestrial plants pump more water into the atmosphere than evaporation from lakes does.
  • Treating watersheds as merely physical units — political borders rarely match them, complicating management.
  • Assuming Canada's water abundance prevents scarcity — Prairie aquifers and First Nations reserves face chronic shortages.
  • Confusing weather (short-term precipitation) with the multi-year hydrologic budget.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.3 — "explain the hydrologic cycle and the distribution and movement of water through Earth's physical systems."

🌱 1.6 Soils — Pedology & the Pedosphere
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Soil — the very thin, fragile, living layer between bedrock and atmosphere — is where most life happens. Geographers use the word pedosphere to emphasise that soil is a system in its own right, formed at the interface of the lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. A typical productive soil might be 50% mineral particles (sand, silt, clay), 5% organic matter, and 45% void space split roughly equally between air and water. The mineral fraction comes from weathered parent rock; the organic fraction comes from decomposed plants and animals; the air and water occupy the pore spaces between aggregates. Without soil, terrestrial agriculture and most forests would not exist, and yet a single centimetre of topsoil may take a century or more to form. Soils are therefore both a renewable resource (on long timescales) and a non-renewable resource (on human timescales).

The classic Russian-developed model of soil formation, refined by Hans Jenny in the 1940s, identifies five factors abbreviated CLORPT: climate (precipitation and temperature determine weathering rate and biological activity), organisms (vegetation type, soil fauna, microorganisms), relief (slope position influences drainage and erosion), parent material (the rock or sediment from which the soil weathers), and time (older soils are typically more differentiated). Operating together, these factors produce a vertical sequence of layers called a soil profile: an organic O horizon at the top (leaf litter), the A horizon (mineral topsoil rich in organic matter), the E horizon (eluvial — leached of soluble materials, where present), the B horizon (subsoil where leached materials accumulate), the C horizon (weathered parent material), and the R horizon (bedrock). Soil scientists call this vertical sequence the solum.

Canada has 10 official soil orders in the Canadian System of Soil Classification, which differs from the U.S. Soil Taxonomy but shares the same logic. The most agriculturally important orders are Chernozems (Prairie grassland soils — black, deep, fertile, formed under tall-grass and short-grass prairie); Luvisols (deciduous-forest soils — moderate fertility, found in southern Ontario); Podzols (acidic boreal-forest soils — heavily leached, low fertility, dominant under Canada's vast taiga); and Gleysols (waterlogged soils with poor drainage). Each soil order corresponds to a climate-vegetation regime, which is why the soil map of Canada looks remarkably similar to the biome map. Maintaining soil productivity — through cover cropping, reduced tillage, nutrient management, and crop rotation — is one of the central challenges of sustainable agriculture and a recurring theme in Strands C and D.

  • Pedosphere — the global soil layer; the interface of all four other spheres.
  • Soil horizon (O, A, E, B, C, R) — distinct soil layer with characteristic properties.
  • Soil profile — vertical cross-section showing horizons.
  • CLORPT — Jenny's five soil-forming factors: climate, organisms, relief, parent material, time.
  • Texture — sand–silt–clay percentages (the soil texture triangle).
  • Chernozem — fertile black grassland soil; backbone of Prairie wheat agriculture.
  • Podzol — acidic, leached soil under coniferous forest, low natural fertility.
  • Eluviation / illuviation — leaching of materials downward / accumulation in lower horizons.

Worked Example — Reading a Soil Texture Triangle

A sample is 30% sand, 50% silt, and 20% clay. To classify it, locate 30 on the sand axis (read along lines parallel to the clay-silt side); 50 on the silt axis (parallel to the sand-clay side); and 20 on the clay axis. The three lines intersect in the silt loam region. Silt loams are agriculturally desirable — they hold water like clay but drain like sand. By contrast, a soil that is 70% sand, 15% silt, 15% clay is a sandy loam, which drains too fast to support most crops without irrigation. A soil that is 20% sand, 20% silt, 60% clay is a clay, which holds nutrients well but drains poorly and is hard to till when wet. The same triangle is used worldwide and is one of the most quantitative tools in geography.

  • Calling soil "dirt" — soil is alive, structured, and irreplaceable in human time.
  • Confusing the A horizon (productive topsoil) with the B horizon (subsoil with accumulated material).
  • Assuming all forests have the same soil — boreal podzols are acidic and infertile while deciduous luvisols are richer.
  • Treating soil formation as fast — 1 cm of topsoil takes 100–1,000 years.
  • Forgetting climate is the master variable — the same parent rock yields very different soils in different climates.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.4 — "describe the processes of soil formation and the characteristics of major soil types in Canada and the world."
