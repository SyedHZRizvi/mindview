🌳 2.1 The World's Major Terrestrial Biomes
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A biome is a large region characterized by a distinctive climate and a distinctive assemblage of plants and animals adapted to that climate. Biome geography follows two master variables — mean annual temperature and mean annual precipitation — and a third modifier, seasonality. The German biogeographer Robert Whittaker plotted the world's biomes on a temperature-precipitation diagram in 1975 and demonstrated that the boundaries are not arbitrary: each biome occupies a predictable climate envelope. Where Earth's three-cell atmospheric circulation produces wet conditions (the ITCZ rising limb at 0°, mid-latitude convergence around 60°), forests dominate; where it produces dry conditions (the Hadley descending limb at 30°), deserts dominate; and where temperatures are too low for tree growth (polar latitudes and high elevations), tundra or grassland takes over. The biome map is therefore a derivative product of atmospheric physics — once you understand circulation, you can predict biomes.

The eight major terrestrial biomes Grade 11 students must know are: (1) tropical rainforest (10° N to 10° S; >2000 mm rainfall; ~26 °C year-round; the Amazon, Congo, Indonesian rainforests; highest biodiversity per hectare on Earth, with three or four layers of canopy); (2) tropical savanna (10°–25° N or S; pronounced wet and dry seasons; grassland with scattered trees; African Serengeti, Brazilian cerrado, Australian outback); (3) desert (30° N or S subtropics, also rain-shadow and interior; <250 mm rainfall; specialised plants like cacti and Welwitschia); (4) Mediterranean / chaparral (30°–40° west-coast latitudes; wet mild winters, hot dry summers; California, central Chile, South Africa's Cape, southwestern Australia, Mediterranean Europe); (5) temperate grassland (interior continents 30°–55°; 250–750 mm; cold winters, hot summers; the North American prairie, Eurasian steppe, South American pampas, South African veld); (6) temperate deciduous forest (35°–55° east coasts and Western Europe; 750–1500 mm; four seasons; eastern North America, including southern Ontario, Western Europe, eastern Asia); (7) boreal forest / taiga (50°–65°; short cool summers, long cold winters; conifers dominate; covers most of Canada and Russia); (8) tundra (above 65° N or alpine; <250 mm; permafrost limits root growth; no trees; mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs).

Within Canada the biome system is refined into 15 terrestrial ecozones by the Canadian Council on Ecological Areas — Mixedwood Plains (southern Ontario, the most agriculturally productive), Boreal Shield (the largest, covering the Canadian Shield from Labrador to Saskatchewan), Hudson Plains, Prairies, Montane Cordillera, Pacific Maritime, Boreal Cordillera, Taiga Plains, Taiga Shield, Taiga Cordillera, Atlantic Maritime, Northern Arctic, Southern Arctic, Boreal Plains, and Arctic Cordillera. Ecozones are the basic unit for Canadian environmental reporting and conservation policy — including the 2022 federal commitment to protect 30% of Canada's lands and waters by 2030. Knowing your ecozone is the first step in understanding what local conservation actually means.

  • Biome — large region with characteristic climate, plants, and animals.
  • Ecozone — Canadian-system land region with similar physiography and ecological character.
  • Tropical rainforest — wet equatorial biome, highest biodiversity, multi-layered canopy.
  • Savanna — tropical grassland with scattered trees; pronounced dry season.
  • Boreal forest / taiga — northern coniferous forest covering most of Canada and Russia.
  • Tundra — treeless biome with permafrost in the subsoil.
  • Mediterranean / chaparral — biome of west-coast mid-latitudes with hot dry summers.
  • Mixedwood Plains — southern Ontario's ecozone; deciduous-temperate-forest biome.

Worked Example — Predicting Biome from Climate Data

A weather station reports mean annual temperature 4 °C and mean annual precipitation 600 mm, with a January mean of −15 °C and a July mean of 18 °C. The cold winters with a frost-free season rule out tropical biomes; precipitation is too high for desert; the temperature range is too cold for Mediterranean; the seasonality and precipitation match either temperate grassland or boreal forest. Mean annual temperature 4 °C is low for grassland (typical mean 5–15 °C) and matches the warm end of boreal forest (mean −5 to 5 °C); 600 mm matches both. The station is probably on the prairie–boreal boundary — perhaps near Edmonton (mean 4.2 °C, 482 mm) or Winnipeg (mean 2.9 °C, 521 mm). The boundary is not a sharp line; it shifts north or south by hundreds of kilometres over decades of climate change. This is exactly the kind of zone modern climate science is watching most closely.

  • Calling all forests "the same forest" — boreal, deciduous, and tropical forests differ in soil, species, and seasonality.
  • Confusing biomes (global climate-vegetation categories) with ecosystems (smaller functional units within a biome).
  • Forgetting that aquatic biomes (freshwater, marine) exist in parallel to terrestrial ones.
  • Treating biome boundaries as fixed — climate change is shifting them poleward at roughly 17 km per decade.
  • Assuming the equatorial rainforest belt is continuous — the Amazon and Congo are separated by 8000 km of ocean and savanna.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "describe the climatic, geological, and biological characteristics of major terrestrial biomes and how these characteristics shape the distribution of life on Earth."

⚡ 2.2 Energy Flow & Trophic Levels
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Ecosystems run on energy that originates almost entirely from the sun. Plants and other autotrophs capture solar energy through photosynthesis at an average efficiency of only 1–2% of incoming sunlight — the rest is reflected, transmitted, or lost as heat. The energy captured at this primary production step then flows through the ecosystem in a strict one-way direction: producers → primary consumers (herbivores) → secondary consumers (carnivores that eat herbivores) → tertiary consumers (top carnivores). At each transfer between trophic levels, much of the available energy is lost as metabolic heat, used for movement, or remains undigested. The 10% rule, first quantified by Raymond Lindeman in 1942 in his classic study of Cedar Bog Lake, Minnesota, summarises this brute fact: on average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level becomes the biomass of the next.

The 10% rule has dramatic geographic consequences. Because each successive trophic level supports only one-tenth of the previous, ecosystems can typically only sustain four or five levels — beyond that, there is not enough energy for a viable population. This is why apex predators (tigers, polar bears, killer whales) are always rare and easily endangered: a population of 1 wolf requires a base of perhaps 10,000 kg of plant biomass to support 1,000 kg of small herbivores to support 100 kg of large herbivores to support the wolf. Ecologists picture this as a trophic pyramid with producers as a wide base and apex predators as a narrow tip. Pyramids of biomass (mass at each level), pyramids of numbers (count at each level), and pyramids of energy flow (energy per unit time at each level) tell complementary stories — and biomass pyramids can occasionally be inverted in aquatic ecosystems where rapidly-reproducing phytoplankton support a larger zooplankton mass at any one instant.

Two related concepts are worth mastering. Gross primary productivity (GPP) is the total energy fixed by photosynthesis. Net primary productivity (NPP) is GPP minus the energy plants themselves respire — NPP is what is available to herbivores. NPP varies dramatically by biome: tropical rainforest averages 2200 g C/m²/yr, temperate forest about 1250, savanna 900, prairie 600, tundra 140, desert 90. Coastal marine systems and wetlands rival tropical forests; open ocean is surprisingly low (125 g C/m²/yr) despite covering most of the planet. The geographic distribution of NPP is therefore the geographic distribution of life's energy budget — and the reason the world's most productive agricultural lands, fisheries, and forests cluster in specific predictable regions.

  • Autotroph / heterotroph — producer (makes its own food) / consumer (eats others).
  • Trophic level — position in the food chain; producer (T1), primary consumer (T2), secondary (T3), etc.
  • Food chain / food web — linear sequence / interconnected network of trophic links.
  • 10% rule (Lindeman) — ~10% of energy transfers from one trophic level to the next.
  • GPP / NPP — gross / net primary productivity (NPP = GPP minus plant respiration).
  • Apex predator — top of a food chain; no natural predators of its own.
  • Detritivore / decomposer — feeds on dead organic matter (earthworms, fungi, bacteria).

Worked Example — Applying the 10% Rule

A meadow ecosystem fixes \(P_1 = 10{,}000\) kJ/m²/yr of solar energy at the producer level. How much energy reaches a top carnivore four trophic levels up? Apply the 10% rule three times (T1→T2→T3→T4): \(P_4 = P_1 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 = 10{,}000 \times 0.001 = 10\) kJ/m²/yr. To sustain a 100 kg wolf, with maintenance energy ~10,000 kJ/day = 3.65 × 10⁶ kJ/yr, the wolf needs about \(3.65 \times 10^6 / 10 = 3.65 \times 10^5\) m² = 36.5 hectares of meadow. A wolf pack of six therefore needs at least 220 hectares — and in reality, given hunting losses and seasonal variation, often needs 100+ km² of territory. This is the bottom line on why apex predators are always rare and require vast intact ecosystems to survive.

  • Saying "energy is recycled in an ecosystem" — energy flows one way and is lost as heat; only matter cycles.
  • Confusing food chains (linear) with food webs (networks) — real ecosystems are always webs.
  • Treating the 10% rule as exact — it is a useful average; real efficiencies vary from 5% to 20%.
  • Forgetting decomposers — they are the only group that completes the matter cycle.
  • Assuming high NPP always means high biodiversity — tropical forests have both, but savannas have high biodiversity with moderate NPP.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "explain how energy flows through ecosystems (e.g., food chains, food webs, trophic levels, the 10% rule)."

♻️ 2.3 Nutrient Cycles — Carbon, Nitrogen, Phosphorus
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Whereas energy flows through ecosystems, matter cycles within them. The most important biogeochemical cycles are those of water, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur — though for Grade 11 geography the first three deserve the most attention. Each cycle moves an element through reservoirs in the atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere on time scales ranging from days (atmospheric water) to millions of years (carbonate rock formation). Understanding the cycles is the prerequisite for diagnosing what humans have changed: anthropogenic disruption of the carbon cycle (fossil-fuel burning, deforestation) drives climate change; disruption of the nitrogen cycle (synthetic fertilizer, fossil-fuel combustion) drives eutrophication and acidification; disruption of the phosphorus cycle (fertilizer runoff from agriculture, sewage) is the leading cause of algal blooms in Lake Erie and Lake Winnipeg.

The carbon cycle moves carbon through five major reservoirs: the atmosphere (as CO₂ and CH₄), the oceans (dissolved CO₂ and bicarbonate, plus marine biomass), the terrestrial biosphere (living plants and soil organic matter), fossil fuel deposits (coal, oil, gas, formed over hundreds of millions of years), and sedimentary rocks (limestone, the largest reservoir by mass but with the longest residence time). Photosynthesis takes CO₂ from the atmosphere into plant tissue; respiration and decomposition return it. The natural cycle is balanced; human burning of fossil fuels adds about 10 gigatonnes of carbon per year, of which roughly 45% accumulates in the atmosphere, 30% is absorbed by the oceans, and 25% by the terrestrial biosphere. Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from a stable 280 ppm before 1750 to over 420 ppm today — the geological record going back 800,000 years shows nothing comparable. The Keeling Curve, measured continuously at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958, is the most-cited graph in modern climate science.

The nitrogen cycle is dominated by atmospheric N₂ (78% of air, but unreactive). Specialised nitrogen-fixing bacteria — including free-living soil bacteria and symbionts in legume root nodules — convert N₂ to ammonium (NH₄⁺), which plants can use. Other bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrate (NO₃⁻) in two steps (nitrification), and denitrifying bacteria return nitrate to atmospheric N₂. The Haber-Bosch process, invented in 1909 and industrialised during World War I, fixes atmospheric N₂ into ammonia using high temperature and pressure; it consumes about 1% of global energy supply and now fixes more nitrogen than all natural processes combined. About half of the protein in the average human's body contains nitrogen that passed through a Haber-Bosch reactor — perhaps the most underappreciated technological fact of the modern world. The phosphorus cycle, by contrast, has no significant atmospheric component; phosphorus moves through weathering of rock, biological uptake, and sedimentation, and is the most common limiting nutrient in freshwater systems.

  • Biogeochemical cycle — movement of an element through biosphere, geosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere.
  • Carbon cycle — exchange of carbon among atmosphere, oceans, biosphere, fossil fuels, rocks.
  • Nitrogen fixation — conversion of N₂ to biologically available forms (NH₃, NH₄⁺).
  • Haber-Bosch process — industrial nitrogen fixation; basis of modern synthetic fertilizer.
  • Eutrophication — nutrient over-enrichment (often phosphorus) triggering algal blooms and dead zones.
  • Keeling curve — continuous CO₂ record at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958.
  • Reservoir vs flux — amount stored vs amount moving per unit time.

Worked Example — Estimating Atmospheric CO₂ Lifetime

Total atmospheric CO₂ mass: 3.2 × 10¹⁵ kg = 3,200 Gt CO₂ (≈ 870 Gt C, since CO₂ is 27% carbon by mass). Annual exchange with oceans + biosphere: ≈ 215 Gt CO₂/yr in each direction. Mean residence time \(\tau = V / Q = 3200 / 215 \approx 15\) years. But this is the residence time of a single CO₂ molecule; the residence time of an added pulse of CO₂ (e.g., from burning fossil fuels) is far longer — about 50% removed in a few decades, but a significant fraction lingers for hundreds to thousands of years because the slow sinks (rock weathering, deep-ocean uptake) operate on geological timescales. This distinction explains why even if humans halt all emissions today, atmospheric CO₂ will not return to 280 ppm for at least 10,000 years. Mathematics-of-the-cycle is therefore directly responsible for the long-term irreversibility of anthropogenic climate change.

  • Confusing atmospheric residence time (molecular turnover, fast) with perturbation lifetime (recovery of a pulse, slow).
  • Forgetting that fossil fuels are part of the carbon cycle — just on a 100-million-year timescale.
  • Treating nitrogen as unlimited because air is mostly N₂ — biologically it is often limiting.
  • Saying "the ocean removes our CO₂" without mentioning that this lowers pH (ocean acidification, currently dropping ~0.02 pH per decade).
  • Imagining phosphorus has an atmospheric phase — it does not; cycling is essentially terrestrial.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "describe the major biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water) and explain how they support life on Earth."

🦋 2.4 Biodiversity & Ecosystem Stability
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Biodiversity — the variety of life — is measured at three levels: genetic diversity within species, species diversity within ecosystems, and ecosystem diversity within regions. The 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity defined it formally as "the variability among living organisms from all sources … this includes diversity within species, between species and of ecosystems." Of the roughly 8.7 million eukaryotic species estimated to exist, only about 1.9 million have been formally described — meaning that more than three-quarters of life on Earth lives anonymously in the scientific literature. This taxonomic gap is one of the great limitations on conservation biology: we can't protect what we haven't named.

Biodiversity is not evenly distributed. The Norwegian biogeographer Wim Hagenmaier's 1988 mapping (refined by Conservation International) identified 36 global biodiversity hotspots — regions containing at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and having lost at least 70% of their primary vegetation. The hotspots cover only 2.4% of Earth's land surface but contain 44% of all plant species and 35% of all terrestrial vertebrate species; conserving them is therefore unusually efficient. Canada has no hotspot, but the Carolinian forest of southern Ontario, the Mediterranean-like Garry Oak ecosystems of southern Vancouver Island, and the Old Growth temperate rainforests of the BC coast are all among Canada's most biologically rich and most threatened ecosystems. Globally, tropical rainforests, coral reefs, Mediterranean ecosystems, and tropical mountain regions consistently rank highest.

Biodiversity matters not only intrinsically but functionally. Diverse ecosystems are more resilient — they recover faster from disturbance, resist invasive species better, and provide more reliable ecosystem services (pollination, pest control, water filtration, carbon storage). Robert Paine's 1966 keystone species experiments at Mukkaw Bay, Washington, showed that removing the predatory starfish Pisaster ochraceus caused a 15-species intertidal community to collapse to 8 species within a year, because mussels outcompeted everything else without predator control. The keystone species concept — that some species have disproportionate ecosystem effects — has shaped conservation biology ever since. Canada's threatened keystone species include the Atlantic cod (whose population collapsed 99% by 1992 and triggered ecological cascades through the entire Grand Banks fishery), the grey wolf, the Pacific salmon, and the polar bear. The current global rate of vertebrate extinction is estimated at 100–1000 times the natural background rate, leading many biologists to call our era the sixth mass extinction.

  • Biodiversity — variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels.
  • Species richness vs evenness — number of species present / how evenly individuals are distributed among them.
  • Endemic species — found only in one geographic region.
  • Biodiversity hotspot — region with high endemism and severe habitat loss.
  • Keystone species — species with disproportionate impact relative to its abundance.
  • Invasive species — non-native species that spreads aggressively and disrupts ecosystems.
  • Sixth mass extinction — current human-caused acceleration of extinction rates.

Worked Example — Calculating Simpson's Diversity Index

An old-growth Carolinian forest plot in Point Pelee, Ontario, contains 50 trees: 25 sugar maples, 15 black walnuts, 7 tulip trees, 3 sassafras. Simpson's index is defined as \(D = 1 - \sum (p_i)^2\), where \(p_i\) is the proportion of species \(i\). Compute: \(p_{maple} = 25/50 = 0.5\); \(p_{walnut} = 15/50 = 0.3\); \(p_{tulip} = 7/50 = 0.14\); \(p_{sassafras} = 3/50 = 0.06\). Squares: 0.25, 0.09, 0.0196, 0.0036. Sum: 0.3632. So \(D = 1 - 0.3632 = 0.637\). For comparison, a monoculture wheat field has \(D = 0\), a perfectly even mix of four species has \(D = 0.75\). Simpson's \(D\) ranges from 0 (no diversity) to nearly 1 (very diverse); higher values mean more diverse. The same calculation, applied to a clear-cut version of the same plot regrown as a single species, would give \(D \approx 0\) — and that quantitative loss is what motivates conservation policy.

  • Counting species without weighting by abundance — evenness matters too.
  • Confusing endemic (found only here) with native (originated here, but may live elsewhere too).
  • Treating extinction as natural and therefore unimportant — current rates are 100–1000× background.
  • Calling all imported species "invasive" — only a small fraction become harmful spreaders.
  • Forgetting that biodiversity supports human well-being through ecosystem services worth trillions of dollars annually.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.4 — "analyse the importance of biodiversity to the functioning and resilience of ecosystems, and the causes and consequences of biodiversity loss."

🌱 2.5 Ecological Succession & Disturbance
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Ecosystems are not static — they change through time in fairly predictable sequences called ecological succession. Frederic Clements proposed the foundational succession theory in 1916, viewing the process as a directional movement toward a stable climax community, although Henry Gleason's 1926 critique pointed out that individual species responses to gradients (not communities as superorganisms) drive the process; both views contain truth and are still being negotiated. The two main types are primary succession — on substrates where no soil existed (bare rock exposed by glacial retreat, fresh lava flows, sand dunes) — and secondary succession on disturbed substrates that retain soil (post-fire landscape, abandoned farmland). Primary succession is slow (centuries to millennia for full forest establishment) because soil must first form; secondary succession is faster (decades) because soil and seed banks survive the disturbance.

A classic primary succession sequence at Glacier Bay, Alaska — first studied by William Cooper in the 1920s and refined by F. Stuart Chapin in the 1990s — shows the process step by step. Year 0: a glacier retreats, exposing bare rock and unweathered sediment. Years 0–50: pioneer species — lichens, mosses, fireweed, dryas (a nitrogen-fixing dwarf shrub) — colonize and begin building soil. Years 50–100: alder thickets dominate, fixing more nitrogen and enriching the soil rapidly. Years 100–200: sitka spruce shades out the alder and replaces it. Years 200+: a mixed sitka-hemlock forest with deep soil and complete canopy is the local climax. Each stage modifies conditions so the next stage can take over — a process called facilitation. Other successional dynamics include tolerance (later species succeed despite the earlier ones) and inhibition (early species block later ones until disturbance intervenes).

Disturbance is no longer thought of as the opposite of stability; it is now understood as essential to many ecosystems' health. Boreal forests have evolved with fire on a 50–150 year return interval — jack pine, lodgepole pine, and black spruce have serotinous cones that open only in the heat of a fire, so without fire they cannot regenerate. The 2016 Fort McMurray fire (which burned 590,000 hectares of Alberta boreal forest and forced 88,000 people to evacuate) and the 2023 record Canadian wildfire season (17.4 million hectares burned, nearly 5% of Canada's forested area) are both reminders that fire is a normal part of the boreal disturbance regime — though climate change is now driving it outside historical norms in frequency, intensity, and area. The general principle that "natural" ecosystems include disturbance is one of the deepest lessons of modern ecology and a central concept for evaluating sustainability proposals.

  • Succession — directional change in ecosystem composition over time.
  • Primary succession — succession on substrate that lacks soil (bare rock, fresh lava).
  • Secondary succession — succession on disturbed substrate where soil persists.
  • Pioneer species — first colonizers; tolerant of harsh conditions, often r-selected.
  • Climax community — relatively stable community at end of succession (concept; subject to debate).
  • Facilitation / tolerance / inhibition — three modes by which early species affect later ones.
  • Disturbance regime — characteristic pattern of frequency, intensity, and extent of disturbances.
  • Serotinous cones — fire-adapted cones that open only with heat (boreal pines and spruces).

Worked Example — Reading a Boreal Disturbance Chronosequence

A boreal forest plot was last burned in 2010. By 2026, ground cover is 70% fireweed, 20% bunchberry, 10% blueberry — pioneers and ericaceous shrubs dominate. By 2050, trembling aspen and birch will likely be the dominant 8–12 m canopy; by 2080, jack pine and white spruce will overtop the deciduous trees; by 2150, a mixed black-spruce-jack-pine canopy reaches the locally climax composition. The same logic applied in reverse — looking at a current forest and reading its age — is called chronosequence reading and is one of the most powerful field techniques in modern ecology: substituting space for time, an ecologist can study forests of different ages at the same moment to infer how each one will change in the decades ahead. The Fort McMurray burn scar is now in approximately year 10 of this sequence and is currently dominated by fireweed and aspen suckers.

  • Treating succession as inevitable and directional toward a fixed climax — chance and disturbance both intervene.
  • Calling all fires "destructive" — many ecosystems require fire for renewal.
  • Confusing primary (no soil) with secondary (soil persists) succession.
  • Forgetting that the modern "fire deficit" in some forests (from suppression policy) is itself a form of degradation.
  • Imagining succession as fast — in boreal Canada full recovery to mature forest takes ≥100 years.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.3 — "explain ecological succession (primary and secondary) and the role of disturbance in shaping ecosystems."

🍁 2.6 Canadian Ecozones & the Boreal Forest
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Canada's vastness and latitudinal range produce extraordinary biome diversity. The Canadian ecological-land classification recognises 15 terrestrial ecozones, each with characteristic climate, geology, soils, and vegetation. The largest by far is the Boreal Shield, which sweeps in a giant arc from Newfoundland and Labrador across northern Quebec and Ontario to northern Saskatchewan — about 1.8 million km², roughly one-fifth of Canada. Most students who grow up in Ontario above the 46th parallel live in the Boreal Shield; the rocky outcrops, white pine, balsam fir, black spruce, lakes, and beaver are its signature features. The Mixedwood Plains (southern Ontario and the St. Lawrence Lowlands) is by contrast one of Canada's smallest ecozones — only 175,000 km² — but it is home to half the country's population and most of the country's prime farmland because of its long growing season and fertile post-glacial soils.

The boreal forest globally (across Russia, Canada, Scandinavia, and Alaska) covers about 30% of Earth's forested area and stores roughly 30% of all terrestrial carbon — more than the tropical rainforest by total amount, because much is locked in soil organic matter and permafrost peatlands. Canada's boreal alone contains about 12% of global forest carbon. The species composition is dominated by a handful of cold-tolerant conifers: black spruce, white spruce, jack pine, balsam fir, tamarack, and a few deciduous broadleaf trees (trembling aspen, paper birch, balsam poplar) that pioneer after fire. Wildlife includes moose, woodland caribou, lynx, snowshoe hare, beaver, black bear, wolf, and over 300 bird species — many of them long-distance migrants that breed in the boreal and overwinter as far south as Argentina.

The boreal is changing rapidly. Mean temperatures in Canada have risen roughly 1.7 °C since 1948 (versus ~0.9 °C globally), with northern Canada warming closer to 3 °C. This has produced longer fire seasons, drying of bogs and fens that previously stored vast amounts of carbon, northward range expansion of mountain pine beetle (which has now killed about 50% of British Columbia's lodgepole pine forest, an area larger than Sweden), and melting of discontinuous permafrost across much of the Boreal Shield's northern edge. The future of the boreal — whether it remains a net carbon sink or flips to a net source — is one of the largest single uncertainties in 21st-century climate projections. For Canadian geography students, the boreal is not an abstract biome but a national landscape whose health is tied directly to global climate trajectories. The Indigenous-led Conservation movement, including the Łutsël K'é Dene's establishment of Thaidene Nene National Park Reserve in 2019 (14,000 km² of boreal lakes and forest), is one of the most promising current stewardship models.

  • Ecozone — Canadian-system regional land unit defined by physiography and ecology.
  • Boreal Shield — Canada's largest ecozone; rocky Canadian Shield with coniferous forest and innumerable lakes.
  • Mixedwood Plains — southern Ontario's ecozone; deciduous-temperate forest on fertile post-glacial soils.
  • Carolinian forest — Canada's southernmost forest type; deep southwestern Ontario.
  • Permafrost — ground that has been frozen for at least two consecutive years.
  • Thaidene Nene — Łutsël K'é Dene-led national park reserve in NWT, established 2019.
  • Mountain pine beetle (MPB) — bark beetle whose range has expanded with warming, devastating BC and Alberta pines.

Worked Example — Mapping Population Onto Ecozones

About 40 million people live in Canada. The Mixedwood Plains ecozone (southern Ontario and the St. Lawrence Lowlands) accounts for roughly 1.75% of Canada's land area (175,000 km² of about 10 million km²) but ~50% of its population (≈ 20 million). Population density is thus about 20 million / 175,000 km² ≈ 114 per km². The Boreal Shield, conversely, is ~18% of Canada's area but houses only ~10% of its population, giving roughly 40 million × 0.1 / (10 million × 0.18) ≈ 2 per km². The 57-fold ratio (114 / 2) between Mixedwood Plains and Boreal Shield densities is one of the starkest geographic facts about Canada — and it explains why almost every Canadian policy decision about land use, environmental protection, and Indigenous reconciliation is mediated by the population-vs-area imbalance between the two ecozones.

  • Treating the boreal as a single uniform forest — Canadian boreal varies from dry interior plains to wet coastal taiga.
  • Forgetting that ecozones are scale-dependent — finer subdivisions (ecoregions, ecodistricts) are used for management.
  • Calling the boreal "pristine wilderness" — much has been logged, mined, or fragmented by roads and seismic lines.
  • Assuming permafrost is permanent — discontinuous permafrost across the boreal southern fringe is now thawing rapidly.
  • Underestimating Indigenous land stewardship — IPCAs are a fast-growing share of Canadian conservation.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "describe the major terrestrial ecozones of Canada and explain how each ecozone's physical characteristics shape its ecosystems and human use."
