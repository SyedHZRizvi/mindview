CGW4U — World Issues: A Geographic Analysis, Grade 12 University Preparation

Curriculum Policy Document · Ontario Ministry of Education · The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 9–12: Canadian and World Studies (2015, Revised) · Aligned to Growing Success (2010)

Course Code: CGW4U  |  Credit Value: 1.0  |  Hours: 110  |  Prerequisite: any university or university/college preparation course in Canadian and world studies, English, or social sciences and the humanities

1. Course Description

This course examines the global challenges of creating a sustainable and equitable future, focusing on current issues that exemplify these challenges. Students will investigate a range of issues involving environmental, economic, social, political, and cultural interrelationships, and will explore responses to those issues by individuals, groups, communities, and nations. Throughout the course, students will apply the concepts of geographic thinking, the geographic inquiry process, and spatial-technology skills to investigate and develop informed opinions about world issues.

3. Concepts of Geographic Thinking

ConceptApplication in CGW4U
Spatial significanceWhy a place, region, or environmental feature matters — for example, why the Sahel matters in food-security debates or why the Strait of Hormuz matters geopolitically.
Patterns and trendsPopulation pyramids, climate trends, trade flows, conflict density maps — identifying recurring forms across time and space.
InterrelationshipsLinking deforestation in the Amazon to global carbon cycles, or linking conflict minerals to consumer electronics in Canada.
Geographic perspectiveExamining issues through environmental, economic, political, social, and cultural lenses to weigh evidence and trade-offs.

4. Big Ideas (by Strand)

  • Geographic Inquiry & Skill Development (A): The geographic inquiry process and the four concepts of geographic thinking are used to investigate world issues.
  • Quality of Life (B): Quality of life varies spatially across the world and is shaped by economic, environmental, and political factors that can be measured with composite indicators.
  • Sustainability & Stewardship (C): Environmental sustainability requires informed individual and collective action that balances human needs with ecosystem health.
  • Conflict & Cooperation (D): Geographic factors influence the emergence of conflict, and international cooperation is essential to global stability and human security.
  • Global Connections (E): Economic, political, social, and cultural globalization links places and peoples in ways that create both opportunity and inequality.

5. Strand A — Geographic Inquiry & Skill Development (embedded across all strands)

A1. Geographic Inquiry

  • A1.1 formulate questions to guide investigations into world issues from a geographic perspective
  • A1.2 gather and organize relevant information from a variety of primary and secondary sources, including statistical, cartographic, and qualitative data
  • A1.3 analyse and interpret information, using concepts of geographic thinking
  • A1.4 evaluate evidence and information and draw conclusions about world issues
  • A1.5 communicate judgements, decisions, and conclusions using cartographic, graphic, written, and digital forms
  • A1.6 use geographic perspectives to make informed judgements and decisions

A2. Developing Transferable Skills

  • A2.1 apply geospatial technologies (GIS, remote sensing, online mapping platforms) to analyse issues
  • A2.2 apply concepts and skills learned in the course to other disciplines and to everyday life
  • A2.3 identify career pathways in geography and related fields

Career Connections

Urban planner, GIS analyst, environmental policy advisor, foreign-service officer, humanitarian worker, climate scientist, demographer, journalist, international development consultant, supply-chain analyst, public-health geographer, conservation officer.

6. Strand B — Quality of Life (Chapter 1)

Overall Expectations

  • B1. analyse social, economic, and political factors that affect quality of life in different regions of the world;
  • B2. analyse interrelationships between human well-being and economic, environmental, social, and political conditions in different parts of the world;
  • B3. demonstrate an understanding of geographic factors that contribute to variations in quality of life globally.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • B1.1 analyse the use of composite indicators (Human Development Index, Gender Inequality Index, Happy Planet Index) to compare quality of life across countries;
  • B1.4 explain the contribution of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and related international agreements to improving quality of life;
  • B2.2 analyse interrelationships between poverty, food security, education, and access to clean water;
  • B2.3 explain the demographic transition model and its application to countries at different levels of development;
  • B3.2 explain the geographic factors that influence the distribution of wealth and well-being globally (resource endowment, colonial legacy, governance, gender equity).
Issue-rich focus: maternal mortality in low-HDI countries; gender literacy gaps; urban-rural well-being divides; Canada's Indigenous-community well-being gap.

7. Strand C — Sustainability & Stewardship (Chapter 2)

Overall Expectations

  • C1. analyse impacts of human activities on natural systems, and assess the sustainability of selected ways of life;
  • C2. analyse responses by governments, organizations, and individuals to environmental issues;
  • C3. demonstrate an understanding of the role of natural systems and biodiversity in maintaining a healthy planet.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • C1.1 analyse the causes and consequences of anthropogenic climate change (greenhouse-gas emissions, deforestation, land-use change);
  • C1.3 calculate and interpret ecological footprints and identify strategies to reduce them;
  • C2.1 analyse the role of the IPCC, UNFCCC, Paris Agreement, and Canada's emissions reduction plan in addressing climate change;
  • C2.4 evaluate the effectiveness of policy tools such as carbon pricing, renewable-energy subsidies, and protected-area designation;
  • C3.3 explain the carbon cycle, the water cycle, and feedback loops (e.g., Arctic ice-albedo feedback, permafrost methane release).
Issue-rich focus: Canada's net-zero 2050 target; the Athabasca oil sands; small island developing states (SIDS) and sea-level rise; the Indigenous-led Land Back movement.

8. Strand D — Conflict & Cooperation (Chapter 3)

Overall Expectations

  • D1. analyse geographic factors that contribute to selected current and historical conflicts;
  • D2. analyse the role of various organizations and individuals in promoting cooperation, peace, and human security globally;
  • D3. demonstrate an understanding of the impact of geographic factors on global conflict and cooperation.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • D1.1 analyse the geographic factors (resources, borders, ethnic distribution, climate stress) contributing to selected conflicts;
  • D1.4 analyse the impact of conflict on refugees, internally displaced persons, and host communities (e.g., Syria, South Sudan, Ukraine, Myanmar);
  • D2.1 explain the roles of the United Nations, UN Security Council, peacekeeping forces, and NATO in maintaining international peace;
  • D2.3 evaluate the effectiveness of humanitarian organizations (UNHCR, ICRC, MSF, WFP) in responding to crises;
  • D3.2 explain how Canada contributes to global peacekeeping, multilateral diplomacy, and the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine.
Issue-rich focus: water sharing in the Nile basin; the Russia–Ukraine war; the rules-based international order; conflict minerals from the DRC; the Rohingya crisis.

9. Strand E — Global Connections (Chapter 4)

Overall Expectations

  • E1. analyse interrelationships between Canada and selected other parts of the world;
  • E2. analyse causes and consequences of globalization;
  • E3. demonstrate an understanding of geographic factors affecting global economic, social, and political connections.

Specific Expectations (selected)

  • E1.2 analyse the influence of trade agreements (CUSMA/USMCA, CETA, CPTPP, WTO rules) on the Canadian economy and labour market;
  • E2.1 analyse the consequences of economic globalization for workers, consumers, and the environment in different parts of the world;
  • E2.3 analyse the roles of transnational corporations, the IMF, and the World Bank in shaping global development;
  • E2.5 evaluate the geographic distribution of supply chains for selected goods (smartphones, fast fashion, lithium, semiconductors);
  • E3.4 explain how digital connectivity, remittances, and migration link distant places.
Issue-rich focus: the EV battery supply chain and Canadian critical minerals; Bangladesh garment industry; semiconductor geopolitics (Taiwan, the U.S. CHIPS Act); migrant remittances to the Philippines and India.

12. Evaluation Policy (70/30)

  • Term Work — 70% of final mark, comprised of unit tests, case-study reports, performance tasks, and assignments. Most-recent and most-consistent evidence is used.
  • Final Evaluation — 30% of final mark, comprised of a culminating geographic inquiry and/or final examination administered near the end of the course.

All four Achievement Chart categories are addressed throughout, in both Term Work and Final Evaluation.

13. Assessment Practices

  • Assessment AS Learning — student-directed self-assessment (practice quizzes, peer feedback, reflection).
  • Assessment FOR Learning — diagnostic, descriptive teacher feedback (no mark); informs instruction.
  • Assessment OF Learning — graded summative (chapter tests, culminating inquiry, final exam).

14. Learning Skills & Work Habits

Reported separately on the report card: responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, self-regulation.
