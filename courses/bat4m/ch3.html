3.1 — Horizontal & Vertical Analysis
Year-over-year percentage changes; common-size income statement and balance sheet; trend lines and benchmarking.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Raw financial statements are difficult to interpret in isolation. Was net income of \$ 4.2 million last year a good result? Compared to what? Two foundational analytical techniques convert raw numbers into comparable ones. Horizontal analysis (also called trend analysis) computes the dollar and percentage change from one year to the next: \( \% \Delta = \dfrac{\text{Current} - \text{Base}}{\text{Base}} \times 100\% \). It answers the question "how did the company change?" — sales up 12%, cost of goods up 18%, net income down 5%. A horizontal analysis over several years reveals trends that are invisible in a single year.

Vertical analysis (also called common-size analysis) expresses every line on a single statement as a percentage of a benchmark: every income-statement item as a percentage of net sales, and every balance-sheet item as a percentage of total assets. The result is a statement where the dollar amounts are gone and only percentages remain. Vertical analysis makes companies of vastly different size directly comparable: a \$ 5-billion grocery chain and a \$ 50-million one can both be described as having "cost of goods sold = 75% of net sales." The technique also exposes structural shifts within a single firm — for example, if interest expense was 2% of sales last year and 7% this year, leverage has grown sharply.

The two techniques are complementary. Horizontal analysis spots changes in absolute level; vertical analysis spots changes in the mix. Together they form the basic toolkit a banker, investor, or manager uses before computing any ratios. Always be alert to the base-year problem: when the base year had an unusually small or negative number, the percentage change can be wildly misleading.

  • Horizontal analysis: \( \dfrac{\text{Year 2} - \text{Year 1}}{\text{Year 1}} \times 100\% \) — measures change over time.
  • Vertical analysis (common-size): every item ÷ a base (Net Sales for income statement, Total Assets for balance sheet).
  • Trend analysis: horizontal analysis extended to three or more years.
  • Benchmark: the value used for comparison — prior year, industry average, or a peer firm.
  • Base-year problem: distortion of percentage change when the base is near zero.

Example: Selected items from Lakeshore Ltd.

Net Sales 2025 \$ 800,000; 2026 \$ 920,000. Cost of Goods Sold 2025 \$ 480,000; 2026 \$ 570,400. Net Income 2025 \$ 64,000; 2026 \$ 73,600. Total Assets at year-end 2026 \$ 1,200,000.

Horizontal analysis (2026 vs 2025):

Sales: \( (920{,}000 - 800{,}000)/800{,}000 = 15\% \) increase.

COGS: \( (570{,}400 - 480{,}000)/480{,}000 = 18.83\% \) increase.

Net Income: \( (73{,}600 - 64{,}000)/64{,}000 = 15\% \) increase.

Vertical (common-size) income statement 2026:

COGS / Sales = \( 570{,}400 / 920{,}000 = 62.0\% \). Gross profit margin = 38%. Net income / Sales = \( 73{,}600 / 920{,}000 = 8.0\% \). Cost of goods grew faster than sales, but selling expenses must have grown more slowly because the net-income percentage still matched sales growth.

  • Reporting a horizontal percentage when the base year is zero or negative — the result is undefined or misleading.
  • Using total revenue (not net sales) as the income-statement common-size base.
  • Drawing causal conclusions from a single horizontal comparison without checking the rest of the income statement.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.1 — "conduct horizontal analysis (year-over-year change, both dollar amount and percentage) on the income statement and balance sheet."

3.2 — Liquidity Ratios
Working capital, current ratio, quick ratio; receivables and inventory turnover; days' sales metrics.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Liquidity measures a company's ability to pay its bills as they come due, within the next twelve months. The simplest indicator is working capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities, expressed in dollars. Positive working capital means the firm has more short-term resources than short-term obligations. Because dollar amounts depend on firm size, the current ratio \( = \dfrac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} \) is more useful for comparison: a ratio of 2 means \$ 2 of current assets for every \$ 1 of current liabilities. Conventional wisdom calls 1.5 to 2.0 healthy for most industries, although a grocery chain might be perfectly safe at 0.9 because its inventory turns over weekly.

The current ratio mixes very liquid assets (cash) with less-liquid ones (inventory, prepaid expenses). The quick ratio (or acid-test) tightens the test: \( \text{Quick Ratio} = \dfrac{\text{Cash} + \text{Short-Term Investments} + \text{AR}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} \). Inventory and prepaid expenses are excluded. A quick ratio above 1.0 typically signals that obligations can be met without selling inventory, which matters during periods of slow sales.

The remaining liquidity ratios measure how fast the current assets convert to cash. Receivables turnover \( = \dfrac{\text{Net Credit Sales}}{\text{Average AR}} \) shows how many times the receivables balance is collected per year; days' sales in receivables \( = 365 / \text{Receivables Turnover} \) translates that into days. Similarly, inventory turnover \( = \dfrac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average Inventory}} \) shows how many times stock is sold per year, and days' sales in inventory \( = 365 / \text{Inventory Turnover} \) gives the same in days. The sum (days' inventory + days' AR) approximates the operating cycle: how long cash is tied up before being collected.

  • Working capital: CA − CL (dollar amount).
  • Current ratio: CA / CL (industry benchmark ≈ 1.5–2).
  • Quick ratio: (Cash + ST Inv. + AR) / CL — excludes inventory.
  • Receivables turnover: Net Credit Sales / Avg AR; days = 365 / turnover.
  • Inventory turnover: COGS / Avg Inventory; days = 365 / turnover.

Example: Selected balances of Maple Co.

Cash \$ 30,000; AR (net) \$ 80,000; Inventory \$ 110,000; Prepaid \$ 5,000; Current Liabilities \$ 90,000; Net Credit Sales \$ 480,000; COGS \$ 290,000; Average AR \$ 75,000; Average Inventory \$ 100,000.

Working capital \( = (30{,}000 + 80{,}000 + 110{,}000 + 5{,}000) - 90{,}000 = 225{,}000 - 90{,}000 = \$ 135{,}000 \).

Current ratio \( = 225{,}000 / 90{,}000 = 2.50 \). Quick ratio \( = (30{,}000 + 80{,}000) / 90{,}000 = 1.22 \).

Receivables turnover \( = 480{,}000 / 75{,}000 = 6.4 \) times. Days' AR \( = 365 / 6.4 \approx 57 \) days.

Inventory turnover \( = 290{,}000 / 100{,}000 = 2.9 \) times. Days' inventory \( = 365 / 2.9 \approx 126 \) days.

Interpretation: ratios indicate healthy liquidity, but a 126-day stock cycle suggests slow-moving inventory worth investigating.

  • Using ending balance instead of average for AR or inventory turnover — averages smooth seasonal fluctuations.
  • Including inventory in the quick ratio. By definition it is excluded.
  • Forgetting that "days" comes from 365 ÷ turnover, not turnover ÷ 365.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "compute and interpret liquidity ratios: current ratio, quick (acid-test) ratio, working capital, receivables turnover, days' sales in receivables, inventory turnover, days' sales in inventory."

3.3 — Solvency (Long-Term) Ratios
Debt-to-assets, debt-to-equity, times-interest-earned; financial leverage and the risk-return trade-off.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Solvency measures a company's ability to meet its long-term obligations — bonds, mortgages, lease commitments. While liquidity focuses on the next 12 months, solvency looks at whether the firm can stay in business over many years. The first solvency ratio is debt-to-assets \( = \dfrac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}} \), expressed as a percentage. A debt-to-assets ratio of 60% means creditors finance 60% of the firm's assets and shareholders the remaining 40%. The matching identity, debt-to-equity \( = \dfrac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Equity}} \), expresses leverage in a different way: a ratio of 1.5 means \$ 1.50 of debt for every \$ 1 of equity.

Debt is not bad in itself. Borrowing at 6% to earn 12% on operations creates financial leverage that magnifies the shareholder return. But it also magnifies losses, and at some level the interest burden becomes unsustainable. The times-interest-earned ratio (also called interest coverage) \( = \dfrac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense}} \), where EBIT is earnings before interest and tax, measures how many times current operating profit covers current interest. A ratio of 5 means the company could lose 80% of its operating profit before being unable to pay interest. Banks typically require a covenant of at least 2.5 before extending term financing.

Solvency analysis is industry-sensitive. Utilities and real-estate operators run at debt-to-assets of 60–80% because their cash flows are stable; technology firms typically stay below 30% because their cash flows are volatile. Always compare a firm to its industry peers before judging.

  • Debt-to-assets: Total Liabilities / Total Assets — percentage of assets financed by creditors.
  • Debt-to-equity: Total Liabilities / Total Equity — leverage relative to owners.
  • Times-interest-earned: EBIT / Interest Expense — coverage of interest from operations.
  • Financial leverage: the use of debt to magnify shareholder returns.
  • EBIT: Earnings Before Interest and Tax — net income with interest and tax added back.

Example: Riverbend Inc.

Total Assets \$ 1,200,000; Total Liabilities \$ 720,000; Total Equity \$ 480,000; Net Income \$ 80,000; Income Tax Expense \$ 32,000; Interest Expense \$ 28,000.

Debt-to-assets \( = 720{,}000 / 1{,}200{,}000 = 60\% \). Debt-to-equity \( = 720{,}000 / 480{,}000 = 1.50 \).

EBIT \( = \text{Net Income} + \text{Tax} + \text{Interest} = 80{,}000 + 32{,}000 + 28{,}000 = \$ 140{,}000 \).

Times-interest-earned \( = 140{,}000 / 28{,}000 = 5.0 \) times.

Interpretation: leverage is moderate; the company comfortably covers interest five times over with operating profit.

  • Using Net Income (not EBIT) in the interest-coverage numerator. Interest must be added back so the same profit pays both interest and tax.
  • Comparing a manufacturer to a utility on debt-to-assets without industry context.
  • Treating zero debt as ideal — a moderate, well-priced debt level usually improves ROE.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "compute and interpret solvency ratios: debt-to-assets, debt-to-equity, times-interest-earned (interest coverage)."

3.4 — Profitability Ratios
Gross profit margin, profit margin, return on assets, return on equity, earnings per share.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Profitability ratios measure how effectively management converts revenue and assets into net income for shareholders. Four core ratios appear on every standard analysis. Gross-profit margin \( = \dfrac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Net Sales}} \) measures the markup above product cost; for a typical retailer this is 25–40%. Profit margin \( = \dfrac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Net Sales}} \) measures what is left after every operating, financing, and tax expense; software firms commonly exceed 20%, while grocery chains may earn 2%.

Return on assets (ROA) \( = \dfrac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Average Total Assets}} \) measures the productivity of the asset base, abstracting from how it is financed. Return on equity (ROE) \( = \dfrac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Average Common Equity}} \) measures the return to ordinary shareholders. ROE is the bottom-line measure for owners, and its decomposition (the DuPont identity) — ROE = profit margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier — shows the three levers a CEO can pull to improve it.

For publicly traded corporations, earnings per share (EPS) \( = \dfrac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted Avg Common Shares Outstanding}} \) is the most widely watched profitability metric. EPS is required by IFRS to be reported on the face of the income statement and is used in the price-to-earnings ratio. Trends in EPS are watched carefully by investors because a falling EPS suggests share dilution or earnings deterioration.

  • Gross profit margin: Gross Profit / Net Sales — markup over product cost.
  • Profit margin: Net Income / Net Sales — bottom-line cents per dollar of sales.
  • ROA: Net Income / Avg Total Assets — productivity of the entire asset base.
  • ROE: (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) / Avg Common Equity — owners' return.
  • EPS: (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) / Weighted Avg Common Shares — per-share earnings.

Example: Selected data for Lakeshore Ltd.

Net Sales \$ 920,000; Gross Profit \$ 350,000; Net Income \$ 73,600; Preferred Dividends \$ 4,000; Average Total Assets \$ 1,100,000; Average Common Equity \$ 450,000; Weighted-average common shares 100,000.

Gross-profit margin \( = 350{,}000 / 920{,}000 = 38\% \). Profit margin \( = 73{,}600 / 920{,}000 = 8\% \).

ROA \( = 73{,}600 / 1{,}100{,}000 = 6.7\% \).

ROE \( = (73{,}600 - 4{,}000) / 450{,}000 = 69{,}600 / 450{,}000 = 15.5\% \).

EPS \( = 69{,}600 / 100{,}000 = \$ 0.70 \) per share.

Interpretation: Lakeshore earns 8 cents on every dollar of sales and generates a 15.5% return for common shareholders — a respectable result for a mid-market firm.

  • Including preferred dividends in the ROE and EPS numerators — they belong to preferred shareholders, not common.
  • Using ending balances in the denominator instead of averages — averages prevent year-end window dressing from distorting the ratio.
  • Comparing EPS levels across firms with different share counts — the ratio is meaningful per firm, but cross-firm comparison requires P/E or another scaled metric.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.3 — "compute and interpret profitability ratios: gross-profit margin, profit margin, return on assets (ROA), return on equity (ROE), earnings per share (EPS)."

3.5 — Market Ratios & Investment Decision-Making
Price-to-earnings, dividend yield, dividend payout; using ratios to recommend invest, hold, or sell.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Market ratios use the price of the share — set by the stock market — in addition to data from the financial statements. They tell us what investors are willing to pay for a unit of earnings or dividends and so reflect the market's expectations about future performance. The most-watched market ratio is price-to-earnings (P/E) \( = \dfrac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{EPS}} \). A P/E of 25 means investors are paying \$ 25 today for every \$ 1 of last year's earnings, implying they expect future earnings to grow. A low P/E may signal a bargain — or a value trap if earnings are deteriorating.

For income-focused investors, dividend yield \( = \dfrac{\text{Annual Dividend per Share}}{\text{Market Price per Share}} \) gives the cash return on the share price; mature utilities and banks routinely yield 4–6%, while growth stocks yield 0–2%. The dividend payout ratio \( = \dfrac{\text{Dividends per Share}}{\text{EPS}} \) shows what fraction of earnings is being returned to shareholders rather than reinvested. A 100% payout leaves nothing for growth; a 30% payout leaves 70% inside the firm to be reinvested. Sustainable payouts in stable industries are typically 40–70%.

Putting it all together: an investment decision is rarely justified by a single ratio. Strong liquidity, conservative solvency, growing profitability, and a reasonable P/E are all desirable. An investor (or BAT4M student writing a recommendation memo) presents three to five ratios that paint a consistent picture, names a comparison benchmark, and finishes with a clear recommendation — invest, hold, or sell — accompanied by the chief risks that could change that view.

  • Price-to-earnings (P/E): Market Price / EPS — market's premium over current earnings.
  • Dividend yield: DPS / Market Price — cash income relative to current price.
  • Dividend payout: DPS / EPS — fraction of earnings paid as dividends.
  • Trailing vs forward P/E: last 12 months' EPS vs expected next 12 months'.
  • Recommendation memo: a short report concluding invest / hold / sell with supporting ratios.

Example: Aurora Corp. EPS \$ 2.40; share price \$ 36; annual dividend \$ 1.20.

P/E \( = 36 / 2.40 = 15.0 \). Dividend yield \( = 1.20 / 36 = 3.33\% \). Dividend payout \( = 1.20 / 2.40 = 50\% \).

Compared with industry averages — P/E 18, yield 2.0%, payout 35% — Aurora is priced more conservatively than peers (lower P/E), pays a higher cash yield, and retains less earnings for growth. For an income investor this is attractive; for a growth investor it may be a sign of a maturing business.

Recommendation: Hold. Strong cash distribution but limited growth potential — buy more only if cash yield is the priority.

  • Mixing up dividend yield (price-based) with dividend payout (earnings-based).
  • Drawing a recommendation from a single ratio without context.
  • Comparing P/E ratios across very different industries — software P/E ≠ utility P/E.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.4 — "compute and interpret market ratios: price-to-earnings (P/E), dividend yield, dividend payout."

3.6 — Limitations of Ratio Analysis
Historical-cost values, accounting-policy differences, window dressing, industry differences, qualitative factors.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Ratios are useful precisely because they reduce a complicated set of statements to a few numbers — but the same simplification can mask real problems. Critical users of financial-statement analysis know its limitations. Historical cost. Most assets are reported at the cost paid years ago, not at today's value. A factory bought in 1990 for \$ 2 million may be worth \$ 20 million in 2026. ROA divided by a 1990 dollar denominator misleads. Accounting-policy differences. Two companies can be identical in operations and yet report different income because one uses FIFO and the other weighted-average, or one uses straight-line amortization and the other declining-balance.

Window dressing. Management may time transactions around year end to flatter the ratios — paying suppliers a day before year end to improve the current ratio, or delaying a large purchase by one day. Comparing several years smooths these effects. Industry differences. Capital intensity, customer terms, and gross margins vary dramatically across sectors. A 1.0 current ratio at a grocery chain is comfortable; the same ratio at a heavy-equipment manufacturer is alarming. Always benchmark within an industry, not across.

Qualitative factors. The strongest set of ratios cannot reveal the quality of the management team, the strength of a brand, the pending lawsuit, or the regulatory change about to be announced. A complete analysis combines quantitative ratios with the management's discussion and analysis (MD&A), the auditor's report, the notes to the financial statements, and recent news. Ratios are a first cut — never the last word.

  • Historical cost: assets at original cost, not current value.
  • Accounting-policy variation: FIFO vs weighted-average, straight-line vs declining-balance, etc.
  • Window dressing: timing transactions around year end to flatter the ratios.
  • Industry effects: sectors have characteristic ratio ranges.
  • Qualitative factors: management quality, brand, regulatory environment.

Example: Two competing retailers — Apex and Beta — both report a current ratio of 2.0. Closer inspection reveals: Apex's inventory is 70% of current assets and turns over 2.0 times per year; Beta's inventory is 30% of current assets and turns 8.0 times.

Although the current ratios are identical, Apex's "liquidity" includes slow-moving stock that may not convert easily. Beta's quick ratio is also stronger. A surface-level comparison of current ratios would miss this — a deeper look at inventory turnover and the composition of current assets is necessary. Lesson: never stop at one ratio.

  • Treating a single year's ratio as definitive. Always look at three to five years.
  • Comparing companies without adjusting for the accounting-policy differences disclosed in the notes.
  • Forgetting that off-balance-sheet obligations (operating leases pre-IFRS 16, contingent liabilities) are not in the ratios.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.2 — "identify limitations of ratio analysis (historical cost, accounting-policy differences, window dressing, industry differences, non-financial factors)."
