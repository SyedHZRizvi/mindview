1.1 — Cash Controls & Bank Reconciliation
Internal controls over cash; preparing a bank reconciliation; outstanding cheques, deposits in transit, NSF cheques, service charges.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Cash is the most liquid asset and therefore the most tempting target for theft and error. Strong internal control over cash rests on a small number of bedrock principles: every cash receipt should be deposited intact and promptly; every cash disbursement (except very small ones from petty cash) should be made by cheque or electronic transfer; and the duties of authorizing, custody, recording, and reconciling cash must be performed by different employees. These controls produce two independent records of cash — the company's own general ledger Cash account and the monthly bank statement — and the difference between the two is reconciled at the end of every month.

A bank reconciliation is the formal document that explains why those two records disagree on the bank-statement date. Reconciling items fall into two buckets. Bank-side adjustments are transactions the company has already recorded but the bank has not: outstanding cheques (cheques written but not yet cleared) are subtracted from the bank's balance, while deposits in transit (deposits made but not yet posted) are added. Book-side adjustments are transactions the bank has recorded but the company has not: NSF cheques from customers (subtract), bank service charges and interest charges (subtract), interest earned and direct customer deposits (add). After both sides are adjusted, the reconciled cash balance must equal — to the penny — on both columns. The book-side adjustments are then formally journalized so the general ledger reflects them; the bank-side items require no entry because the bank will catch up next month.

  • Internal control: the policies and procedures designed to safeguard assets, ensure accurate records, promote efficiency, and encourage adherence to policy.
  • Outstanding cheque: a cheque written by the company but not yet presented to the bank for payment.
  • Deposit in transit: a deposit recorded in the company's books but not yet posted by the bank (usually a deposit on the last day of the month).
  • NSF cheque: "Not Sufficient Funds" — a customer cheque returned because the customer's account lacked funds; the company must reverse the original credit to Cash.
  • Petty cash: a small fund of currency for minor disbursements, replenished on a fixed-imprest basis.

Example: Bank reconciliation for Maple Co. at March 31.

Ledger Cash balance: \$ 8,420. Bank statement balance: \$ 9,100. Reconciling items: outstanding cheques \$ 1,250; deposit in transit \$ 600; NSF cheque from customer Lee \$ 75; bank service charge \$ 15; interest earned \$ 20.

Bank side: \( 9{,}100 + 600 - 1{,}250 = \$ 8{,}450 \).

Book side: \( 8{,}420 - 75 - 15 + 20 = \$ 8{,}350 \). The sides do not match, so an error must exist. Re-checking the deposit slip reveals that the cash receipts journal recorded a \$ 200 deposit as \$ 100. Correcting it: \( 8{,}350 + 100 = \$ 8{,}450 \) — now both sides equal \$ 8,450. The book-side journal entry: debit Cash \$ 105 net (\(+100+20-75-15\)) with a debit/credit to each affected account.

  • Adjusting the wrong side: NSF cheques and service charges adjust the book balance because the company didn't know about them; outstanding cheques adjust the bank balance.
  • Treating the reconciliation as optional. Every month it must balance to the penny — otherwise a hidden error or fraud is present.
  • Forgetting to record the book-side adjustments as journal entries afterward.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A1.2 — "demonstrate an understanding of internal controls over cash and prepare a bank reconciliation statement, identifying outstanding cheques, deposits in transit, NSF cheques, and bank service charges."

1.2 — Accounts Receivable & Bad Debts
Allowance method vs direct write-off; percentage of sales vs aging of receivables; write-offs and subsequent recoveries.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

When a business sells on credit, some customers will not pay. The matching principle requires that the expense of these uncollectible accounts be recognized in the period of the sale, not in some later period when the company finally gives up trying to collect. That is why Canadian GAAP (and IFRS) require the allowance method: at the end of each period, the company estimates the dollar amount of receivables it does not expect to collect and records a Bad Debt Expense with a corresponding credit to a contra-asset called Allowance for Doubtful Accounts. The receivables on the balance sheet are then reported at net realizable value = Accounts Receivable − Allowance.

Two estimation techniques are common. The percentage-of-sales method (income-statement approach) multiplies a historical bad-debt rate by credit sales for the period; the result is the entry — the existing allowance balance is ignored. The aging-of-receivables method (balance-sheet approach) sorts receivables by how many days past due and multiplies each bracket by a higher rate; the result is the target ending allowance, and the journal entry brings the allowance up (or down) to that target. The direct write-off method, by contrast, simply expenses bad debts when an account is finally judged uncollectible — it is not GAAP because it violates matching, but it may be used by very small businesses or when amounts are immaterial.

When a specific account is identified as uncollectible (after collection efforts have failed), the entry is to debit Allowance for Doubtful Accounts and credit Accounts Receivable (no effect on income — that expense was recognized in an earlier period). If the customer later pays unexpectedly, the original write-off is reversed (debit AR, credit Allowance) and then the cash receipt is recorded normally.

  • Allowance method: GAAP-compliant; estimates bad debts in the period of sale.
  • Direct write-off: recognizes bad debts only when actually identified; not GAAP for material amounts.
  • Allowance for Doubtful Accounts: a contra-asset that reduces Accounts Receivable to net realizable value.
  • Net realizable value (NRV): the cash the company expects ultimately to collect.
  • Aging schedule: a table sorting receivables by days past due (Current, 1–30, 31–60, 61–90, 90+).

Example: Aging method. At Dec 31, AR = \$ 200,000 with the following aging:

Current \$ 140,000 (1% uncollectible); 1–30 days \$ 40,000 (3%); 31–60 \$ 12,000 (10%); 60+ \$ 8,000 (35%).

Target allowance \( = 140{,}000(0.01) + 40{,}000(0.03) + 12{,}000(0.10) + 8{,}000(0.35) \) \( = 1{,}400 + 1{,}200 + 1{,}200 + 2{,}800 = \$ 6{,}600 \).

If the unadjusted allowance is a credit of \$ 1,000, the year-end entry is: debit Bad Debt Expense \$ 5,600, credit Allowance for Doubtful Accounts \$ 5,600 (bringing the allowance from \$ 1,000 to \$ 6,600). The balance sheet shows AR \$ 200,000 less Allowance \$ 6,600 = NRV \$ 193,400.

  • Forgetting that the percentage-of-sales method adds the computed expense to whatever balance the Allowance already has, whereas aging targets the ending Allowance balance.
  • Crediting AR (instead of the Allowance) on year-end estimate entries — the specific customer is not yet known.
  • Treating the write-off entry as an expense; the expense was recorded earlier when the allowance was set up.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "estimate uncollectible accounts using percentage of sales and aging of receivables; compare the two methods."

1.3 — Short-Term Investments
Accounting for short-term marketable securities at fair value through profit or loss; recording unrealized and realized gains/losses; dividend and interest income.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Most businesses hold a buffer of cash beyond their day-to-day needs. Rather than leave that cash idle, treasurers invest it in short-term investments — money-market funds, treasury bills, term deposits, and publicly traded shares — that can be liquidated within a year. Under IFRS (and ASPE), securities the company intends to trade are classified as fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL): each balance-sheet date they are re-measured to their market price, and the gain or loss is reported on the income statement, even though no sale has occurred. This treatment is sometimes called "marking to market" and is the modern replacement for the older "lower of cost or market" rule that applied to all short-term investments before 2011.

The journal entries follow a simple pattern. At purchase: debit Short-Term Investment for the total cost (including commissions); credit Cash. During the holding period: dividend or interest income is debited to Cash and credited to Dividend/Interest Revenue. At year end: if the market value differs from the carrying amount, an unrealized gain or loss is recorded — debit Investment / credit Unrealized Gain when the price rises, and the reverse when it falls — and the investment is brought to fair value. At sale: Cash is debited for proceeds; Investment is credited at its carrying value; and the difference is recorded as a realized gain or loss.

One subtle point: when an investment is revalued in one period and sold the next, the previously recorded unrealized gain/loss has already affected income. To avoid double counting, the realized gain/loss at sale is computed by comparing the cash proceeds to the most recent carrying value, not the original cost.

  • Short-term investment: a security expected to be sold within one year; reported under current assets.
  • Fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL): investments re-measured to market each period, with gains/losses on the income statement.
  • Unrealized gain/loss: the change in fair value on a security that has not been sold.
  • Realized gain/loss: the difference between cash proceeds and carrying value at the time of sale.
  • Dividend revenue: earnings from holding equity investments; recognized when declared.

Example: Aurora Corp buys 1,000 shares of TD Bank at \$ 80/share, paying a \$ 50 commission. Cash dividends of \$ 0.90/share are received during the year. At year end, the shares trade at \$ 86. The shares are sold the following March for \$ 84/share (commission \$ 50).

Purchase: debit Short-Term Investment \$ 80,050, credit Cash \$ 80,050.

Dividend: \( 1{,}000 \times 0.90 = \$ 900 \) — debit Cash \$ 900, credit Dividend Revenue \$ 900.

Year-end fair value adjustment: market value \( = 1{,}000 \times 86 = \$ 86{,}000 \). Unrealized gain \( = 86{,}000 - 80{,}050 = \$ 5{,}950 \). Debit Short-Term Investment \$ 5,950, credit Unrealized Gain (income) \$ 5,950.

Sale: proceeds \( = 1{,}000 \times 84 - 50 = \$ 83{,}950 \). Carrying value at sale \$ 86,000. Realized loss \( = 86{,}000 - 83{,}950 = \$ 2{,}050 \). Debit Cash \$ 83,950, debit Realized Loss \$ 2,050, credit Short-Term Investment \$ 86,000.

  • Reporting the year-end fair-value adjustment as a separate "Unrealized Gain Reserve" in equity — that's the OCI treatment used for FVOCI securities, not FVTPL.
  • Using the original cost (not the carrying value) when computing the realized gain or loss at sale, double-counting the prior period's unrealized gain.
  • Recording dividend revenue when received in cash instead of when declared — under accrual accounting, declaration is the trigger.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A3.1 — "account for short-term investments at fair value through profit or loss, including unrealized and realized gains and losses."

1.4 — Inventory Cost Flows: FIFO, Weighted-Average, LCNRV
Periodic vs perpetual; first-in-first-out; weighted-average; lower of cost or net realizable value; effects on the financial statements.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

For most merchandising and manufacturing firms, inventory is the largest current asset. Two questions must be answered every period: (1) how much inventory is on hand at the balance-sheet date, and (2) what was the cost of the goods that were sold (COGS)? The answer depends on the inventory system (periodic or perpetual) and the cost-flow assumption chosen.

Under the periodic system, the company counts inventory at the end of the period and computes COGS as Beginning Inventory + Purchases − Ending Inventory. Under the perpetual system, every purchase and every sale is recorded immediately in the Inventory account, so the company always knows the on-hand quantity — but a year-end physical count is still done to detect shrinkage. Cost-flow assumptions only matter when unit costs change. FIFO (first-in, first-out) assumes the oldest costs leave first, so ending inventory is valued at the most recent costs. Weighted-average uses a moving (perpetual) or end-of-period (periodic) average of all available units. Canadian GAAP (and IFRS) prohibit LIFO. Specific identification is permitted for unique, expensive items like cars and jewellery.

Once the cost is determined, the lower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV) rule kicks in: inventory is reported at the lower of its cost and its net realizable value (selling price − costs to complete and sell). When NRV is lower, the company writes down inventory and debits Loss from Inventory Write-down (or COGS) — never overstating an asset.

  • FIFO: first-in-first-out; oldest costs become COGS, newest costs remain in inventory.
  • Weighted-average: COGS and ending inventory share the average cost per unit.
  • Periodic system: inventory and COGS computed only at period end; no perpetual record.
  • Perpetual system: running balance of inventory; entry made on every sale.
  • LCNRV: conservative rule — write inventory down when its NRV is below cost.

Example: Inventory at Northwind Sports.

Beginning inventory: 10 units @ \$ 20 = \$ 200. June 5 purchase: 20 units @ \$ 22 = \$ 440. June 12 sale: 15 units. June 20 purchase: 15 units @ \$ 25 = \$ 375.

FIFO (perpetual): The 15 sold come from the oldest layers — 10 @ \$ 20 + 5 @ \$ 22 = \$ 200 + \$ 110 = \$ 310 COGS. Remaining: 15 @ \$ 22 + 15 @ \$ 25 = \$ 330 + \$ 375 = \$ 705 ending inventory.

Weighted-average (perpetual): After the June 5 purchase the moving average = \( (200 + 440)/30 = \$ 21.33 \). The 15 sold cost \( 15 \times 21.33 = \$ 320 \). Remaining 15 units @ \$ 21.33 = \$ 320; plus June 20 purchase \$ 375 gives ending inventory \$ 695.

If the selling price falls and NRV = \$ 18/unit, the 30 ending units must be written down to \( 30 \times 18 = \$ 540 \), and the company recognizes a write-down expense.

  • Mixing systems — pretending a "FIFO weighted-average" exists. The cost-flow assumption and the system are independent choices.
  • Comparing FIFO ending inventory to weighted-average COGS — only compare like to like.
  • Forgetting that under rising prices, FIFO inflates net income (because COGS is based on older, lower costs) — leading to higher tax.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A4.1 — "apply inventory cost-flow assumptions (specific identification, FIFO, weighted-average) under both periodic and perpetual systems."

1.5 — Capital Assets: Acquisition Cost & Amortization Methods
Determining acquisition cost of PP&E; straight-line, declining-balance, and units-of-production amortization; allocation vs valuation.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A capital asset (property, plant, and equipment, or PP&E under IFRS) is a tangible asset held to produce goods or services for more than one year. Because the benefit extends over many periods, the cost is not expensed all at once; instead it is capitalized and then allocated as expense across the asset's useful life. That allocation is called amortization (IFRS & ASPE) or depreciation (older or U.S. usage) — the words are synonyms in this context. The key conceptual idea is that amortization is allocation, not valuation: the carrying value on the balance sheet is not what the asset is worth in the market; it is the unallocated cost still waiting to be expensed.

The acquisition cost includes every expenditure necessary to get the asset to its intended location and condition: purchase price, sales tax (HST is recoverable for registered businesses and excluded), freight, installation, testing, and even the cost of removing existing items so the asset can be set up. After acquisition, three amortization methods are commonly used: straight-line (equal expense each year, ideal for assets like buildings whose value-in-use is steady); declining-balance (higher expense in early years, mimicking the heavier productivity of new equipment); and units-of-production (expense proportional to usage, ideal for vehicles measured in km or machines measured in operating hours).

At the end of the useful life — or when the asset is sold or scrapped — the remaining carrying value is compared to the cash received. A debit balance left over after removing both the asset and the accumulated amortization, and crediting cash, becomes a gain (credit) or loss (debit) on disposal. The gain or loss is reported on the income statement, often as part of "other gains and losses."

  • Capitalize: record a cost as an asset rather than an expense.
  • Carrying value (net book value): cost − accumulated amortization.
  • Residual (salvage) value: estimated proceeds at the end of useful life.
  • Useful life: the expected period of use to the current owner; not necessarily physical life.
  • Accumulated amortization: a contra-asset that grows with each period's amortization expense.

Example: A delivery van is purchased for \$ 40,000 cash plus \$ 1,800 freight and \$ 200 lettering. Residual value \$ 4,000; useful life 5 years or 250,000 km.

Capitalized cost \( = 40{,}000 + 1{,}800 + 200 = \$ 42{,}000 \). Amortizable base \( = 42{,}000 - 4{,}000 = \$ 38{,}000 \).

Straight-line: \( \dfrac{38{,}000}{5} = \$ 7{,}600 \) per year.

Double-declining-balance: rate \( = \dfrac{2}{5} = 40\% \). Year 1 expense \( = 0.40 \times 42{,}000 = \$ 16{,}800 \). Year 2 \( = 0.40 \times (42{,}000 - 16{,}800) = 0.40 \times 25{,}200 = \$ 10{,}080 \). Residual is checked but not subtracted until the final year.

Units-of-production: rate \( = \dfrac{38{,}000}{250{,}000} = \$ 0.152 \) per km. If 60,000 km are driven in year 1, expense \( = 60{,}000 \times 0.152 = \$ 9{,}120 \).

  • Using residual value as a reduction in the declining-balance formula — the formula uses cost, not amortizable base.
  • Forgetting to stop amortizing once carrying value equals residual value.
  • Confusing amortization (allocation of cost) with impairment (a permanent loss in recoverable value); both reduce carrying value but for different reasons.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A5.2 — "calculate amortization expense using straight-line, declining-balance, and units-of-production methods; explain why amortization is allocation, not valuation."

1.6 — Disposal of Capital Assets
Recording gains and losses on sale, scrapping, and trade-ins; updating accumulated amortization to the date of disposal.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Every capital asset eventually leaves the business: it is sold, scrapped, or traded in. The disposal entry has three parts, executed in a strict order. Step 1: bring amortization up to date — if the asset is sold partway through the year, record a partial year of amortization expense and credit Accumulated Amortization for the months elapsed. Step 2: remove the asset and its contra-account from the books — credit the asset account at its original cost; debit Accumulated Amortization at its current balance. Step 3: record the consideration received — debit Cash (or whatever is received) and balance the entry with a gain (credit) or loss (debit) on disposal.

The decision rule is straightforward: compare cash proceeds to carrying value. If cash > carrying value, the difference is a gain on disposal; if cash < carrying value, the difference is a loss on disposal. Gains and losses on disposal are reported on the income statement, typically under "other income/expense." For scrapping (cash = 0), the loss equals the carrying value. For trade-ins, the cash equivalent is the trade-in allowance plus any cash paid; the new asset is recorded at its cash-equivalent acquisition cost.

One reason this topic causes confusion is that students forget Step 1 — the partial-period amortization. Without it, the accumulated amortization removed at Step 2 is too small, and the resulting gain or loss is incorrect. Always update first, then dispose.

  • Carrying value at disposal: original cost − updated accumulated amortization.
  • Gain on disposal: a credit account on the income statement when proceeds exceed carrying value.
  • Loss on disposal: a debit account on the income statement when proceeds are below carrying value.
  • Scrapping: disposal with zero proceeds; the entire carrying value becomes a loss.
  • Trade-in: the old asset is exchanged plus cash for a new one; the new asset is recorded at its cash-equivalent cost.

Example: A machine purchased Jan 1, 2023 for \$ 80,000 (residual \$ 5,000, 10-year straight-line life) is sold July 1, 2026 for \$ 55,000.

Annual amortization \( = (80{,}000 - 5{,}000)/10 = \$ 7{,}500 \). Years of full amortization: 2023, 2024, 2025 = 3 full years = \$ 22,500. Plus half year of 2026 = \$ 3,750. Updated accumulated amortization \$ 26,250.

Carrying value at July 1, 2026 \( = 80{,}000 - 26{,}250 = \$ 53{,}750 \). Proceeds \$ 55,000 > carrying value, so a \$ 1,250 gain.

Entry: debit Cash \$ 55,000, debit Accumulated Amortization \$ 26,250, credit Machine \$ 80,000, credit Gain on Disposal \$ 1,250.

  • Skipping Step 1 — the partial-period amortization — and computing a wrong carrying value.
  • Crediting Cash and debiting Loss (a debit balance) for a scrapped asset is correct only if amortization is up to date.
  • Recording the new asset in a trade-in at the list price instead of the cash-equivalent (trade-in allowance + cash paid) cost.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A5.3 — "record disposals of capital assets including gain or loss on disposal and accumulated amortization removal."
