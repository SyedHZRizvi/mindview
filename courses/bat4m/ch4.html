4.1 — Objectives & Components of Internal Control (COSO)
The five objectives of internal control; the five components of the COSO framework; tone at the top.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An organization's internal control system is the set of policies and procedures that management designs and implements to provide reasonable assurance — never absolute certainty — that five interlocking objectives will be achieved: safeguarding assets from theft, error, and misuse; accurate accounting records that faithfully reflect transactions; operational efficiency in the use of resources; adherence to management policy by employees; and reliable financial reporting for external users. These five objectives drive every specific control activity (locked vaults, password security, dual signatures) and every detective control (reconciliations, audits, surprise counts).

The framework that organizes how managers build a control system is the 2013 COSO Internal Control – Integrated Framework. It identifies five components, often summarized by the mnemonic CRIME (Control environment, Risk assessment, Information & communication, Monitoring, control activitiEs). Control environment is the "tone at the top": the ethical climate, organizational structure, and competence of personnel set by management. Risk assessment is the process of identifying and analysing risks to objectives. Control activities are the actual policies — authorization, reconciliation, physical safeguards, segregation of duties. Information and communication ensures relevant information reaches the people who need it. Monitoring includes ongoing supervision and periodic audits to make sure the controls are still working.

An effective control system cannot succeed without an ethical control environment. A 2024 ACFE report finds that companies with weak "tone at the top" suffer fraud losses roughly twice as large as those with strong leadership commitment. No locked door, password, or audit can compensate for a CEO who tolerates dishonesty.

  • Internal control: processes designed by management and the board to provide reasonable assurance of achieving control objectives.
  • COSO: the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations — author of the integrated framework used globally.
  • Tone at the top: the ethical climate set by senior management.
  • Reasonable assurance: high but not absolute confidence; costs of control must not exceed benefits.
  • Inherent limitations: human error, collusion, management override, cost-benefit trade-offs.

Example: A municipal recreation centre's coin-operated arcade has \$ 3,000 a week in receipts. Identify a control failure for each COSO component.

Control environment: the supervisor jokes that "skimming a little doesn't matter." (Tone is permissive.)

Risk assessment: no one has identified theft as a risk; counts are not analysed.

Control activities: the same employee opens the machines, counts cash, and writes the deposit slip.

Information & communication: meter readings are not shared with the accounting office.

Monitoring: no surprise counts or supervisor review.

Fixing each layer reduces the risk of theft without requiring police investigation.

  • Believing controls give "absolute assurance" — they don't. Collusion and management override always remain risks.
  • Implementing controls in only one COSO area (e.g., locking doors) and ignoring the rest (e.g., culture).
  • Treating control as a compliance exercise rather than a continuous discipline.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "describe the five components of the COSO framework (control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information & communication, monitoring)."

4.2 — Segregation of Duties
The four classic duties — authorization, custody, recording, reconciliation; why no single person should perform two or more.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The single most effective internal-control technique is segregation of duties (also called "separation of incompatible functions"). The principle: no individual should be in a position to both perpetrate and conceal a fraud or significant error. Auditors and the COSO framework identify four duties that must be separated: authorization (approving a transaction), custody (holding the physical asset), recordkeeping (entering the transaction in the books), and reconciliation (independently checking that the records match the assets). When all four are performed by different people, a successful fraud usually requires collusion among multiple employees — a much rarer event than solo wrongdoing.

A small business often has too few employees to fully separate the four duties. In that situation the owner (or another senior person) must perform the reconciliation step personally — opening the bank statement, comparing it to the deposit book, and reviewing the journal entries. Compensating controls like surprise counts and mandatory two-week vacations (so a different person handles the function while the regular employee is away) further reduce the risk of an employee covering up a long-running scheme. Job rotation has the same effect: fraud rarely survives a fresh pair of eyes.

Segregation should be visible on an organization chart with reporting lines. Whenever a manager performs two of the four duties — say, the bookkeeper also signs cheques — the auditor flags it as a control deficiency and recommends a fix. Software systems implement segregation through access controls that prevent a single user from posting both sides of a transaction.

  • Segregation of duties: dividing authorization, custody, recordkeeping, and reconciliation among different people.
  • Collusion: two or more employees working together to defeat segregation.
  • Compensating controls: alternative procedures (mandatory vacations, surprise audits) used when full segregation isn't possible.
  • Access control: system-level restrictions enforcing segregation in software.
  • Job rotation: periodic reassignment of duties to expose hidden activity.

Example: At Pinecrest Hardware, the accounts-payable clerk receives supplier invoices, enters them in the AP journal, prepares cheques for the owner's signature, and after the owner signs, mails the cheques. Identify the segregation problem and propose a fix.

The clerk performs recordkeeping and partial custody (handles the cheque after signing). A fraudulent clerk could enter an invoice for a fictitious supplier, prepare a cheque payable to that supplier, get it signed, and cash it.

Fix: a second person should mail signed cheques directly from the owner's office (taking custody out of the AP clerk's hands), and the bank reconciliation should be performed by yet a third person to detect any unusual payee. Authorization remains with the owner.

  • Believing two-person segregation (e.g., one person does authorization and recordkeeping; a second does custody) is enough — four distinct functions are the goal.
  • Allowing the bookkeeper to perform the bank reconciliation. Reconciliation must be done by someone who does not record transactions.
  • Forgetting that segregation must extend to IT roles — the developer who writes the payroll module should not also be able to run payroll.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "apply segregation of duties: separating authorization, custody, recordkeeping, and reconciliation."

4.3 — Specific Control Procedures: Cash, Inventory, Payroll
Practical controls — pre-numbered receipts, dual-signature cheques, cycle counts, payroll registers, vendor-master review.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Beyond the framework, the BAT4M syllabus expects students to design specific control procedures for the major risk areas: cash receipts, cash payments, inventory, and payroll. For cash receipts: all incoming cheques are stamped "For Deposit Only" and entered on a pre-numbered receipt log by a clerk; a second person prepares the daily deposit and a third reconciles the bank statement. Pre-numbered receipts close the loop because a missing number cannot be lost without trace. For cash payments: all disbursements (except petty cash) are made by cheque or EFT, every cheque is supported by a properly approved invoice and receiving report, large amounts require dual signatures, and signed cheques are mailed directly by someone other than the AP clerk.

For inventory: goods receive a receiving report on arrival, are stored in a locked warehouse with restricted key access, and are removed only via a pre-numbered issue slip authorized by a supervisor. Cycle counts (random monthly counts of selected items) supplement the annual physical inventory and detect shrinkage in real time. For payroll: a separate "human resources" function authorizes new hires and pay rates, the timekeeping function records hours independently, the payroll department computes pay, and the treasurer signs cheques — four roles, four people. Unclaimed wages are turned over to a senior officer who investigates, since fictitious-employee fraud often appears as recurring unclaimed cheques.

For each control the auditor performs a test of controls — for example, by tracing a sample of receiving reports to the purchase journal — to confirm that the control is operating as designed. Failed tests of controls trigger substantive procedures like more detailed verification of balances.

  • Pre-numbered documents: any missing number signals a missing transaction.
  • Dual signature requirement: two managers must sign cheques above a threshold (e.g., \$ 10,000).
  • Cycle count: partial inventory count performed throughout the year.
  • Receiving report: the document that triggers payment for goods received.
  • Test of controls: auditor's procedure to confirm a control operates as designed.

Example: Design a payroll-fraud control. Concern: phantom employees on the payroll.

1. HR (not payroll) maintains the master employee file and authorizes new hires with a signed contract and SIN verification.

2. Time records are produced by a separate timekeeping system (badge swipe or supervisor sign-off).

3. Payroll computes pay and produces a payroll register, which is independently approved by a department manager before pay is released.

4. The treasurer issues direct deposits or signs cheques, not the payroll department.

5. Unclaimed wages or returned cheques are investigated by a senior officer who reports back to internal audit.

A phantom employee requires either an HR insider to enrol the fake worker or a payroll insider to slip them in undetected — and the manager-approved register makes either path very hard.

  • Designing control procedures without thinking of who performs each — the procedure must name positions.
  • Forgetting that pre-numbered documents only work if the number sequence is reviewed and gaps are investigated.
  • Confusing "test of controls" with "substantive procedures" — the first confirms controls work; the second tests dollar accuracy.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.2 — "design specific internal-control procedures for cash receipts, cash payments, inventory, and payroll."

4.4 — Internal & External Auditors
Roles, independence, audit committee; types of audit opinions: unqualified, qualified, adverse, disclaimer.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Audit is the independent examination of records and operations. Two distinct roles exist. Internal auditors are employees of the company who continuously evaluate the design and operation of internal control, report to senior management and to the audit committee, and recommend improvements. They are operationally independent — they don't manage the activities they audit — but they are not independent of the company itself. External auditors are licensed CPAs working for a public-accounting firm (Deloitte, KPMG, EY, PwC, etc.) hired by the shareholders (via the board) to provide an independent opinion on whether the financial statements are fairly presented in accordance with the applicable framework (IFRS for publicly accountable enterprises in Canada; ASPE for many private companies).

The external auditor's job ends with one of four audit opinions. An unqualified (clean) opinion means the financial statements present fairly, in all material respects, the financial position and performance of the company; this is the goal of every reporting cycle. A qualified opinion says the statements are fair "except for" a specific issue (a scope limitation or a departure from GAAP that, while material, doesn't pervade the statements). An adverse opinion says the statements do not present fairly — extreme and rare. A disclaimer means the auditor was unable to gather enough evidence to form an opinion at all.

The audit committee of the board of directors — composed of independent directors with financial expertise — supervises the external auditor on behalf of the shareholders, approves the audit fee, receives reports of significant deficiencies, and serves as a channel for whistleblowers. Since the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (and Canada's Bill 198), the audit committee has been required at all publicly traded companies and carries personal accountability for the integrity of the financial reporting.

  • Internal auditor: employee who continuously evaluates internal control; reports to the audit committee.
  • External auditor: licensed CPA who provides an independent opinion on the fair presentation of the financial statements.
  • Unqualified opinion: "clean" — financial statements present fairly in all material respects.
  • Qualified opinion: fair "except for" a specific limitation or departure.
  • Audit committee: the board subcommittee that hires/supervises the external auditor on behalf of shareholders.

Example: After several years of clean opinions, Aurora Corp.'s 2026 audit is qualified because the auditor was unable to obtain confirmations from a major customer representing 18% of accounts receivable.

What does this tell investors? The financial statements may still be fairly presented overall, but the AR figure cannot be verified by independent evidence to a high level of assurance. Investors should: (a) read the qualification's full text in the audit report, (b) request management's explanation of why the customer didn't respond, and (c) examine the AR collection trend before relying on the asset value.

A qualified opinion is a yellow flag, not a red one — but multiple years of qualifications, or movement toward adverse, is a serious warning.

  • Believing the external auditor guarantees no fraud — the audit provides reasonable assurance about material misstatement, not a fraud guarantee.
  • Treating internal and external auditors as substitutes; their roles are complementary.
  • Reading only the audit opinion's first paragraph; the modifications and key audit matters paragraphs carry the substantive findings.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.2 — "explain the auditor's opinion: unqualified (clean), qualified, adverse, and disclaimer; and the role of the audit committee."

4.5 — Fraud: The Fraud Triangle & Red Flags
Pressure, opportunity, rationalization; common schemes — skimming, ghost employees, lapping; earnings management.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Most occupational fraud is committed by trusted insiders who exhibit three coinciding factors known as the fraud triangle, first articulated by criminologist Donald Cressey in 1953. Pressure is the financial or personal need that motivates the act — unmanageable debt, an addiction, the loss of a spouse's job. Opportunity is the chance to commit the act without being caught — a control weakness such as poor segregation of duties. Rationalization is the mental story the perpetrator tells themselves — "I'm only borrowing," "the company underpays me," "I'll pay it back when my bonus comes in." All three legs are required; remove any one and the fraud is far less likely to occur. Strong internal control attacks the opportunity leg; strong ethical culture attacks the rationalization leg.

Three common schemes illustrate the triangle in action. Skimming: an employee pockets cash before it is recorded — common in retail, where a cashier rings up a sale as "no sale" and keeps the customer's cash. Ghost employees: a payroll insider keeps a fired employee on the books and diverts the pay. Lapping: an AR clerk steals a customer's payment, then covers it by applying a later customer's payment to the first customer's account, and so on — a slow-motion Ponzi scheme inside the AR ledger. Each of these schemes leaves red flags: lifestyle inconsistent with salary, refusal to take vacation, control over a process from end to end, complaints from customers about payments not being credited.

Earnings management is a related but distinct concern — not employee theft but management's deliberate use of accounting judgment to smooth or hit a target. Aggressive revenue recognition (booking sales before delivery), big-bath restructuring charges, and cookie-jar reserves are classic examples. Earnings management can be perfectly legal yet still mislead investors, which is why GAAP and IFRS continually tighten the rules around judgmental items.

  • Fraud triangle: pressure + opportunity + rationalization; all three must coincide.
  • Skimming: stealing cash before it is recorded.
  • Lapping: covering a stolen receipt by applying a later customer's payment to the first account.
  • Ghost employee: a fictitious or terminated employee kept on the payroll.
  • Earnings management: using accounting judgment to influence reported income, often in lawful but misleading ways.

Example: Lapping at Riverside Plumbing — detection.

The AR clerk steals \$ 800 from Customer A on June 1 and applies Customer B's \$ 800 payment on June 5 to A's account; later applies C's payment to B; and so on. The scheme grows each month and survives only as long as new payments keep covering old gaps.

Red flag: customer A complains that her June 1 payment was never credited (she has the cancelled cheque). Internal audit pulls the daily deposit register, observes that the date of the customer's cheque (June 1) is later applied "on behalf of" customer C — an unusual cross-application. A surprise reconciliation of the AR subsidiary detects the entire scheme.

Prevention: rotate the AR clerk's role, send customer statements directly to customers (not via the clerk), and reconcile AR subsidiary to control every month with an independent review.

  • Treating fraud as inevitable; the goal is to make it costly and quickly detectable.
  • Believing fraud is committed by strangers — most occupational fraud is by trusted long-term employees.
  • Confusing earnings management (legal but misleading) with fraud (intentional deception that is a crime).

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D4.1 — "describe the fraud triangle (pressure, opportunity, rationalization) and identify red flags that indicate fraud or earnings management."

4.6 — Sarbanes-Oxley, Bill 198, & the CPA Canada Handbook
Legislative response to Enron and WorldCom; Section 404 and the management certification of controls; Canadian equivalent.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The 2001 collapse of Enron and the 2002 fraud at WorldCom (each involving billions of dollars in misstated earnings) destroyed Arthur Andersen, a Big Five audit firm, and provoked a sweeping legislative response in the U.S.: the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX). SOX requires (among many provisions) the CEO and CFO to personally certify the financial statements (Section 302); imposes criminal penalties for falsified certifications; requires management to assess and report on the effectiveness of internal control over financial reporting (Section 404); and requires the external auditor to express an opinion on that internal-control report. The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) was created to regulate auditors of public companies.

Canada's parallel response was Ontario's Bill 198 (sometimes called "C-SOX"), adopted in 2002, which led to National Instrument 52-109. Canadian publicly traded companies must have the CEO and CFO certify quarterly and annually, and must report on the effectiveness of their internal control over financial reporting and disclosure controls. The Canadian rules are less prescriptive than U.S. SOX but cover the same core requirements. Both regimes have substantially raised the cost of being a public company but also raised the bar for the integrity of public-company financial information.

The CPA Canada Handbook sets out the professional accounting standards in Canada (IFRS, ASPE, the Public Sector Handbook, and the Canadian Auditing Standards). It is the single most-cited reference in Canadian financial reporting. Knowing your way around the Handbook — including the relevant section number for revenue recognition (IFRS 15 / ASPE Section 3400), leases (IFRS 16), inventory (IAS 2 / ASPE Section 3031) — is a hallmark of a working accountant.

  • Sarbanes-Oxley (2002): U.S. legislation requiring CEO/CFO certification and Section 404 internal-control reporting.
  • Bill 198 / NI 52-109: Canada's parallel framework for public-company internal control.
  • PCAOB: Public Company Accounting Oversight Board — U.S. regulator of audit firms.
  • CPA Canada Handbook: the authoritative source for Canadian accounting and auditing standards.
  • Section 302 / 404: the SOX provisions requiring quarterly officer certification and annual internal-control reporting.

Example: A startup decides to list on the TSX. List four new compliance costs the company will incur in its first listed year.

1. CEO/CFO certification of every quarterly and annual filing (NI 52-109).

2. Documentation and testing of internal control over financial reporting (ICFR), including written policies and an annual management assessment.

3. An audit committee composed of three independent directors, with at least one "financial expert."

4. External-auditor opinion on the financial statements at the higher PIE (Public Interest Entity) standard, with mandatory partner rotation every five years.

These are in addition to disclosure costs (quarterly MD&A, prospectus, AIF, continuous disclosure) and the cost of an investor-relations function. Together, the compliance load for a small public company is often \$ 1–3 million per year.

  • Believing SOX applies in Canada directly — it doesn't; NI 52-109 is the relevant Canadian rule for TSX-listed companies.
  • Treating compliance as paperwork. The certification creates personal liability for the CEO and CFO.
  • Forgetting that private Canadian companies follow ASPE, not IFRS, unless they choose otherwise.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D4.2 — "explain the role of legislation (CPA Canada Handbook, Bill 198 / CSOX, Sarbanes-Oxley Act) in promoting financial integrity."
