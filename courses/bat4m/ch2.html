2.1 — Specialized Journals: Sales & Cash Receipts
Recording high-volume credit sales and cash receipts in dedicated columnar journals; totals posted to the general ledger.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A real business records hundreds or thousands of transactions every month. Posting every one of them into the general journal — debit one line, credit one line — would be unbearably slow and would offer no protection against fraud or error. The solution, in use since the nineteenth century, is to group similar transactions into specialized journals, each designed to record exactly one kind of event. The four classical specialized journals are the sales journal (credit sales of merchandise), the cash receipts journal (all cash inflows), the purchases journal (credit purchases of merchandise), and the cash payments journal (all cash outflows). Anything that doesn't fit a specialized journal — adjusting entries, depreciation, write-offs — still goes in the general journal.

The sales journal has one row per credit sale. Columns typically include: date, customer name, invoice number, AR debit, Sales credit, and HST Payable credit. Posting is two-tiered: the individual sale is posted daily to the customer's account in the AR subsidiary ledger, and the column totals are posted monthly to the general ledger. This separation keeps the general ledger uncluttered while still letting the company answer the question "how much does customer Lopez owe?" at any moment.

The cash receipts journal is more complex because cash inflows have several sources: payments from credit customers, cash sales, owner contributions, and miscellaneous receipts. It therefore has more columns: Cash debit, Sales Discount debit (if a customer pays within the discount period), AR credit, Sales credit, HST credit, and "Other Accounts" credit (with the account name written in). At month end, every column except "Other Accounts" is posted to its general-ledger account; the Other Accounts column is posted line by line.

  • Special journal: a journal designed for one type of repetitive transaction.
  • Sales journal: records credit sales of merchandise only; cash sales go in the cash receipts journal.
  • Cash receipts journal: records all cash inflows regardless of source.
  • Sales discount: a reduction in the invoice price for prompt payment, e.g. "2/10 n/30".
  • Posting reference (PR): the account number entered after each line is posted, providing the audit trail.

Example: On June 12, Maple Co. sells \$ 1,000 of merchandise on account to customer Wong (terms 2/10 n/30). HST 13%. On June 18 Wong pays within the discount period.

Sales journal (June 12): AR debit \$ 1,130; Sales credit \$ 1,000; HST Payable credit \$ 130. Post \$ 1,130 daily to Wong's AR subsidiary card.

Cash receipts journal (June 18): Wong gets a 2% discount on \$ 1,000 = \$ 20. Cash debit \$ 1,110; Sales Discount debit \$ 20; AR credit \$ 1,130. Post \$ 1,130 daily as a credit to Wong's AR card. Month-end posting: Cash, Sales Discount, and AR column totals go to the general ledger.

  • Posting a credit-sale entry directly to the general ledger and forgetting to update the customer's subsidiary card.
  • Putting cash sales into the sales journal — they belong in the cash receipts journal.
  • Forgetting that the sales discount is recorded only when the customer actually takes the discount (i.e., pays within the discount window), not at the time of sale.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.1 — "record transactions in the four specialized journals: sales journal, cash receipts journal, purchases journal, and cash payments journal."

2.2 — Subsidiary Ledgers & Control Accounts
Accounts-receivable and accounts-payable subsidiary ledgers; control-account reconciliation; the schedule of accounts.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Imagine 200 credit customers, each with their own balance. If the general ledger had to keep an account for every customer, every monthly trial balance would have hundreds of lines and become unmanageable. The accountant's solution is a two-tier ledger system: a single control account in the general ledger (for example, "Accounts Receivable") holds the total, while a separate subsidiary ledger holds one card per customer. The subsidiary ledger is not part of the general ledger and is not included in the trial balance; the two are linked by the requirement that the sum of the subsidiary ledger balances must equal the control-account balance at all times.

Two subsidiary ledgers are universal: Accounts Receivable subsidiary ledger, organized alphabetically by customer; and Accounts Payable subsidiary ledger, organized alphabetically by supplier. Specialized journals automate the synchronization. Every line posted to the AR subsidiary card is also accumulated in the sales-journal "AR debit" column, which is posted monthly to the AR control account. At month end the company prepares a schedule of accounts receivable: a list of every customer's ending balance, totalled at the bottom. If that total equals the AR control account, the system is "in balance" and posting is correct.

The same logic applies to inventory (an inventory subsidiary ledger one card per stock-keeping unit) and to capital assets (one card per piece of equipment with date, cost, life, and accumulated amortization). Modern computerized systems hide the mechanics, but the underlying control-account concept is unchanged: a single ledger value summarises many detailed records, and the two must always agree.

  • Control account: a single general-ledger account whose balance equals the total of a subsidiary ledger.
  • Subsidiary ledger: a group of detailed accounts (one per customer, supplier, asset) supporting a control account.
  • Schedule of accounts receivable / payable: a month-end listing of all subsidiary balances, totalled.
  • Posting reference (PR): the column showing where each amount was posted, used to audit the trail.
  • Reconciliation: the process of verifying the schedule total against the control-account balance.

Example: At June 30, the AR control account shows a debit balance of \$ 18,450. The schedule of AR is: Wong \$ 6,200; Lopez \$ 4,800; Patel \$ 5,000; Singh \$ 2,250; total \$ 18,250.

The system is out of balance by \$ 200. The bookkeeper traces back: the sales journal for June 12 shows a credit sale to Wong of \$ 1,000 + HST \$ 130 = \$ 1,130. The AR debit column total was \$ 18,650 (incorrect) instead of \$ 18,450; the difference came from posting Wong's daily entry as \$ 930 (transposed digits). Correcting Wong's subsidiary card adds \$ 200; now both the control and the schedule equal \$ 18,450.

  • Posting only to the subsidiary ledger and forgetting the monthly total to the control account — the trial balance will be wrong.
  • Treating the schedule of AR as optional. It is the primary control over the AR control account.
  • Mixing up "schedule of AR" (a current listing of customer balances) with "aging of AR" (the same listing, but broken out by days past due).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.2 — "maintain accounts-receivable and accounts-payable subsidiary ledgers; reconcile them to their general-ledger control accounts."

2.3 — Perpetual vs Periodic Inventory Systems
Journal entries for purchases, freight, returns, discounts, and sales under each system; computing COGS.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A merchandising business must answer the question "what is the cost of goods sold (COGS)?" Two systems answer it differently. Under the perpetual system, Inventory is a running general-ledger account: every purchase debits Inventory, and every sale of merchandise triggers two entries — one for the revenue (debit Cash/AR, credit Sales) and one for the cost (debit COGS, credit Inventory). The company always knows the carrying value of inventory and the cumulative COGS in real time. Under the periodic system, purchases instead go into a temporary "Purchases" account; Inventory is updated only at period end, when a physical count is taken. COGS is then computed by the formula \( \text{COGS} = \text{Beginning Inventory} + \text{Net Purchases} - \text{Ending Inventory} \).

Each system has trade-offs. Perpetual is information-rich and supports tight control (you can detect shrinkage by comparing book inventory to a count) but requires either bar-code scanning or constant manual updates. Periodic is cheap and was the norm before computerization; its weakness is that shrinkage and theft are absorbed into COGS and become invisible. Modern small businesses with point-of-sale software use perpetual; bulk-stocking warehouses with cheap, plentiful goods (nails, fasteners) may still use periodic.

Two contra-purchase accounts appear in the periodic system: Purchase Returns & Allowances and Purchase Discounts. Their balances reduce gross purchases to "net purchases." Likewise on the sales side, Sales Returns & Allowances and Sales Discounts are contra-revenue accounts reducing gross sales to net sales. Freight-in on goods purchased is added to inventory cost; freight-out on goods sold is a delivery expense (a selling expense, not part of COGS).

  • Perpetual system: Inventory is a real-time general-ledger account; COGS is recorded on every sale.
  • Periodic system: Inventory is updated only at period end; COGS is computed by formula.
  • Freight-in (FOB shipping point): buyer pays; added to cost of inventory.
  • Freight-out (FOB destination): seller pays; classified as a delivery/selling expense.
  • Gross profit: Net Sales − COGS; key margin metric in financial analysis.

Example: Sale of 10 units at \$ 50 each (cost \$ 30 each), HST 13%, cash sale.

Perpetual entries: (1) Cash \$ 565; Sales \$ 500; HST Payable \$ 65. (2) COGS \$ 300; Inventory \$ 300.

Periodic entries: (1) Cash \$ 565; Sales \$ 500; HST Payable \$ 65. No second entry at sale. Under periodic, COGS appears only at year end via the formula. If Beginning Inventory \$ 8,000, Net Purchases \$ 22,000, Ending Inventory (by count) \$ 6,500, then \( \text{COGS} = 8{,}000 + 22{,}000 - 6{,}500 = \$ 23{,}500 \).

  • Forgetting the second entry (debit COGS, credit Inventory) under perpetual — net income will be overstated by the cost.
  • Including freight-out in cost of inventory — it is a selling expense.
  • Using the gross-purchases figure instead of net purchases when computing COGS under periodic.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "differentiate between perpetual and periodic inventory systems, including the journal entries required under each."

2.4 — Partnerships: Formation, Profit Division & Changes
Capital and drawing accounts for partners; dividing profits by ratio, salary & interest, and capital-balance methods; admission and withdrawal.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A partnership is an unincorporated business owned by two or more people who share profits, losses, and management. Its accounting differs from a sole proprietorship only in one place: equity. Each partner has a separate Capital account (long-term equity) and a Drawings account (short-term withdrawals), and the partnership agreement spells out how profit and loss are divided among the partners. Three common methods appear on the BAT4M exam: (1) fixed ratio, (2) salary and interest allowances with the remainder shared in a residual ratio, and (3) ratio based on capital balances.

The fixed-ratio method is simplest: if Asha and Brian agree to share 2:3, then for every \$ 5 of profit, Asha gets \$ 2 and Brian gets \$ 3. The salary-and-interest method recognizes that partners may contribute unequally — one provides labour, another provides capital — by first allocating salary and interest on capital, then dividing whatever remains in a residual ratio. The total allocated to a partner can be negative (a loss) even if the partnership had positive net income, because guaranteed salary/interest is paid before the residual ratio is applied.

When a new partner is admitted, their cash investment is debited to Cash and credited to their new Capital account. When a partner withdraws, their Capital balance is closed and they receive the corresponding cash. If the new partner pays more or less than their share of net assets, the difference is a bonus distributed to the existing partners (or to the new partner) according to their old ratio.

  • Capital account: long-term equity interest of a partner.
  • Drawings account: short-term withdrawals, closed to capital at year end.
  • Profit-sharing ratio: the agreed split of residual profit/loss.
  • Salary allowance: a fixed amount allocated before residual sharing (not a cash payment in itself).
  • Bonus method: the technique used when a new partner's investment doesn't equal their share of net assets.

Example: Asha and Brian's partnership had net income of \$ 60,000. Agreement: salaries — Asha \$ 20,000, Brian \$ 15,000; interest at 5% on opening capital (Asha \$ 100,000, Brian \$ 50,000); residual shared 60:40.

Salaries: A \$ 20,000, B \$ 15,000; total \$ 35,000.

Interest: A \( 0.05 \times 100{,}000 = \$ 5{,}000 \), B \( 0.05 \times 50{,}000 = \$ 2{,}500 \); total \$ 7,500.

Allocated so far: \$ 42,500. Remainder \$ 60,000 − \$ 42,500 = \$ 17,500. Split 60:40 — A \$ 10,500, B \$ 7,000.

Total to Asha: \( 20{,}000 + 5{,}000 + 10{,}500 = \$ 35{,}500 \). Total to Brian: \( 15{,}000 + 2{,}500 + 7{,}000 = \$ 24{,}500 \). Check: \( 35{,}500 + 24{,}500 = \$ 60{,}000 \). ✓

  • Treating the salary allowance as a cash payment — it is not; it is just a mathematical step in dividing profit.
  • Allocating the residual before salary and interest. The residual is, by definition, whatever is left.
  • Forgetting that a "loss residual" still gets divided in the same residual ratio.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.1 — "record formation, division of profit/loss, admission of new partners, withdrawal of partners, and liquidation of a partnership."

2.5 — Corporations: Share Issuance, Dividends & Retained Earnings
Authorized, issued, and outstanding shares; common and preferred shares; cash, stock, and dividend declarations; statement of retained earnings.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A corporation is a legal entity separate from its owners (shareholders). Equity has two main components: Share Capital (the proceeds from issuing shares) and Retained Earnings (cumulative profits not yet distributed). When shares are issued for cash, the entry is straightforward: debit Cash for the proceeds and credit Common Shares (or Preferred Shares) for the same amount. Canadian corporations issue no-par-value shares, so the entire proceeds become share capital — there is no "premium" or "discount" account. Common shares carry the residual claim and voting rights; preferred shares carry a stated dividend preference and usually no vote.

The Board of Directors may distribute profits as cash dividends. A dividend has three dates: the declaration date (when the Board declares; entry: debit Cash Dividends Declared, credit Dividends Payable); the record date (no entry — only a snapshot of who owns shares); and the payment date (entry: debit Dividends Payable, credit Cash). Preferred shareholders are paid first, up to the stated rate; common shareholders receive whatever the Board decides to distribute thereafter. Stock dividends issue additional shares (no cash leaves the business): the entry is to debit Retained Earnings and credit Common Shares at the fair value of the issued shares. Stock splits divide each existing share into more pieces — no journal entry, just a memo, because total share capital is unchanged.

At year end the corporation prepares a statement of retained earnings: Opening RE + Net Income − Dividends Declared = Closing RE. The Cash Dividends Declared account is a temporary equity account closed to Retained Earnings, just as Drawings is closed to Capital in a sole proprietorship.

  • Authorized shares: the maximum number a corporation can issue per its articles.
  • Issued shares: the number that have ever been sold by the corporation.
  • Outstanding shares: issued shares currently held by investors (issued less any treasury shares).
  • Preferred shares: equity with a stated dividend rate paid before common dividends.
  • Stock split: a proportional increase in shares (e.g., 2-for-1); no journal entry.

Example: Riverbend Inc. is authorized to issue 1,000,000 common shares and 100,000 \$ 4 preferred shares. On Feb 1 it issues 50,000 common shares at \$ 10 cash and 5,000 preferred shares at \$ 50 cash. On Dec 15 the Board declares a \$ 0.30 common cash dividend and the regular preferred dividend; both are paid Jan 15.

Feb 1: Cash \$ 500,000; Common Shares \$ 500,000. Cash \$ 250,000; Preferred Shares \$ 250,000.

Dec 15: Common dividend \( = 50{,}000 \times 0.30 = \$ 15{,}000 \). Preferred dividend \( = 5{,}000 \times 4 = \$ 20{,}000 \). Total declared \$ 35,000.

Entry on Dec 15: Cash Dividends Declared \$ 35,000; Dividends Payable \$ 35,000. Entry on Jan 15: Dividends Payable \$ 35,000; Cash \$ 35,000.

  • Recording a "premium" on no-par-value shares — Canadian shares are no-par, so the entire proceeds go to Share Capital.
  • Skipping the entry on declaration date; the liability exists from the moment the Board declares, not from the payment date.
  • Confusing a stock dividend (journal entry required) with a stock split (memo only).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B3.3 — "record declaration and payment of cash dividends, stock dividends, and stock splits; prepare a statement of retained earnings."

2.6 — Payroll Accounting
Gross pay, statutory deductions (CPP, EI, income tax), net pay; the employer's share; payroll journal entries.
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Payroll is one of the largest operating expenses for most Canadian businesses and one of the most heavily regulated. From the employee's perspective the calculation works in three steps: gross pay = hours × rate (or salary for the period), plus overtime and commissions; statutory deductions (Canada Pension Plan contribution, Employment Insurance premium, federal and provincial income tax) are subtracted to give net pay, which is what hits the employee's bank account. The employer is the agent: it withholds the deductions and remits them to the Receiver General of Canada along with its own share. From the employer's perspective there is a second set of costs — the employer's matched CPP, the employer's EI at 1.4× the employee rate, the Workplace Safety and Insurance Board premium, vacation pay accrual, and the Employer Health Tax (in Ontario for payrolls over the exemption). These employer costs are payroll expenses, not deductions from the employee.

The journal entries follow this two-perspective logic. Entry 1 (payroll expense to the employee): debit Salaries & Wages Expense for gross pay; credit each statutory liability (CPP Payable, EI Payable, Income Tax Payable); credit Cash for net pay. Entry 2 (employer's own payroll cost): debit Employer Payroll Expense; credit CPP Payable (employer's share), EI Payable (employer's share at 1.4× the employee's), WSIB Payable, and other accrued benefits. Periodically the company remits the combined Payables to the CRA, debiting each Payable and crediting Cash.

The CPP and EI rates change every January 1. For BAT4M problems the rates given in the question are the operative rates — students should use them rather than memorize. Income-tax withholding uses CRA tax tables that combine federal and provincial brackets; for exam problems the income tax amount is usually given.

  • Gross pay: earnings before any deduction.
  • Statutory deductions: mandatory withholdings — CPP, EI, federal & provincial income tax.
  • Net pay: the cash actually paid to the employee.
  • Employer's share: CPP (1:1 match), EI (1.4× employee), WSIB, vacation, EHT — paid by the employer in addition to gross pay.
  • Remittance: the periodic payment to the CRA, due monthly or quarterly.

Example: Employee Maria earns \$ 1,000 gross. Assume CPP 5.95% of pensionable earnings, EI 1.66% of insurable earnings, income tax \$ 130.

Employee deductions: CPP \$ 59.50; EI \$ 16.60; Tax \$ 130.00; total \$ 206.10. Net pay \$ 1,000 − \$ 206.10 = \$ 793.90.

Entry 1 (employee payroll): Salaries Expense \$ 1,000; CPP Payable \$ 59.50; EI Payable \$ 16.60; Income Tax Payable \$ 130; Cash \$ 793.90.

Employer's share: CPP \$ 59.50; EI \( 16.60 \times 1.4 = \$ 23.24 \). Plus WSIB say 1% of gross = \$ 10.

Entry 2 (employer cost): Employer Payroll Expense \$ 92.74; CPP Payable \$ 59.50; EI Payable \$ 23.24; WSIB Payable \$ 10.00.

  • Forgetting the second entry — the employer's portion is a real expense and must be recognized.
  • Recording the employer's share as a deduction from gross pay — those are two separate streams.
  • Confusing CPP and EI rates: CPP is 1:1 employer/employee; EI is 1.4× employer share.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B4.1 — "compute gross pay, statutory deductions (CPP, EI, federal/provincial tax), and net pay; record payroll journal entries."
