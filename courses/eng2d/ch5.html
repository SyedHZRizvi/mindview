📺 5.1 What Is a Media Text?
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A media text is any text — verbal, visual, audio, or multimodal — produced and distributed through mass-media channels (broadcast, print, film, web, social platforms). In Strand D of ENG2D, you study media texts the same way you study literary texts in Strand B: you analyse their conventions, their messages (overt and implied), their audiences, and the choices their creators have made. The key insight of media literacy is that no media text is "natural" or "neutral" — every text is a construction, and every construction reflects choices.

The basic media-literacy questions are five: Who created this text? What techniques are used to attract and hold attention? How might different audiences understand it differently? What values, lifestyles, and points of view are represented or omitted? Why was this text created — to inform, persuade, entertain, sell? These are sometimes called the "five key questions" of media literacy (the Centre for Media Literacy formulation). They work for any text from a billboard to a viral video to a Sunday-night news broadcast.

Media texts use codes and conventions specific to their form. Film has its own grammar: shot type (close-up, medium, long shot, extreme close-up), camera angle (high angle to suggest weakness, low angle to suggest power, eye-level to suggest equality), lighting (high-key for cheerful, low-key for dramatic, side-lighting for ambiguity), sound (diegetic, occurring within the world of the film, vs. non-diegetic, like music added in editing), editing (cuts, fades, dissolves, montage). Print advertising has its own: typography, colour palette, image cropping, headline / body-copy / call-to-action structure. Becoming literate in these codes is the first step toward decoding a media text.

One useful exercise: take a 30-second ad and pause it every 5 seconds. Each frame has been chosen. Ask why this shot, why this angle, why this colour, why this sound. Most ad creators have spent more time on those choices than a film director has on individual cuts; nothing in the frame is accidental. The same exercise can be performed on a news photo, a social-media post, or a film poster. The more you slow down to ask "why this choice?", the more visible the construction becomes — and the harder it is to be persuaded by the surface effect alone. Media literacy does not make you cynical; it makes you a more careful audience. You can still be moved by a film or compelled by an ad — but you understand why, and you choose whether to accept the message.

  • Media text — any text distributed through mass media.
  • Codes / Conventions — the rules and patterns of a media form.
  • Construction — the deliberate choices a media creator makes.
  • Overt message — what the text explicitly says or shows.
  • Implied message — what the text suggests but does not state.
  • Camera angle — high (subject weak), low (subject powerful), eye-level (equal).
  • Shot type — close-up, medium, long shot, extreme close-up.
  • Diegetic / Non-diegetic sound — inside the story world / added in editing.
  • Mise-en-scène — everything in the frame: setting, props, lighting, costume, blocking.

Worked Example — Decoding a Cosmetic Ad

Take a typical perfume ad: a model in close-up, soft side-lighting, low-key blue tones, slow-motion movement, a barely audible whispered voice-over of the brand name. The five questions reveal the construction. Who? A luxury cosmetics brand with a budget for high-production cinematography. What techniques? Close-up framing for intimacy; side-lighting for ambiguity and depth; slow-motion to make the model's gesture feel sacred and timeless. How might audiences differ? Teenagers may absorb the implied promise of glamour; adults may recognise the codes from decades of similar ads; critics may notice the absence of any explicit claim about the product. What is represented / omitted? Wealth, leisure, and a specific (usually narrow) standard of beauty; race, age, and economic diversity are usually absent. Why? To associate the brand with luxury feelings, not to inform about ingredients or function. Decoding this ad does not make it less effective — but it makes it visible as a construction, and lets a Grade 10 audience choose whether to accept the implied promise.

  • Treating media texts as "natural" rather than constructed.
  • Asking only about overt messages and ignoring the implied ones.
  • Forgetting that codes vary by medium (a film code is not a print code).
  • Confusing aesthetic preference with critical analysis (liking it is not analysing it).
  • Skipping the "who created this and why" question.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.2 — "interpret media texts, identifying and explaining the overt and implied messages they convey."

📢 5.2 Advertising Techniques
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Advertising is the most studied media form because it makes its persuasive intent explicit. Every ad has a goal — to make you remember a brand, want a product, or take an action. Advertisers use a recurring toolkit of techniques, most of which are at least a century old, refined for whatever medium currently reaches the most people. Learning to name the techniques is the first step in resisting them when you choose to and accepting them when you choose to.

The most common techniques include bandwagon (everyone is doing it, you should too — "Join the millions"); testimonial (a celebrity, expert, or "ordinary person" endorses the product); glittering generality (vague positive language without specifics — "Premium quality. Total satisfaction."); plain folks (the advertiser appears as one of "us," not them); name-calling (associating a competitor with negative qualities); card stacking (presenting only favourable evidence, omitting unfavourable); transfer (linking the product to a positive symbol that has nothing to do with the product — flag, mountain, baby, puppy); repetition (saying the brand name often enough to lodge it in memory); and scarcity / urgency ("Limited time only!", "While supplies last!").

Modern digital advertising adds techniques specific to its medium. Native advertising disguises ads as journalism (articles labelled "sponsored content" or "promoted"). Influencer endorsement uses the apparent authenticity of a social-media personality, often without the formal-ad signals consumers learned to recognise in TV ads. Behavioural targeting uses data about your past activity to serve ads tailored to your inferred interests, which is why the same product follows you across different websites. A/B testing lets advertisers test multiple versions of the same ad on different audience segments to optimise click-through rates. None of these techniques is inherently bad — but Grade 10 students should learn to recognise them, because the techniques rely on your not recognising them. An ad that you recognise as an ad is less effective than one that arrives disguised as content.

Aristotle's three appeals (Unit 2) apply to advertising as well. Ads using logos argue from logic: "Our toothpaste removes 99% of plaque. Clinical studies confirm." Ads using pathos argue from emotion: a child reuniting with a parent because of a long-distance phone plan. Ads using ethos argue from credibility: a doctor in a white coat, a professional athlete in branded gear. Most strong ads combine all three. The toothpaste ad above uses logos (statistics), ethos (the implied dentist), and pathos (the white smile of the model). When you analyse an ad, name the techniques, name the appeals, and explain what assumptions the ad asks you to make. The hidden assumptions are usually where the persuasion lives. By the time you can name the assumption, you have already begun resisting the persuasion — or accepting it on your own terms rather than the advertiser's.

  • Bandwagon — appeal to popularity.
  • Testimonial — endorsement by celebrity, expert, or "ordinary person."
  • Glittering generality — vague positive language without specifics.
  • Plain folks — appearing as "one of us."
  • Transfer — linking to an unrelated positive symbol.
  • Scarcity / Urgency — "Limited time," "Last chance," "Only X left."
  • Native advertising — paid content disguised as editorial.
  • Influencer marketing — endorsement by a social-media personality.
  • Behavioural targeting — ads tailored using data about past activity.
  • Ethos / Pathos / Logos — credibility / emotion / logic appeals.

Worked Example — Analysing a Soft-Drink Ad

A 30-second ad shows young people at a beach party, ocean visible, sun setting, music up-tempo, all of them drinking the brand's soda. The brand name appears in a final shot with a quick tagline like "Taste the moment." Analysis: Techniques — bandwagon (everyone is drinking it), transfer (sunset + beach + friends = brand), plain folks (the people are young and ordinary, not celebrities), repetition (brand name visible in nearly every shot). Appeals — pathos dominates (joy, friendship, freedom); ethos is in the implied "people like you do this"; logos is virtually absent (no claims about taste or ingredients). Hidden assumption — that drinking this brand will make your life feel like the ad. Omission — anything about sugar content, manufacturing, or environmental impact. A Grade 10 paragraph would name the techniques and explain that the ad makes no factual claims about the product because factual claims are not what soft-drink ads persuade through; they persuade through associating the brand with the feeling the audience already wants.

  • Listing techniques without explaining what each accomplishes.
  • Treating ads as bad — they are persuasive texts, not enemies.
  • Forgetting to ask what the ad omits.
  • Confusing the brand with the feeling the ad sells.
  • Naming only one of the three appeals when most ads combine them.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D2.1 — "identify a wide variety of forms of media texts and explain how the conventions and techniques associated with them are used to create meaning."

📰 5.3 News vs Opinion — Critical Reading of Journalism
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Journalism is the daily report on what is happening in the world, but the word covers very different kinds of writing — and a media-literate reader needs to tell them apart. The basic distinction is between news (which aims to report verified facts), analysis (which interprets the news), opinion / editorial (which argues a position), and commentary / columns (an individual writer's perspective). All four can appear in the same newspaper or website, but they have different standards and different purposes.

A traditional news story follows the inverted pyramid structure: the most important information first (the lead — the "five W's" of who, what, when, where, why), with less important details as the article continues. The structure exists so a reader who reads only the first paragraph still gets the most important facts. News stories use neutral diction wherever possible ("the legislation was passed," not "the long-overdue legislation was finally passed"), attribute every claim to a source ("according to Statistics Canada," "the mayor said"), and separate fact from interpretation.

Opinion writing — editorials, columns, op-eds — has a different purpose. It argues. A column by a regular writer (like an opinion columnist at The Globe and Mail or The Toronto Star) makes a case, uses evidence selectively, and reflects the writer's perspective. The piece is labelled "Opinion" or "Editorial" at the top to signal this to the reader. The label is the contract: the reader knows they are getting argument, not just report. A serious problem in 21st-century media is that the labels are not always present, and articles that look like news may actually be opinion in disguise — or vice versa.

A media-literate reader uses a few quick checks. SIFT, developed by Mike Caulfield, gives four moves: Stop (do not share before you check), Investigate the source (who is this outlet, who is the writer?), Find better coverage (do reputable sources confirm the story?), Trace claims, quotes, and media to the original context (is the quotation accurate? is the photograph from this event?). Lateral reading means opening new tabs to check a claim, rather than evaluating a source by what it says about itself. Fact-checkers do not read top-to-bottom — they open new tabs and check the source against other sources. These habits are the difference between informed citizens and people who spread misinformation without intending to. They take less than three minutes per article. Build the habit; the cost of getting it wrong, especially in a connected world, is rarely zero.

  • News — report of verified facts, neutral diction, attributed sources.
  • Analysis — interpretation of news; often labelled as such.
  • Editorial / Opinion / Op-ed — argument from a labelled perspective.
  • Inverted pyramid — most important facts first, then less important.
  • Five W's — Who, What, When, Where, Why (plus How).
  • Lead — opening paragraph of a news story.
  • Source attribution — naming who said or provided each piece of information.
  • Primary source — original document, photograph, recording.
  • Secondary source — analysis or reporting based on primary sources.
  • SIFT — Stop, Investigate, Find better coverage, Trace to original.
  • Lateral reading — checking a source by opening new tabs about it.

Worked Example — Applying SIFT to a Viral Claim

A social-media post shares a screenshot claiming "Scientists confirm: 12 years until global catastrophe." Apply SIFT. Stop — do not share yet. Investigate the source — who is the "scientist," what publication, what date? Often the post will have stripped the original context. Find better coverage — search the claim in reputable news outlets and the IPCC website. (The "12 years" figure is a popularised version of a 2018 IPCC special report finding that limiting warming to 1.5 °C requires emission cuts of about 45% by 2030 — not a deadline for "catastrophe.") Trace to original — read the IPCC summary itself. By the end of three minutes, a Grade 10 reader has moved from a viral oversimplification to a defensible understanding of what the climate science actually says. The habit transfers: every viral claim is worth three minutes before you pass it on.

  • Confusing opinion writing with news reporting.
  • Reading only the headline and not the article.
  • Evaluating a source by what it says about itself (vertical reading) instead of lateral reading.
  • Sharing before checking — emotional engagement before verification.
  • Trusting all "studies" equally — research quality varies dramatically.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "evaluate how effectively information, ideas, issues, and opinions are communicated in media texts and decide whether the texts achieve their intended purposes."

📱 5.4 Social-Media Literacy
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Social media is the dominant media environment for Grade 10 students. It is also the most algorithmically shaped, the most personally addictive, and the most poorly understood by the people using it. Media literacy in the social-media era is no longer optional — it is fundamental to citizenship, mental health, and the ability to learn.

The core insight is that what you see on a social-media platform is not "the world" or even "what your friends are saying" — it is what the platform's algorithm has decided to show you. The algorithm optimises for engagement (likes, comments, shares, time-on-platform) because engagement produces ad revenue. Content that engages strongly — outrage, fear, awe, indignation — is amplified because it works for the algorithm, not because it is more true or more important. A study by MIT in 2018 found that false news spreads roughly six times faster on Twitter than true news; falsity is sometimes engineered specifically for engagement.

The filter bubble is the personalised set of content the algorithm has curated for you, based on what you have liked or watched in the past. Two students sitting next to each other in class can see entirely different versions of the same platform. The filter bubble feels comfortable because it mostly shows you things you already agree with; this is also why it narrows your view of the world. The echo chamber is the social version of the same phenomenon — when the people you follow share your views, and disagreement is filtered out, your sense of "what most people think" becomes a measurement of your own bubble rather than of public opinion.

Several habits help. Diversify your inputs — follow accounts you disagree with (in good faith). Resist outrage — content designed to make you angry is usually content designed to be shared. Lateral-read viral claims — open new tabs, check primary sources, look at multiple outlets. Mind the timing — old footage, old quotes, and old photographs are routinely re-posted as if they were new (the "context collapse" of social media). Recognise reposted memes — many of the most-shared political claims on social media originate from anonymous accounts and are designed to look like grassroots speech but are produced by paid campaigns. None of this is a counsel of paranoia — social media has genuine value for friendship, learning, art, and political organising. But it has been engineered to be persuasive in ways the engineers themselves have, in many cases, admitted are harmful. A Grade 10 reader of media texts treats the platform as text — constructed, motivated, designed — and reads it with the same critical attention applied to a newspaper headline or a Shakespearean sonnet. Different form, same disciplined attention.

  • Algorithm — the platform's rules for deciding what to show you.
  • Engagement — measurable interactions (likes, comments, shares, watch time).
  • Filter bubble — personalised content that narrows your view.
  • Echo chamber — a social environment where dissent is filtered out.
  • Confirmation bias — the tendency to favour evidence that confirms what you already believe.
  • Context collapse — old or out-of-context material recirculated as if new.
  • Misinformation — false content shared without intent to deceive.
  • Disinformation — false content shared with intent to deceive.
  • Influencer — a social-media personality with a paying audience.
  • Lateral reading — checking a source by opening new tabs about it.

Worked Example — Reading a Viral Video on a Social Platform

A 15-second clip appears on TikTok or Instagram, captioned with strong emotional language ("UNBELIEVABLE — watch what this teacher did!"). Apply media-literacy questions. Who created this and why? Almost always, the uploader is not the person filmed; the uploader is harvesting an emotional clip from elsewhere. What is omitted? The footage before and after the clip; the full context of what was happening; the identities and consent of the people filmed. What does the algorithm gain? Engagement from outrage, sharing, comments. What is the likely outcome? A teacher is misidentified or denounced based on 15 seconds; the real story is forgotten when the next outrage cycle arrives. A media-literate response: pause, lateral-read, check whether reputable outlets have covered the story, look for the longer footage, then decide whether the clip is worth sharing. The pause itself is often the entire skill.

  • Treating the algorithm's feed as "what most people are saying."
  • Sharing emotionally charged content without verification.
  • Confusing "I saw it on social media" with "it happened."
  • Following only accounts you agree with.
  • Forgetting that influencers are usually paid (formally or informally) by brands.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D1.3 — "evaluate how effectively information, ideas, issues, and opinions are communicated in media texts and decide whether the texts achieve their intended purposes."

🎬 5.5 Creating an Original Media Text
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Strand D culminates in a creation task: students plan and produce an original media text — for example, a public-service announcement, a movie poster, an infographic, a news article, a podcast clip, or a short video. The creation task is not just about producing a polished artefact; it is about applying everything Unit 5 (and the whole course) has taught about audience, purpose, form, and convention.

The first step is planning. Identify the topic, the target audience, the purpose, and the form. The topic is what the text is about. The audience is who you are addressing — and "everyone" is not an audience. A media text aimed at Grade 9 students will use very different conventions from one aimed at retirees; identifying the audience is the design decision that controls everything else. The purpose is what you want the audience to think, feel, or do — to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to provoke reflection. The form is the chosen medium (PSA video, poster, podcast). Filling in these four lets you start making informed design choices instead of guessing.

The second step is storyboarding (for video) or thumbnailing (for visual print media). A storyboard is a frame-by-frame sketch of the planned text — shots, on-screen text, voice-over, music cues. A thumbnail is a small rough sketch of the visual layout. Both exist to test ideas cheaply before committing to a finished product. Sketches do not need to be artistic; they need to record decisions. "Close-up of a hand putting a plastic bottle in a bin. Text overlay: 'You can help.' Music: gentle piano." That is enough detail to plan production.

The third step is production — making the actual artefact — and the fourth is reflection. After production, write a short rationale explaining the choices: who is the audience, what techniques did you use to reach them, what did you choose to include and exclude, and what ethical considerations shaped your work. Ethics matter especially in media creation: are you accurately representing your subject? Have you fact-checked claims? Do you have permission to use images of identifiable people? Are you exaggerating or fear-mongering? Reflective work shows you understand that media literacy is not only critical reception but responsible creation. The most valuable thing Strand D can do is make you both a better reader and a better, more ethical writer of media. The two skills are halves of the same competency. By the end of Grade 10, you should be able to look at a PSA your peers made and ask the same five questions you asked of a perfume ad — and you should be able to make a PSA that holds up to those same questions. That is the goal of the unit.

  • Audience analysis — identifying who you are addressing and what they bring to the text.
  • Purpose — what you want the audience to think, feel, or do.
  • Form — chosen medium (poster, video, podcast, infographic, article).
  • Storyboard — frame-by-frame sketch of a planned video.
  • Thumbnail — small rough sketch of a print or web layout.
  • Production — making the artefact.
  • Rationale / Reflection — written explanation of design choices.
  • Ethics — accurate representation, fact-checking, consent, no fear-mongering.
  • Call to action — the action the audience is invited to take.

Worked Example — Planning a 30-second PSA on Phone Use While Driving

Topic: Distracted driving by texting. Audience: New Ontario drivers (ages 16–18). Purpose: Persuasion — get them to put the phone away while driving. Form: 30-second PSA video for social media (Instagram Reels / TikTok).

Storyboard: Shot 1 (5 s): close-up of a phone face-down in the passenger seat; engine ambient sound. Shot 2 (10 s): driver's-eye view of road; phone vibrates; text overlay reads "It can wait." Shot 3 (10 s): same shot — driver picks up phone, eyes leave road; cut to black; sound of tires screeching (off-screen). Shot 4 (5 s): black screen; white text: "1 second of texting at 60 km/h = 17 metres travelled blind. Don't be that driver."

Rationale: Quiet opening to build attention; pathos (the screech, the implied crash) without graphic imagery; logos (the 17 metres statistic); ethos (no fear-mongering, no graphic footage). The PSA is honest about the data and uses the social-media short-form vertical video conventions the target audience already watches. The form fits the audience.

  • Skipping audience analysis ("everyone" is not an audience).
  • Producing the artefact before storyboarding or planning.
  • Forgetting the ethics check — accuracy, fact-checking, consent.
  • Confusing high production value with effective communication.
  • Producing without writing a rationale; reflection is part of the assessment.

Curriculum expectation: Strand D, Expectation D3.4 — "produce media texts for several different purposes and audiences, using appropriate forms, conventions, and techniques."
