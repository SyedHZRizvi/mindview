📝 2.1 The Writing Process
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Writing is not a single act but a recursive process — meaning you move back and forth between its stages, not straight through them in order. The traditional five stages are prewriting, drafting, revising, editing, and publishing. Strong Grade 10 writers spend more time on prewriting and revising than weak writers do, because those are the stages where most of the thinking happens. The drafting stage often feels like the "real" writing, but the work of figuring out what you want to say happens before drafting, and the work of refining what you actually said happens after.

Prewriting means generating, exploring, and organising ideas before you begin a first draft. Useful prewriting strategies include brainstorming, mind-mapping, freewriting (writing without stopping for a set time), outlining, and the journalistic five W's (who, what, when, where, why). Different writers prefer different strategies for different tasks. The point of prewriting is to test ideas cheaply — on a scratch page — before committing to a paragraph structure that may be hard to undo.

Revision is different from editing. Revision ("re-vision") means looking again at the big-picture choices: thesis, argument, paragraph order, evidence selection, voice. Editing happens after revision and focuses on the sentence level: word choice, grammar, punctuation, spelling. New writers often confuse the two and try to edit a paragraph that should have been re-organised. Always revise before you edit — there is no point fixing comma errors in a paragraph that will be deleted.

One of the most powerful habits is to draft fast and revise slow. A draft is a discovery; you usually do not know what you think until you have tried to say it. So write a rough first draft without stopping to fix things — let the ideas spill onto the page even if the sentences are messy. Then put it away for at least a few hours (overnight is better), and return as if you were a different reader. The distance lets you spot weak claims, unclear connections, missing evidence, and repeated phrases. Reading aloud during revision is another useful trick: your ear catches what your eye approves. Most Grade 10 essays improve more from a careful second draft than from any amount of editing on the first.

  • Prewriting — generating, exploring, and organising ideas before drafting.
  • Drafting — writing a complete (rough) version of the piece.
  • Revising — re-seeing the piece at the level of argument, structure, and voice.
  • Editing — fixing sentence-level grammar, mechanics, and word choice.
  • Publishing — preparing the final, presentation-ready version (formatting, citation, layout).
  • Brainstorming / Mind-mapping — visual idea-generation strategies.
  • Freewriting — writing non-stop for a fixed time without editing.
  • Outline — a hierarchical sketch of the essay's structure.

Worked Example — Mini Mind-Map for a Romeo and Juliet Essay

Topic: Why does the Friar marry Romeo and Juliet so quickly?

Centre: "Friar Lawrence's haste."

Branches: his hope of ending the feud; his role as adult/parent figure; his earlier scolding of Romeo for loving Rosaline; his herb / poison knowledge (foreshadowing his later miscalculation).

Sub-branches: specific quotations: "For this alliance may so happy prove, / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3); "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3 — ironic foreshadowing).

This 10-minute mind-map gives the writer a working thesis (the Friar's haste creates the tragedy) and three evidence clusters (his motive, his hypocrisy, his foreshadowing) — enough to draft a focused essay.

  • Editing the first paragraph for spelling before you know your thesis.
  • Mind-mapping for thirty seconds and pretending it is enough prewriting.
  • Treating revision as proofreading — they are different stages.
  • Trying to draft a perfect first paragraph before drafting the rest.
  • Skipping the "put it away overnight" step before revising.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "generate, expand, explore, and focus ideas for potential writing tasks, using a variety of strategies and resources."

🧱 2.2 Paragraph Structure (PEEL / TEXAS)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A paragraph is a single complete unit of thought — usually one sub-claim, supported by evidence, with analysis that links it back to the thesis. The most common Grade 10 paragraph error is what teachers call "the floating quotation": dropping a quotation in without a signal phrase, without integration into a sentence, and without explanation. The PEEL and TEXAS scaffolds exist to prevent this and to make the analysis explicit.

PEEL stands for Point, Evidence, Explain, Link. The Point is your topic sentence — the sub-claim of the paragraph. The Evidence is a direct quotation or specific textual reference. The Explain step is the analytical work — interpreting how and why the evidence proves the point. The Link is a closing sentence that connects this paragraph back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph. TEXAS is a longer cousin: Topic sentence, Example, eXplain, Analyse, So-what — it just spells out the "explain" step into two: explain what the evidence shows, then analyse why it matters.

Whichever scaffold you use, the longest part of a paragraph should be the explanation/analysis, not the quotation. If your evidence is two sentences and your explanation is one, you are summarising; if your evidence is one sentence and your explanation is three, you are analysing. A quotation is not self-explaining — it is a piece of evidence that needs you to interpret it for the reader.

Embedded quotations integrate the quoted material grammatically into your own sentence. Compare two versions: "Romeo says, 'Juliet is the sun.' This shows love." That is weak — the quotation is floating and the explanation is empty. Now: "Romeo's declaration that 'Juliet is the sun' (2.2.3) reorients his world so completely that, by the end of the scene, he is willing to renounce his family name to be hers." The quotation is woven into your sentence with a signal phrase ("Romeo's declaration that"), and the rest of the sentence does the analytical work. Good embedded quotations make the rest of your paragraph easier to write, because they leave you with a clear interpretive sentence already started.

  • Topic sentence — the analytical sub-claim of the paragraph.
  • Evidence — direct quotation or specific textual reference.
  • Signal phrase — words introducing and attributing a quotation.
  • Embedded quotation — quoted material grammatically integrated into your sentence.
  • Explanation / Analysis — interpreting how the evidence proves the point.
  • Link / So-what — connecting the paragraph back to the thesis.
  • PEEL / TEXAS — paragraph scaffolds for evidence-based analysis.

Worked Example — A PEEL Paragraph on Friar Lawrence

Point: Friar Lawrence's quick decision to marry Romeo and Juliet is the play's first step toward tragedy. Evidence: When Romeo confesses his love for Juliet, the Friar responds, "For this alliance may so happy prove, / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91–2). Explain: The Friar imagines a political solution — a marriage that will end the family feud — and lets that hope override his pastoral caution. His own earlier warning, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94), becomes ironic because he himself runs fast. Link: By rushing the wedding, the Friar trades careful counsel for political calculation, setting up the chain of secrecy that will end in two deaths.

  • Floating quotations dropped in without a signal phrase.
  • Paragraphs where evidence is longer than analysis.
  • Ending the paragraph with the quotation, not the interpretation.
  • Topic sentences that announce the topic instead of stating a claim.
  • Mixing PEEL and TEXAS midway, so the paragraph never reaches the analysis.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.2 — "establish an identifiable voice in their writing, modifying it for different forms, audiences, and purposes."

📚 2.3 The Literary-Analysis Essay
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A literary-analysis essay is an extended argument about a text — usually 500–800 words at the Grade 10 level. It makes a debatable claim about what the text does (the thesis), proves the claim through close reading of three or four passages (the body paragraphs), and ties everything together at the end (the conclusion). It is not a book report — it does not summarise the plot — and it is not a personal response — it does not focus on how the text made you feel. Its centre of gravity is the text itself.

The classic structure is the five-paragraph essay: introduction, three body paragraphs, conclusion. The introduction does three things in order: hooks the reader, supplies just enough context (author, title, genre, the situation in the text), and states the thesis. The three body paragraphs each prove one sub-claim using PEEL. The conclusion synthesises — it does not just restate the thesis, but explains what the argument as a whole reveals about the text or about the reader's experience of the text.

Use the present tense for literary analysis. Even though Shakespeare wrote in the 1590s, when you describe the play you write "Romeo sees Juliet at the ball," not "Romeo saw Juliet at the ball." The literary present tense reflects the convention that the text is always happening, every time it is read. Use the past tense only for historical fact (Shakespeare wrote, the Globe burned, Elizabethan audiences attended).

Avoid the first person ("I think," "I believe") and the second person ("you can see") in formal literary analysis. They make the argument sound tentative and shift attention from the text to the writer. Write in the third person, with confident analytical verbs: argues, reveals, frames, complicates, critiques, destabilises. Compare: "I think the play is about love" (tentative, vague) with "The play frames love as both salvation and self-destruction, holding the two together until the lovers cannot" (confident, specific). The same idea, but written in a register that earns the reader's trust. Grade 10 essays improve quickly when writers swap out hedge-words ("maybe," "kind of," "sort of") for direct analytical verbs.

  • Introduction — hook, context, thesis (in that order).
  • Body paragraph — one sub-claim per paragraph, PEEL structure.
  • Conclusion — synthesis, not restatement; one sentence on broader significance.
  • Literary present — using present tense to describe textual events.
  • Third-person voice — avoiding "I" and "you" in formal analysis.
  • Analytical verb — strong, specific verb naming what the text does.
  • Transition — phrase linking one paragraph to the next ("similarly," "in contrast," "by Act 3").

Worked Example — Introduction Paragraph for a Romeo-and-Juliet Essay

Hook: The most famous line of Romeo and Juliet calls its protagonists "star-crossed lovers" (Prologue, 6), but the play that follows offers very little evidence that the stars are at fault. Context: Shakespeare's tragedy, written around 1595, frames its tale of teenage romance and family feud as fated — but the action consistently shows adult failure and rushed decisions, not cosmic interference. Thesis: The play uses the rhetoric of fate to mask its real causes: a feud sustained by Capulet and Montague, a marriage rushed by Friar Lawrence, and a delay of news that anyone with five extra minutes could have undone. By blaming the stars, Shakespeare gives his audience the comfort of fate; by dramatising the choices, he gives them the discomfort of responsibility.

  • Beginning with "Throughout history" or "Since the beginning of time."
  • Putting the thesis as the first sentence with no context.
  • Plot summary in the body paragraphs.
  • Conclusions that simply repeat the introduction.
  • Mixing past and present tenses across the essay.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "write for different purposes and audiences using a variety of literary, informational, and graphic forms."

⚖️ 2.4 The Persuasive Essay
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A persuasive essay argues that the reader should accept a specific position or take a specific action. Where a literary-analysis essay argues about a text, a persuasive essay argues about an idea, policy, or issue (school start times, the ethics of social media, voting age). It uses evidence (statistics, expert opinion, examples, anecdotes) and the three classical appeals — ethos, pathos, and logos — to bring the reader to its side.

Aristotle's three appeals describe the basic ways argument works. Ethos appeals to the credibility or character of the speaker — quoting a respected expert, demonstrating fairness, presenting yourself as someone worth listening to. Pathos appeals to the audience's emotions — anecdotes, vivid examples, language that makes the audience feel hope, fear, anger, or pride. Logos appeals to logic and reason — statistics, evidence, cause-and-effect chains, clear definitions. Most persuasive writing combines all three; the strongest persuasive essays know which appeal each paragraph is leading with.

A persuasive essay needs a clear counterargument paragraph. Acknowledging the strongest opposing view shows the reader you have considered the issue from multiple sides — which itself is an ethos move (you sound fair) and a logos move (you can refute the strongest objection, not just a strawman). The standard structure is: state the opposing view fairly, concede what is reasonable about it, then refute the part that does not hold up. This is more persuasive than ignoring the opposition.

A common Grade 10 trap is reaching for pathos without supporting it. "We should ban single-use plastics because the planet is dying!" is pure pathos with no evidence. Strong persuasive writing pairs every emotional appeal with at least one piece of evidence: "Canadians throw away three million tonnes of plastic each year, and only 9 percent of it is recycled (Government of Canada, 2019). The cost of inaction is not abstract — it is the wildlife footage of seabirds with stomachs full of bottle caps." That paragraph combines logos (the 9 percent statistic) with pathos (the seabirds) and gains both kinds of strength. The reader feels and is persuaded. The persuasive essay's hardest skill is finding the balance — too much pathos sounds manipulative, too much logos sounds inhuman, too much ethos sounds preachy. Watch the proportions.

  • Position / claim — the specific stance the essay argues for.
  • Ethos — appeal to credibility/character.
  • Pathos — appeal to emotion.
  • Logos — appeal to logic, evidence, reasoning.
  • Counterargument — the strongest opposing view, stated fairly.
  • Refutation — the response to the counterargument.
  • Call to action — the action the essay urges the reader to take.
  • Rhetorical question — a question that makes its point without expecting an answer.

Worked Example — Persuasive Thesis with All Three Appeals

Topic: Should high schools start later in the morning?

Thesis: Ontario high schools should push first period to 9:00 a.m. because the science of adolescent sleep is conclusive, the academic consequences of early starts are measurable, and the cost of changing bus schedules is one we can pay.

Logos: "The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends a start time no earlier than 8:30 a.m., citing studies showing teenagers' natural sleep cycle does not allow restful sleep before 11:00 p.m. (AAP, 2014)."

Ethos: "Even school boards that have changed start times — including Halton in 2018 — report rising graduation rates and falling tardiness, suggesting the gains outweigh the bus-schedule complaints."

Pathos: "Every parent of a teenager has watched their child stagger out of bed before sunrise, slump over breakfast, and arrive at first period without the alertness any teacher would expect. The cost of pretending this is acceptable is paid one day at a time by tired sixteen-year-olds and the teachers who try to teach them."

  • All pathos and no evidence ("This is just wrong!").
  • All logos and no human stakes ("Statistics show … therefore …").
  • Ignoring the counterargument or strawman-ing it.
  • Rhetorical questions used as filler instead of pivots ("Right?", "Isn't that obvious?").
  • Call-to-action that is vague ("We need to do something").

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "write for different purposes and audiences using a variety of literary, informational, and graphic forms (e.g., literary-analysis paragraph, persuasive essay, short narrative)."

📑 2.5 MLA Citation Basics
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Citation is the academic habit of telling your reader exactly where you got each piece of information. It protects you against accidental plagiarism, lets your reader track your sources, and signals that you are participating in a community of writers who treat one another's ideas with respect. In English class, the standard format is MLA (Modern Language Association), currently in its 9th edition. The MLA system has two parts: in-text citations and a Works Cited list.

In-text citations are short references in parentheses immediately after a quotation or paraphrase. For a print book, the form is (Author's last name, page number) — for example, (Shakespeare 24). For a play with acts and scenes, the convention shifts to act.scene.line, separated by periods: (1.5.45) means Act 1, Scene 5, line 45. For a poem, line numbers go in parentheses: (line 3) or just (3) once you have established you mean line numbers. Web sources use the author's last name and, if useful, a paragraph or section reference.

The Works Cited list goes at the end of the essay, alphabetised by author's last name, with a hanging indent. A book entry is: Last name, First name. Title of Book. Publisher, Year. For example: Shakespeare, William. Romeo and Juliet. Edited by René Weis, Bloomsbury, 2012. A short story or poem in an anthology: Last name, First name. "Title of Story." Title of Anthology, edited by Editor Name, Publisher, Year, pages.

The most common Grade 10 citation mistake is dropping a quotation without citing where it came from. Every direct quotation needs a citation, even if you have already mentioned the work. Paraphrases also need citations when they rely on a specific source. The second most common mistake is what teachers call "patchwriting" — copying a sentence and changing two words while keeping the structure. Patchwriting is plagiarism even with a citation; either quote the source word-for-word with quotation marks and a citation, or read the source, close it, and write the idea in your own words and your own sentence structure. The simplest rule: if you can still hear the original author's voice in the sentence after you have rewritten it, you have not yet rewritten it. Writing in your own voice — and crediting the sources whose ideas helped you — is the goal of good academic citation.

  • In-text citation — short parenthetical reference after a quotation or paraphrase.
  • Works Cited — alphabetical list of sources at the end of the essay.
  • MLA 9 — Modern Language Association style, ninth edition.
  • Hanging indent — first line of each Works Cited entry flush, subsequent lines indented.
  • Plagiarism — passing off another's work as your own, with or without intent.
  • Patchwriting — copying with minimal word changes; counts as plagiarism.
  • Paraphrase — rephrasing a source's idea in your own words and structure; still requires citation.

Worked Example — Citing Romeo and Juliet

In the essay: Romeo's first vision of Juliet replaces his earlier infatuation with Rosaline: "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! / For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5.51–2).

Works Cited entry for the play:

Shakespeare, William. Romeo and Juliet. Edited by René Weis, Bloomsbury, 2012.

Notice four conventions: (a) the line citation uses act.scene.line; (b) the slash separates verse lines inside running prose; (c) the full Works Cited entry lists editor and publisher; (d) the play title is italicised both in-text and in the Works Cited list.

  • Forgetting in-text citation after a quotation.
  • Quoting a play with page numbers instead of act.scene.line.
  • Patchwriting a paraphrase and citing it as if it were original.
  • Missing a Works Cited entry for a source quoted in the essay.
  • Mixing MLA with other styles (APA, Chicago) within a single essay.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.5 — "produce pieces of published work that follow the conventions of the chosen form; credit sources accurately to avoid plagiarism."
