📖 1.1 Literary Devices & Figurative Language
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Literary devices are the tools writers use to make meaning richer, more memorable, and more emotionally precise. Saying "she is graceful" is honest; saying "she moves like water finding its way" is doing extra work — it adds image, motion, and quietness all at once. In Grade 10 we move beyond simply spotting a metaphor or simile to explaining the effect the device produces. A device is not a label you pin on a sentence; it is a mechanism for generating meaning that you should be able to describe.

Figurative language differs from literal language in that it asks the reader to hold two ideas in mind at once. A simile says "A is like B," a metaphor says "A is B," and personification gives human qualities to non-human things. When William Wordsworth describes daffodils as "fluttering and dancing in the breeze," he is not asserting that flowers have feet — he is inviting the reader to see the daffodils as joyful living beings, and that perception is the meaning. Stronger analytical writing always names the device, quotes the words, and explains the effect.

Sound devices are easy to overlook in print but powerful in oral reading. Alliteration (repeated initial consonant sounds — "wild and weary"), assonance (repeated vowel sounds — "lake / fate"), and onomatopoeia (words that imitate sounds — "buzz," "crash") shape tone and mood. Read passages aloud whenever possible: your ear will catch what your eye misses.

Irony is the device that Grade 10 students most often misuse. Verbal irony happens when a speaker says one thing and means another (a friend caught in pouring rain says "lovely weather!"). Situational irony happens when the actual outcome is the opposite of what one would expect (a marriage counsellor gets divorced). Dramatic irony happens when the audience knows something a character does not (in Romeo and Juliet, the audience knows Juliet is only sleeping, but Romeo does not). Students often call any surprise "ironic," but real irony requires a structured gap between two layers of meaning, not just an unexpected twist.

  • Metaphor — direct comparison stating one thing is another ("Time is a thief").
  • Simile — comparison using "like" or "as" ("brave as a lion").
  • Personification — giving human qualities to non-human things ("the wind whispered").
  • Symbol — a concrete image standing for an abstract idea (the dove for peace).
  • Imagery — sensory language appealing to sight, sound, touch, smell, or taste.
  • Hyperbole — deliberate exaggeration ("I've told you a million times").
  • Alliteration — repetition of initial consonant sounds for rhythmic effect.
  • Onomatopoeia — words whose sound suggests their meaning ("hiss," "buzz").
  • Irony — verbal, situational, or dramatic gap between expectation and reality.

Worked Example — Reading a Metaphor in Hughes

Langston Hughes opens "Mother to Son" with the lines:

"Well, son, I'll tell you: / Life for me ain't been no crystal stair."

The metaphor is a "crystal stair." A crystal stair would be smooth, clear, ornamental — easy to climb and pleasing to look at. By saying that life has not been that, the speaker tells her son that her life has been hard, broken, uncertain. Hughes then extends the metaphor — "tacks," "splinters," "boards torn up" — so the staircase becomes a full picture of struggle. A Grade 10 analysis should name the metaphor, quote it, and explain that Hughes uses the staircase to make hard work and persistence visible: she keeps climbing, and so should her son.

  • Naming a device without explaining its effect ("there is a simile").
  • Calling every comparison a metaphor — similes use "like" or "as."
  • Confusing irony with coincidence or surprise.
  • Treating sound devices as "just decoration" — they shape tone and pace.
  • Misquoting — inventing or paraphrasing words inside quotation marks.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "identify a variety of text features and explain how they help communicate meaning."

🔍 1.2 Close Reading of Short Fiction
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Close reading is slow, attentive reading that treats every choice in a story — every word, every comma, every shift in time or setting — as meaningful. Where casual reading asks "what happens?", close reading asks "how does it happen?" and "why these words?". The goal of Grade 10 close reading is to make your interpretation specific enough that a peer who has read the same story would still learn something from your reading.

Active annotation is the basic habit. As you read, underline striking phrases, circle repeated words, bracket changes in setting or tone, and write a one-sentence reaction in the margin every page or two. By the end, your annotations form a map of the story's pressure points. Patterns — what gets repeated, what gets withheld, what gets contradicted — are the doorway to theme.

A theme is not a topic. "Love" is a topic; "Sandra Cisneros's 'Eleven' suggests that growing up is not linear — older feelings live inside us alongside younger ones" is a theme. A theme is a defensible claim about a topic that the text makes. Themes are arguable, evidence-based statements, not single words.

Point of view controls how much we know and whom we trust. A first-person narrator (the "I" in Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart") shares experience directly but may be biased, deceived, or — as Poe shows us — frighteningly unreliable. A third-person limited narrator stays outside the action grammatically (using "he/she/they") but follows one character's mind. A third-person omniscient narrator can move between many minds. Identifying the point of view is the first analytical move when you start reading a short story, because every other inference depends on knowing whose perception you are inside.

  • Annotation — written marks recording a reader's responses, questions, and noticings.
  • Theme — a defensible claim about a topic the text develops.
  • Point of view — first person, second person, third-person limited, third-person omniscient.
  • Setting — time and place treated as a meaningful force, not backdrop.
  • Plot stages — exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution.
  • Conflict — internal (within a character) or external (character vs. character/society/nature).
  • Characterisation — direct (told) or indirect (shown through speech, action, thought).
  • Inference — a conclusion drawn from textual evidence plus reader's reasoning.

Worked Example — Reading the Opening of "The Tell-Tale Heart"

Edgar Allan Poe begins his story with:

"TRUE!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am! but why will you say that I am mad?"

A close reader notices: (1) the all-caps "TRUE!" begs the reader to believe him, which already invites distrust; (2) the dashes and stutters ("very, very dreadfully nervous") mimic agitated speech; (3) the rhetorical question accuses the reader of doubting the narrator before the reader has done so. The story has not started — the murder has not been described — and yet we already suspect the narrator is unreliable. This is Poe's gift: he constructs a voice that betrays itself in its very first sentence. A Grade 10 analytical paragraph would quote the opening, name the techniques (punctuation, repetition, rhetorical question), and explain that they create the unreliable narrator the story depends on.

  • Plot summary instead of analysis ("Then she did this, then she did that").
  • Theme statements that are single words ("love," "fear") — themes are claims, not topics.
  • Treating the narrator's view as identical to the author's.
  • Ignoring the title — it is part of the text.
  • Reading once and stopping — close reading requires re-reading.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "make and explain inferences about texts, supporting their explanations with stated and implied ideas from the text."

🎭 1.3 Reading Poetry
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Poetry compresses meaning into the smallest possible space. Every word matters, every line break matters, every silence matters. Where prose can take a paragraph to set a mood, a poem may take a single line — and that one line will repay close attention more generously than ten pages of casual reading. The Grade 10 poetry reader's task is to slow down: to read aloud, to count beats, to notice where the poem turns.

The basic English-language metre is the iamb — an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one (da-DUM). A line with five iambs is iambic pentameter, the metre Shakespeare uses for most of his plays and sonnets. When poets break their pattern — a heavy stressed-stressed foot called a spondee, an extra syllable, an early caesura — the break is a signal. Train yourself to read for the surprises, not the regularities.

Form names a poem's argument before the words do. A sonnet promises a turn (called the volta) somewhere in its 14 lines. A ballad tells a story in rhymed quatrains. Free verse breaks the inheritance of regular form to claim its own shape. When you meet a new poem, look at the title, the shape on the page, the rhyme scheme, and the metre before you decide what the poem is "about." Form tells you what the poem is trying to do.

Reading aloud is the most underused poetry skill. Print collapses speech onto paper; reading aloud restores the missing dimension. You hear the way a line break creates a tiny pause that the printed page hides. You hear alliteration and assonance physically as your mouth shapes them. You feel the rhythm in your breath. Even when the poem is metrically irregular (Walt Whitman, Maya Angelou), reading aloud reveals the cadence the poet wanted — the breath-line, not the printed line. Make a habit: read every new poem aloud at least once before you analyse it. The second reading is for annotation; the third reading is when you start writing.

  • Iamb / trochee / spondee / dactyl / anapest — the five common metrical feet.
  • Iambic pentameter — five iambs per line; Shakespeare's standard.
  • Enjambment — running a sentence past a line break without punctuation.
  • Caesura — a strong pause within a line, often marked by punctuation.
  • Sonnet (Shakespearean) — 14 lines in three quatrains and a couplet, rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG.
  • Volta — the turn in argument, often at line 9 (Petrarchan) or 13 (Shakespearean).
  • Free verse — verse without regular metre or rhyme scheme.
  • Speaker — the voice of the poem; not necessarily the poet.
  • Tone / mood — speaker's attitude (tone); reader's emotional atmosphere (mood).

Worked Example — Reading "Mother to Son"

Langston Hughes uses free verse and conversational diction to embed an extended metaphor:

"Life for me ain't been no crystal stair. / It's had tacks in it, / And splinters, / And boards torn up."

Notice the way the short lines mimic broken steps. The single-word fragment "And splinters," visually isolated on its own line, gives "splinter" the same painful sharpness in the poem that splinters have on actual stairs. Hughes's choice of free verse rather than a fixed form is part of the message — the speaker's life will not fit the orderly stairs of formal poetry. The dialect ("ain't been no") roots the voice in lived experience. A strong analytical paragraph would identify the extended metaphor, quote it, and explain that the chosen form (free verse, short lines, dialect) enacts the speaker's argument: that life is uneven, broken, but climbable.

  • Paraphrasing a poem and calling the paraphrase the meaning.
  • Confusing the speaker with the poet (Hughes is not "the mother" — he wrote her).
  • Counting syllables wrongly because contractions ("ain't," "I'll") count as one.
  • Treating rhyme as decoration — rhyme controls emphasis and surprise.
  • Reading the poem silently only once, then writing.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.1 — "identify a variety of characteristics of literary, informational, and graphic text forms and explain how they help communicate meaning."

✍️ 1.4 From Paraphrase to Inference and Analysis
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

One of the biggest jumps between Grade 9 and Grade 10 English is the move from paraphrase to inference and analysis. Paraphrase tells the reader what a text says in different words. Inference draws a conclusion from clues. Analysis explains how the text produces its effect and why that matters. All three matter, but a paragraph that ends in paraphrase has not earned its conclusion; a paragraph that ends in analysis has.

A useful test: read what you have written and ask, "Could a careful summary-bot have written this?" If yes, you have paraphrased. If your paragraph names a device, attributes an effect to it, and links the effect to a larger claim, you have analysed. The simplest analytical move is: quote → name technique → explain effect → connect to thesis.

Inference sits between paraphrase and analysis. To infer is to combine textual evidence with your own reasoning. If a character "shoved her hands into her pockets and looked away when her mother asked about school," the text does not say she is hiding something — but the reader infers it. Strong inferences are anchored in specific words ("shoved" is more forceful than "put"; "looked away" implies avoidance). Inferences without textual anchors are guesses.

The PEEL paragraph keeps your analysis honest. Point — your sub-claim, written as a topic sentence. Evidence — a direct quotation, properly integrated. Explain — how and why the evidence supports your point; this is where analysis happens, and it should be longer than the evidence sentence. Link — a sentence that connects this paragraph back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph. PEEL prevents the most common Grade 10 paragraph error: dropping a quotation in and not interpreting it. A paragraph that ends with the quotation has done evidence-gathering, not analysis. A paragraph that ends with your interpretation has done what the form asks.

  • Paraphrase — restating the text's content in different words.
  • Inference — a reasoned conclusion drawn from text + reader's reasoning.
  • Analysis — explaining how and why a textual choice produces its effect.
  • PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explain, Link — a paragraph scaffold.
  • Topic sentence — the analytical claim of a paragraph.
  • Embedded quotation — a quoted phrase grammatically integrated into your sentence.
  • Signal phrase — words that introduce and attribute a quotation.
  • So-what move — the final sentence connecting the analysis back to the thesis.

Worked Example — Same Evidence, Three Levels

The evidence: Romeo says, "But, soft! what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (Romeo and Juliet, 2.2.2–3).

Plot summary: "Romeo sees Juliet at her window and compares her to the sun." This adds nothing the reader did not have.

Paraphrase: "Romeo uses a metaphor to say Juliet is like the sun coming up in the east." Closer, but still restatement.

Inference: "By comparing Juliet to the sun, Romeo suggests that she will now centre his world the way the sun centres day." Adds reasoning.

Analysis: "Shakespeare's metaphor does more than praise Juliet — it announces a new cosmology for Romeo. The sun is what other things orbit; by calling Juliet the sun, Romeo silently demotes Rosaline and reorients his whole emotional universe around this new figure. The metaphor's astronomical scale ('the east') is the play's first warning that his love will not be ordinary or proportional — it will be total, and therefore vulnerable to total loss." Analysis names the device, accounts for its effect, and links to the larger argument.

  • Summarising plot instead of analysing technique.
  • Dropping a quotation in without integrating it grammatically into your sentence.
  • Ending a paragraph at "this shows" without saying what it shows.
  • Confusing the writer with the speaker or narrator.
  • Treating any inference as analysis — analysis explains how, not just what.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "make and explain inferences about texts, supporting their explanations with stated and implied ideas from the text."

🔖 1.5 Building a Thesis Statement
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

A thesis is the central claim of an essay — the single sentence the rest of the essay sets out to prove. In Grade 10 the leap from "topic" to "thesis" is the difference between a basic and a strong essay. A topic is a subject (e.g., "loyalty in Romeo and Juliet"). A thesis is a specific, debatable, supportable claim about that subject (e.g., "In Romeo and Juliet, Shakespeare presents loyalty as a force that protects characters when directed inward to family, but destroys them when redirected toward forbidden love").

A strong thesis has three qualities. First, it is specific — it names the text, the technique, the theme, or the angle. A vague thesis ("This essay will look at Romeo and Juliet") gives the writer nothing to prove. Second, it is debatable — a thoughtful reader could disagree. A fact ("Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy") is not debatable; an interpretation ("Shakespeare's tragedy depends less on fate than on adult failure") is. Third, it is supportable — the writer can actually find textual evidence for it. Asking "What three quotations would I use to prove this?" is the fastest way to test a thesis.

The structure of a strong thesis often takes the form: [Author] uses [technique / pattern] to [accomplish / argue / reveal / critique] [theme]. This pattern is not a straitjacket — it just guarantees that all three required parts are present. Once the pattern feels familiar, you can vary the word order and the verbs to make the thesis sound less mechanical.

One useful refinement: avoid first-person announcements ("I think," "I believe," "In this essay I will"). They make the writer's claim sound tentative and shift the reader's attention from the text to the writer. State the claim as a fact about the text: not "I think Hughes is hopeful about his son's future," but "Hughes ends the poem with the speaker still climbing, leaving the son with a model of persistence rather than a promise of arrival." That construction is more confident and more analytical — and it gives the rest of the essay a clear claim to argue. The single sentence of a thesis is doing a lot of work; it is worth spending real time drafting it, then revising it again after the essay is written.

  • Thesis — the central, debatable, supportable claim of an essay.
  • Topic — the subject area; broader than a thesis.
  • Working thesis — an early draft that may be revised as evidence accumulates.
  • Counterargument — a reasonable alternative reading; a strong thesis acknowledges one.
  • Hook — the opening sentence(s) that draw the reader into the introduction.
  • Roadmap — a sentence after the thesis that previews the essay's body paragraphs (optional in Grade 10).

Worked Example — From Topic to Thesis

Topic: Fate in Romeo and Juliet.

Weak thesis: "In Romeo and Juliet, fate is important." (True, but unspecific and not debatable.)

Working thesis: "Shakespeare uses references to the stars to show that fate controls Romeo and Juliet." (Closer, but vague — what kind of control? Whose fault?)

Strong thesis: "Shakespeare frames Romeo and Juliet as 'star-crossed lovers' in the prologue, but the play's tragedy is produced less by fate than by adult feud and rushed decisions — the stars take the blame for choices the characters make." (Specific, debatable, supportable. A reader could disagree; a writer can prove it with the prologue, the Friar's haste, and Capulet's command to Juliet to marry Paris.)

  • Topics disguised as theses ("This essay is about loyalty").
  • Stating facts no one disputes ("Shakespeare wrote in the 1590s").
  • First-person announcements ("In my opinion …").
  • Theses that promise more than the essay can deliver in 500 words.
  • Forgetting to revise the thesis after writing — by the end, the essay often proves a slightly different claim.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "identify the most important ideas and supporting details in texts and explain how they relate to each other and to the central idea."
