🎬 3.1 Elizabethan Context & Dramatic Conventions
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Shakespeare wrote Romeo and Juliet around 1594–96, when London's theatres were still relatively new institutions. The play was first staged at the Theatre in Shoreditch and later at the Globe (built in 1599). Performances took place in daylight, with no electric lighting; the audience stood in an open-air "yard" (the "groundlings") or sat in covered galleries. There were no spotlights, very little scenery, and no curtain — actors signalled time of day and location through their lines. When Romeo says "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! / For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5.51–2), the word "night" is doing scenic work — telling the audience the time of day the staging cannot show.

Elizabethan audiences were mixed in class and rowdy compared with modern theatre crowds. Groundlings paid a penny to stand close to the stage; better seats cost more. Shakespeare wrote with both audiences in mind: the bawdy puns of the Nurse and the servants kept the groundlings happy; the soliloquies and political resonances spoke to the educated patrons. This is one reason the language can feel uneven — it is doing different work for different listeners.

Drama is a literary form designed for performance. It is not a novel; it is a script. A Grade 10 reader should imagine the play in performance, not just on the page. Stage directions are sparse in Shakespeare's printed text (mostly "Enter," "Exit," and the occasional "Aside"), so most of the staging must be inferred from the dialogue. Lines like "give me my long sword, ho!" (1.1) signal both action (Capulet calls for a weapon) and character (he is older, less able to fight, and asking someone to fetch it for him). Reading Shakespeare requires reading through the dialogue into the implied action.

Several conventions repay attention. Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) is the standard for nobles and serious moments — Romeo and Juliet speak it almost exclusively. Prose is used for servants, low comedy, and madness — the Nurse and the servants speak mostly in prose. Rhyming couplets often signal the end of a scene or the conclusion of an important speech. Dramatic irony — where the audience knows something the characters do not — runs through this play in particular, beginning with the prologue that tells us how it will end. These conventions are not decoration. They are how Shakespeare makes meaning. Mark them as you read and ask, every time, "What does Shakespeare's choice tell me about this character or moment?"

  • Globe Theatre — Shakespeare's company's principal venue from 1599; open-air, daylight performance.
  • Groundlings — audience standing in the yard who paid one penny.
  • Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter; the standard verse for serious speech.
  • Rhyming couplet — two consecutive rhymed lines; often closes a scene or speech.
  • Prose — non-verse speech; used for servants, madness, and comedy.
  • Stage direction — bracketed instruction such as Enter, Exit, Aside.
  • Aside — a brief remark heard by the audience but not by the other characters.
  • Dramatic irony — audience knows what characters do not.

Worked Example — Reading the Opening Brawl in Performance

The play opens (1.1) with two Capulet servants — Sampson and Gregory — exchanging bawdy puns about violence and sexuality. A first-time reader skims these lines as filler. A performance-aware reader notices that Shakespeare uses prose (not verse) for these characters, which positions them as common; uses sexual puns as a hook for the groundlings; and stages a verbal escalation that becomes a sword fight when Tybalt arrives. The scene's structural job is to show that the feud is not abstract but daily, fought on the street by the lowest-ranking members of both households. By the time Prince Escalus arrives to declare a death penalty for further brawling, the audience has seen exactly the kind of unruly behaviour he is trying to suppress. A Grade 10 paragraph on Act 1, Scene 1 could analyse the choice of prose, the rapid escalation, and the dramatic irony of the Prince's late entry.

  • Reading the play as a novel — missing the implied staging.
  • Ignoring the difference between verse and prose speakers.
  • Treating stage directions as more reliable than they are — Shakespeare's are sparse.
  • Assuming all Elizabethan audiences understood every reference — they did not.
  • Skipping the prologue because "it tells you the ending" — the prologue is doing structural work.

📜 3.2 The Prologue & Act 1
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The play opens with one of the most famous prologues in English drama — a 14-line sonnet spoken by the Chorus. Shakespeare's choice of a sonnet for the prologue is deliberate and surprising. Sonnets are traditionally love poems; here, the love poem announces a tragedy. The form itself begins the play's central tension: love and death are not opposites but partners. The prologue tells us, in advance, that two "star-crossed lovers" will "take their life," that their deaths will end the feud, and that "the two hours' traffic of our stage" will give us the full story. Knowing the ending does not spoil the play — Shakespeare wants us to watch every choice with the knowledge that it leads here.

The phrase "star-crossed" places the lovers' fate in the stars. Elizabethans believed celestial bodies influenced human destinies (modern English still preserves traces — "lunatic" from luna, "jovial" from Jove). The play presents this fatalism through its language but consistently shows the audience that human choices, not stars, drive events. The prologue's framing as fate is part of what the play complicates.

Act 1 establishes the world before the lovers meet. Scene 1 stages the brawl that gives Verona its violent texture. Scene 2 introduces Paris, who is courting Juliet with Capulet's blessing — already, before Romeo appears, Juliet has a proposed future she has not chosen. Scene 3 introduces Juliet (only 13 years old), the Nurse, and Lady Capulet, who urges her daughter to consider Paris. Scene 4 brings Romeo (somewhere around 16), still pining for Rosaline, persuaded to attend the Capulet feast in disguise — and warned by Mercutio in the Queen Mab speech against believing in dreams. Scene 5 is the feast itself, where Romeo and Juliet meet, fall in love within minutes, and discover only as they part that they are children of feuding houses.

Pay particular attention to two passages in Act 1. First, the prologue (14 lines, a sonnet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG): it is a master class in compression — exposition, theme, and form all in 14 lines. Second, the meeting of Romeo and Juliet at the ball (1.5.92–105), which is itself a sonnet shared between the two characters. They speak the form of a love poem as they fall in love. The sonnet ends with the line "Then move not, while my prayer's effect I take" (1.5.104) — and they kiss. Shakespeare has them complete a 14-line sonnet, kiss, and start a second sonnet that gets interrupted. The form enacts the love story: brief, beautiful, broken. A close reader of the play notices that Shakespeare's structural choices are themselves arguments about what love is. Bring this awareness to every scene as you read.

  • Prologue — opening sonnet spoken by the Chorus, summarising what is to come.
  • Chorus — a single speaker (or group) who comments on action; Shakespeare uses one only in Romeo and Juliet and Henry V.
  • Sonnet — 14-line form Shakespeare embeds in the prologue and the first meeting.
  • Exposition — opening information that sets up character, setting, conflict.
  • Foreshadowing — hints of later developments (the prologue's announcement of death).
  • Queen Mab speech — Mercutio's long monologue (1.4) on the fairy queen who controls dreams.
  • Tybalt — Juliet's hot-headed cousin; first agent of the feud's violence.
  • Paris — young noble proposed as Juliet's husband; never an antagonist, only a complication.

Worked Example — The Shared Sonnet at the Ball

When Romeo first speaks to Juliet (1.5.92–105), Shakespeare gives them an embedded Shakespearean sonnet. Romeo opens with a religious metaphor — Juliet is a "holy shrine," his hand a "pilgrim":

"If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss."

Juliet matches him in the next quatrain. Their lines fall into perfect alternating rhyme (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG). The sonnet ends in their first kiss — and a second sonnet begins, only to be interrupted by the Nurse calling Juliet away. A Grade 10 paragraph could argue that Shakespeare uses the sonnet form as plot: the form's completion mimics emotional completion; the form's interruption mimics the constant interruption of their love by the world around them. The wedding will be rushed (no time), the meeting in the tomb will be missed (timing failure), and even the messages will arrive too late. The interrupted second sonnet is the play's first sign that the world will not let this love have its 14 lines.

  • Reading the prologue as plot summary and not noticing it is a sonnet.
  • Missing that Romeo and Juliet's first conversation is also a sonnet.
  • Treating Mercutio's Queen Mab speech as filler — it is a warning.
  • Forgetting Juliet's age (13) when analysing the play's depictions of agency.
  • Assuming Romeo and Juliet meet in private — they meet at a public feast.

🌙 3.3 The Balcony Scene (Act 2)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The balcony scene (2.2) is the most quoted scene in Shakespeare. Romeo has slipped over the Capulet garden wall to look for Juliet after the feast; she appears on her balcony, unaware that he is below, and speaks her famous lines:

"O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo? / Deny thy father and refuse thy name; / Or, if thou wilt not, be but sworn my love, / And I'll no longer be a Capulet."

Two things often confuse Grade 10 readers. First, "wherefore" does not mean "where" — it means "why." Juliet is not asking where Romeo is; she is asking why he has to be Romeo Montague, given the feud. Second, she does not yet know he is in the garden. She is speaking to herself, into the night, and Shakespeare lets the audience hear her thinking. When Romeo answers her aloud, the privacy collapses and the courtship begins.

The scene is a master class in dramatic irony. The audience can see both characters; each character can only see the other once Romeo speaks. Their first exchange is shaped by Juliet's fear that he has overheard her ("My ears have yet not drunk a hundred words / Of thy tongue's uttering, yet I know the sound" 2.2.58–9), her concern that he will be killed if Capulet's guards find him, and her worry that her sudden declaration will seem light: "Dost thou love me? I know thou wilt say 'Ay,' / And I will take thy word: yet, if thou swear'st, / Thou mayst prove false" (2.2.90–2). She is 13 and entirely aware of the difference between performed love and tested love. The scene is romantic, but it is also a negotiation.

The scene's most powerful image is Juliet's argument that names are arbitrary: "What's in a name? that which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43–4). It is one of Shakespeare's most quoted lines, and it is also philosophically dangerous. The play will prove Juliet wrong: the Montague–Capulet feud is precisely a war over names, and the names will kill them. The romantic rhetoric of love-conquers-all bumps up against the social reality that names carry real consequences. Reading the balcony scene against the play's outcome reveals Shakespeare's argument: the lovers' optimism about names is beautiful and youthful — and tragic, because the world they live in does not share it. A close reading paragraph could trace how the play's later actions (Tybalt's challenge over "Romeo," the dispatch of news, the rushed marriage) make Juliet's "rose by any other name" line a kind of innocent error the audience must grieve. Even her wisdom is too young to predict the world's brutality.

  • Soliloquy — a speech delivered alone on stage, voicing inner thought.
  • Aside — a brief remark heard by audience but not other characters.
  • Apostrophe — direct address to an absent person, abstract idea, or object.
  • Dramatic irony — audience knows what characters do not; central to this scene.
  • Hyperbole — Romeo's repeated cosmic comparisons (Juliet = sun, stars).
  • Light/dark imagery — pervasive through the scene; Juliet is "the sun" in darkness.
  • Stichomythia — rapid line-by-line dialogue between two speakers (their later exchange uses it).

Worked Example — The Sun Metaphor in Romeo's Opening Speech

When Romeo first sees Juliet at the window (2.2.2–6), he speaks an extended astronomical metaphor:

"But, soft! what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun. / Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon, / Who is already sick and pale with grief, / That thou her maid art far more fair than she."

The metaphor does three things at once. (1) It places Juliet at the centre of a new universe — she is what Romeo's world will now orbit. (2) It uses the rhetoric of dawn (the sun rising, killing the moon) — a metaphor of birth, beginning, possibility. (3) But the metaphor is also self-defeating: dawn is the time when night ends, and the lovers' meeting is happening at night. Romeo's eagerness for sunrise is, structurally, an eagerness for the end of their stolen time. A Grade 10 paragraph would note the cosmic scale ("east," "sun," "moon"), the imagery of replacement (Juliet "kills" the moon), and the tragic irony — Romeo's sun-rhetoric forecasts both their love's intensity and its brevity.

  • Misreading "wherefore" as "where" — it means "why."
  • Assuming Juliet knows Romeo is listening — she does not, until he speaks.
  • Treating the scene as pure romance — it is also a careful negotiation.
  • Quoting "Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo" without analysing the name-question.
  • Forgetting the dramatic irony — the audience knows the lovers' fate from the prologue.

⚔️ 3.4 The Turning Point — Act 3
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Act 3 is the centre and the hinge of the play. Tybalt comes looking for Romeo; Mercutio steps in; Tybalt kills Mercutio; Romeo, in grief and rage, kills Tybalt; the Prince banishes Romeo from Verona. Up to this point, the story has been a comedy moving toward marriage. After 3.1, it is a tragedy moving toward death. Shakespeare lets the audience watch the genre shift in a single scene.

Pay particular attention to Mercutio's death (3.1.92–106). His most famous line — "A plague o' both your houses!" — names what the play has been about all along: not love-versus-hate but the cost of clan loyalty. Mercutio is neither a Montague nor a Capulet, and his death pulls Romeo into the violence Romeo had been trying to refuse. Earlier in the scene, Romeo had told Tybalt "I do protest, I never injured thee, / But love thee better than thou canst devise" (3.1.69–70). Romeo was trying to leave the feud behind through his secret marriage. Mercutio's death forces him back in. The scene asks: can private love survive public violence? The play's answer is no.

Romeo's killing of Tybalt is morally complicated and Shakespeare wants it that way. Romeo acts from grief; the audience understands him. But he also acts from rage, and he is not justified by Verona's law (the Prince has set a death penalty for any further brawling). The result — banishment, not execution — is partial mercy and partial sentence. Juliet's response in 3.2 is one of the play's most extraordinary speeches: she discovers her cousin is dead and her new husband killed him, and she must decide whom to mourn first. Her language tangles: "O serpent heart, hid with a flowering face! / Did ever dragon keep so fair a cave?" (3.2.73–4). Then she reverses herself in a few lines and chooses Romeo. The speech shows a 13-year-old doing political and emotional work most adults could not.

By the end of Act 3, the wedding night has been compressed into one dawn (3.5), Juliet has been ordered to marry Paris on Thursday, the lovers are separated, and the Friar's plan begins. Shakespeare has shortened the timeline: Romeo and Juliet meet on Sunday night, marry Monday afternoon, and are separated by Tuesday morning. The play covers four days of fictional time. Every "wisely and slow" warning the Friar has given is contradicted by this acceleration. Grade 10 readers often ask why the lovers do not just talk to their parents; the play's answer is that the speed of the events allows no such conversation, and the speed itself is the tragedy.

  • Tybalt — Juliet's cousin and the feud's most committed warrior.
  • Mercutio — Romeo's friend, neither Capulet nor Montague; the play's first major death.
  • Banishment — exile from Verona; Romeo's punishment for killing Tybalt.
  • Hamartia — the tragic hero's fatal flaw or error; Romeo's hot temper in Act 3.
  • Catalyst — event that triggers the genre shift from comedy to tragedy.
  • Peripeteia — sudden reversal of fortune in a tragedy.
  • Pathetic fallacy — natural phenomena reflecting human emotion (Juliet's "gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" 3.2 calls on the night).

Worked Example — Reading Mercutio's "A Plague" Curse

Mercutio's dying line (3.1.99) is repeated three times in the scene: "A plague o' both your houses! / They have made worms' meat of me." A Grade 10 paragraph could note three things: (1) the repetition gives the line ritual force — it sounds like a curse, not a complaint; (2) "both your houses" pointedly refuses to take sides — Mercutio dies blaming Capulet and Montague equally; (3) the dehumanising "worms' meat" image strips Mercutio of his earlier comedic vitality. By dying with a curse rather than a regret, Mercutio gives the audience the play's clearest moral verdict: the feud kills people who never chose it. The line's force resonates through the rest of the play — every subsequent death (Tybalt's, Paris's, Romeo's, Juliet's) plays out the curse Mercutio uttered with his last breath.

  • Reading 3.1 as just a fight scene rather than the genre-shift hinge.
  • Treating Romeo's killing of Tybalt as fully justified — Shakespeare complicates it.
  • Skipping Juliet's "serpent heart" speech as confusion — it is moral reasoning under pressure.
  • Missing the compressed timeline (the whole play covers four days).
  • Quoting "A plague o' both your houses" without noting it is repeated three times.

⚱️ 3.5 The Tragic Ending (Acts 4–5)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Acts 4 and 5 dramatise the failure of a plan. Friar Lawrence gives Juliet a sleeping potion that will mimic death for 42 hours; she is to be placed in the Capulet tomb, where Romeo (informed by the Friar's letter) will retrieve her, and the two will escape together. The plan fails because Friar John, sent with the letter, is quarantined in a plague-house and never reaches Romeo. Instead, Romeo's servant Balthasar arrives in Mantua with news that Juliet is dead. Romeo buys poison from a Mantuan apothecary, races back to Verona, kills Paris at the tomb, drinks the poison, and dies just before Juliet wakes. Juliet sees Romeo's body, tries to kiss poison from his lips, then stabs herself with his dagger.

The plan's failure is what makes the ending tragic rather than merely sad. The audience knows the lovers could have lived: only a few hours of timing or a working postal system separated them from a happy ending. Shakespeare puts that knowledge in our hands as a kind of grief — we are reading a tragedy that did not have to be one. The plague that quarantines Friar John is mentioned almost casually (5.2.6 — "the searchers of the town, / Suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Seal'd up the doors"). One throwaway bureaucratic detail kills both lovers. The political reading is unavoidable: institutions fail private love.

The Friar's role complicates the moral picture. He marries the lovers in haste; he gives Juliet the potion; he writes the letter that does not arrive; he then panics and runs from the tomb when Juliet wakes — leaving her alone with Romeo's body. The Friar is not a villain, but he is consistently the wrong adult: well-meaning, hopeful for peace, slow to take responsibility for outcomes he set in motion. Shakespeare's portrait suggests that the play's tragedy is not in the stars but in the gap between adult institutions (church, family, state) and the children they were supposed to protect.

The ending is famously double-edged. Capulet and Montague reconcile over the bodies of their dead children. The Prince delivers the final couplet: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309–10). The feud ends, exactly as the prologue promised, "with their children's end." The audience must decide whether the reconciliation justifies the cost. Most readings, including most performances, suggest it does not — the price is too high, the parents' grief comes too late, and the play's last image is of statues of the lovers being raised "in pure gold" (5.3.299), a memorial that fixes them in place but cannot undo their deaths. The tragedy refuses to be redeemed. That refusal is part of what makes Romeo and Juliet still readable 430 years later: it does not pretend that grief can be balanced by reconciliation, and it asks the audience to leave the theatre dissatisfied.

  • Sleeping potion — Friar Lawrence's herbal mixture that induces death-like sleep.
  • Mantua — the city where Romeo is exiled; messages travel slowly.
  • Apothecary — Mantuan pharmacist who sells Romeo poison "though Mantua's law is death to any he that utters them" (5.1.66–7).
  • Tomb / monument — Capulet family vault; setting of the final scene.
  • Catastrophe — in classical tragedy, the final unwinding; here, the deaths.
  • Anagnorisis — the moment of recognition; the parents seeing what the feud has cost.
  • Reconciliation — the families' truce over the bodies.
  • Tragic couplet — the Prince's closing rhymed couplet announcing the play's ending.

Worked Example — The Apothecary as Social Critique

In 5.1 Romeo buys poison from a Mantuan apothecary who is breaking the law to sell it. Romeo persuades him: "Famine is in thy cheeks, / Need and oppression starveth in thine eyes, / Contempt and beggary hangs upon thy back. / The world is not thy friend, nor the world's law" (5.1.69–72). Romeo argues the apothecary should sell because the legal system has already failed him. A close reading paragraph could note that Shakespeare uses this brief, almost throwaway, scene to expand the play's argument beyond the lovers: institutions fail not just the rich (Romeo and Juliet) but the poor (the apothecary). The ending of the feud will save no future apothecaries. The reconciliation Shakespeare offers in 5.3 is, by the play's own logic, partial — it does not address the structural conditions that kill ordinary people.

  • Blaming the lovers entirely — the play also implicates adults and institutions.
  • Treating the Friar as a villain — he is well-meaning but consistently wrong.
  • Forgetting the plague subplot — one bureaucratic failure kills both lovers.
  • Reading the reconciliation as fully redemptive — Shakespeare resists that reading.
  • Reducing the play to "fate" when its mechanism is timing, secrecy, and adult failure.

📐 3.6 Shakespeare's Language: Blank Verse & Wordplay
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Shakespeare's language can feel like a barrier to Grade 10 readers, but the difficulty is mostly habit, not vocabulary. Once you have read fifty lines of Shakespeare aloud, the patterns become familiar. The play uses three distinct registers: blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter), rhymed verse (often couplets or sonnets), and prose. Each register signals something about who is speaking and why. Romeo and Juliet, when speaking in private love-language, use rhymed verse. The Nurse and the servants speak prose. Friar Lawrence uses formal blank verse. Tracking who uses what register — and when they shift — is one of the most useful close-reading habits.

Iambic pentameter is five iambs per line (da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM). Try scanning Romeo's "But, soft! what light through yon-der win-dow breaks?" — five iambs. The metre tracks ordinary English sentence rhythm closely, which is why Shakespeare wrote in it; it sounds natural while being formally controlled. When Shakespeare violates the metre — an extra syllable, a heavy spondee, a missing beat — the violation is meaningful. A stressed-stressed foot at the start of a line can signal urgency, anger, or emphasis. Reading aloud is the only reliable way to feel where the verse pushes and pulls.

Wordplay is Shakespeare's other signature. Puns are not jokes added for entertainment; they are how the play makes meaning. Mercutio's dying line — "Ask for me to-morrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1.94) — puns on "grave" (serious) and "grave" (burial). The pun is a coping mechanism (he is making a joke as he dies) and a thematic statement (the gravity of the moment is also literal gravity). When you encounter a pun, ask: what are both meanings, and what does Shakespeare gain by having both at once?

Don't try to translate Shakespeare into modern English line-by-line — that flattens the play. Instead, read for the shape of each speech: where does the syntax open up, where does it cinch tight, where does a long sentence trail off, where does a short sentence land hard? Notice when a character speaks in three-syllable bursts versus long-flowing periods. The Nurse's speech in 1.3, recounting Juliet's weaning, is intentionally messy — it imitates how an elderly woman tells a story. Romeo's speeches at the start of the play (before he meets Juliet) are full of artificial paradoxes — "O brawling love! O loving hate!" (1.1.176) — which Shakespeare deliberately uses to mark Romeo as still performing love rather than feeling it. Once Romeo meets Juliet, his language becomes simpler and more direct. The journey from artificial Petrarchan paradox to plain devotion is itself a character arc Shakespeare writes into the syntax. Pay attention to it.

  • Iambic pentameter — five iambs per line; Shakespeare's standard verse.
  • Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter; serious noble speech.
  • Prose — non-verse speech; servants, low comedy, madness.
  • Rhymed couplet — two consecutive rhymed lines; often closes a scene.
  • Pun — wordplay with two meanings; central to Shakespeare's wit.
  • Petrarchan paradox — love-poetry oxymorons ("brawling love," "icy fire").
  • Caesura — strong pause within a line, marked by punctuation.
  • Enjambment — sentence running past a line break without punctuation.

Worked Example — Scanning Romeo's First Line to Juliet

Romeo's first words at the ball (1.5.92): "If I profane with my unworthiest hand."

Scansion: If / I / pro-FANE / with / MY / un-WOR-thi-EST / HAND. The line is roughly iambic pentameter, but "unworthiest" is forced (Shakespeare often elides the middle syllable: un-WOR-thiest). Notice that the line opens with a religious metaphor — "profane" suggests sacred ground — and that Romeo has shifted from his earlier Petrarchan paradoxes to a single sustained image. By the end of the four lines, he and Juliet have completed a quatrain of a sonnet. The language is doing the courtship: Shakespeare's metre is exactly as deliberate as his diction.

  • Trying to translate every Shakespearean phrase into modern English.
  • Reading Shakespeare silently — the verse needs to be heard.
  • Ignoring puns as "just jokes" — they carry thematic weight.
  • Missing register shifts (verse to prose, prose to verse) as signals.
  • Treating Petrarchan oxymorons as "weird" instead of as character markers.
