📖 1.1 What Close Reading Means
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Close reading is slow, attentive reading that treats every choice in a text as potentially meaningful. The Grade 11 reader is moving from a Grade 10 habit of reading for plot (what happened?) to a habit of reading for craft (how does the writer make me feel and think this?). The shift is more important than any single device or term you will memorise this year, because it is the foundation of every analytical task in ENG3U and ENG4U.

A close reader annotates. Underline phrases that strike you. Circle words that recur. Bracket places where the tone shifts. Write a short reaction in the margin every page. The point is not to produce a clean copy but to leave a trail of attention that you can return to. Many students arrive at Grade 11 having been told never to write in books; in this course, the well-annotated text is the prerequisite for the well-written essay.

Re-reading is part of close reading, not a sign of failure. The first read absorbs the plot and the voice; the second read sees the structure; the third notices contradictions in your first interpretation. Skilled readers expect their initial reading to be partial and treat re-reading as where the real understanding happens. If you are writing an essay from a single read, you are guessing.

One useful diagnostic: after you finish reading a story, write four sentences before you do anything else — one sentence about plot, one about a character, one about a phrase that stuck with you, and one about a question you still have. Those four sentences are the seed of every analytical paragraph you will write about that text. They are not your final answer; they are evidence of having read closely enough to notice something specific. Build the habit now, in Grade 11, and the Grade 12 essays — and the university essays after that — become much easier.

  • Annotation — marks and notes left on a text to record a reader's response.
  • Close reading — attentive, evidence-based interpretation of a text.
  • Inference — reasoning beyond the literal to a claim the text supports.
  • Diction — an author's specific word choices.
  • Tone — the author's attitude toward the subject.
  • Mood — the emotional atmosphere the text creates in the reader.
  • Theme — a defensible claim about life the text is making.
  • Topic — what the text is about (love, war, family); narrower than theme.

Worked Example — Annotating an Opening Sentence

The opening sentence of George Orwell's 1984:

"It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen."

A close reader notices: (1) "bright cold day" — paired adjectives that should not go together, signalling discomfort; (2) "April" — the month of renewal, used here to feel wrong; (3) "the clocks were striking thirteen" — clocks do not strike thirteen in our world, so the sentence has slipped sideways from realism without announcing it. None of this is "the theme yet." It is what a reader who has been paying attention can show you after the first sentence. A weaker reader writes, "The opening is in April." A stronger reader writes, "The opening pairs ordinary detail with one quietly impossible image, training the reader from the first sentence to look for what is wrong in this world." That second sentence is what close reading produces.

  • Reading once and writing the response — almost every analytical insight comes on the second pass.
  • Confusing topic with theme: "love" is a topic; "love survives loss only when it is named" is a theme.
  • Treating annotations as graffiti instead of a working record.
  • Skipping the opening sentence — the first line of any text is the writer's most deliberate choice.
  • Treating tone and mood as synonyms; tone is the writer's, mood is the reader's.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.3 — "identify the most important ideas and supporting details in texts."

🔍 1.2 Text Features & Literary Devices
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Literary devices are tools that produce meaning, not decorations bolted onto plain speech. When a writer reaches for metaphor, irony, or symbol, she is making a claim that ordinary description cannot carry. Calling something "the heart of the city" is not a flowery substitute for "the centre"; it asserts that the city is alive and that the centre is the part keeping it alive. The Grade 11 reader is expected to move from device-spotting ("there's a metaphor") to device-interpreting ("this metaphor produces this effect because…").

The first family of devices to know cold is figurative language. A metaphor implicitly identifies one thing with another ("Juliet is the sun"). A simile compares with "like" or "as" ("My love is like a red, red rose"). Personification gives human qualities to non-human things ("the wind sighed"). A symbol is a concrete image that stands for an abstract idea (a flag, a road, a dagger). Imagery appeals to the five senses to make a scene vivid. Each device works by displacement — it asks the reader to hold two ideas at once and notice the friction between them.

Irony deserves its own paragraph because Grade 11 essays often misname it. Verbal irony says one thing and means another. Situational irony is the gap between what is expected and what actually happens. Dramatic irony is the gap between what a character knows and what the audience knows (we know Macbeth's witches are dangerous; he does not yet). Students reach for "irony" whenever something is surprising, but surprise alone is not irony — the device requires a structured gap between two layers of meaning. The analytical move is to name which two layers and what the gap reveals.

Sound and structure are devices too. Alliteration (repeated initial consonants) and assonance (repeated vowel sounds) shape mood at a level below conscious attention. Foreshadowing plants hints that prepare a later development. Repetition insists, builds rhythm, makes a phrase or image carry more weight every time it returns. None of these "are themselves" the meaning — they are how the writer engineers an effect on the reader. Naming the device is step one; explaining its effect is the work.

  • Metaphor / simile / personification — the core figures of comparison.
  • Symbol — a concrete image standing for an abstract idea, often built through repetition.
  • Imagery — sensory description (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory).
  • Verbal / situational / dramatic irony — three structured gaps between expectation and reality.
  • Alliteration / assonance / sibilance — patterned repetition of sound.
  • Foreshadowing — hints that prepare a later development.
  • Repetition — insistence that builds weight or rhythm.
  • Hyperbole / understatement — deliberate exaggeration or restraint for effect.

Worked Example — Reading a Metaphor

Robert Burns opens a famous love poem:

"O my Luve is like a red, red rose / That's newly sprung in June."

Strictly, this is a simile (it uses "like"), not a metaphor — a distinction many Grade 11 essays get wrong. Beyond naming the device, the close reader asks: why a rose specifically, and why a red one, and why one "newly sprung"? Roses are beautiful but short-lived; "red, red" doubles the colour to insist on its intensity; "newly sprung in June" places this love at the moment of bloom, before any decay. Burns is not just praising his beloved — he is naming the fragility of his praise. A Level 4 paragraph would notice all of that. A Level 2 paragraph would say "Burns uses a simile to show his love is beautiful" and stop.

  • Listing devices ("there is a metaphor and a simile") without explaining their effect.
  • Misquoting — dropping or inventing words inside quotation marks.
  • Treating every comparison as a metaphor: similes use "like" or "as."
  • Calling something a symbol because it is mentioned twice — symbols accumulate weight through repetition and context.
  • Using "irony" loosely whenever something is unexpected.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B2.3 — "identify a variety of text features and explain how they help communicate meaning."

🎯 1.3 Inference and Finding Evidence
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

An inference is a reasoned claim about a text that goes beyond what is stated literally but that the text supports. "The narrator's father is dead" might be an inference (if the story shows his absent place at dinner, a black armband, the mother's tears) even if no sentence says it. Inference is what makes reading more than transcription: the reader fills in gaps the writer has deliberately left, and a strong reader fills them in ways the writer's other choices confirm.

Inference is not guessing. A guess is a claim you cannot defend; an inference is a claim you can back with at least two specific textual details. The Grade 11 expectation is that you can name the inference, name the evidence, and explain why the evidence supports the inference. Students who skip the third step — "explain why the evidence supports it" — are working at Level 2; students who do all three are working at Level 3 and beyond.

Finding evidence is a skill in itself. The best evidence is specific (a phrase, not a paragraph), directly relevant (it does the work you are claiming it does), and representative (the text supports the same reading elsewhere). When you quote, quote tightly — three to seven words is often enough, and embedded inside your own sentence reads more skilfully than a free-standing block. Save block quotations for moments when the full passage's structure is itself the evidence.

An evidence log is the simplest tool that separates Level 3 work from Level 2 work in Grade 11. As you read, copy out short quotations into a running document, grouped by theme, character, or motif, each with the page or line number. When you sit down to write, the evidence is already organised and the essay drafts itself around it. Students who try to find evidence only at the drafting stage usually settle for whichever quotation surfaces first, which is rarely the strongest one. Build the log from your second reading onward — and use it.

  • Inference — a reasoned claim beyond the literal that the text supports.
  • Evidence — specific textual material that supports a claim.
  • Quotation, direct — exact words of the text, in quotation marks, with citation.
  • Quotation, embedded — short quoted phrase integrated into your own sentence's syntax.
  • Block quotation — longer quoted passage set off without quotation marks (used sparingly).
  • Paraphrase — restating the text's content in your own words.
  • Signal phrase — words introducing a quotation ("The narrator observes…").
  • Implicit / explicit — stated outright vs. implied through other choices.

Worked Example — From Guess to Inference

A weak reader writes: "I think Macbeth is going to kill Duncan because he's an evil character."

That is a guess plus a value judgement. The text has not yet shown Macbeth as "evil" — at this point in Act 1 he has just been called "brave Macbeth" by the captain (1.2.16) for his loyal service. A stronger inference, grounded in evidence, would be: "Macbeth is considering the murder seriously. After the witches greet him as 'Thane of Cawdor' and 'king hereafter' (1.3.50), Macbeth's first aside imagines a 'horrid image' that 'doth unfix my hair / And make my seated heart knock at my ribs' (1.3.135–6). The physical reaction — stiffened hair, pounding heart — is involuntary, the sort of thing the body does only when a thought has been entertained long enough to feel real. He is not merely curious about the prophecy; he is already imagining the act."

The second version names the inference (Macbeth is taking the prophecy seriously enough to imagine murder), names the evidence (two specific quotations), and explains why the evidence supports the inference (the involuntary physical response).

  • Confusing inference with assumption — an assumption is something you bring; an inference is something the text supports.
  • Quoting whole paragraphs when a phrase would do.
  • Dropping a quotation in without grammatical integration.
  • Citing evidence and then forgetting to explain how it supports the claim.
  • Treating one piece of evidence as proof — patterns are stronger than single instances.

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.4 — "make and explain inferences about texts, supporting their explanations with stated and implied ideas from the texts."

✍️ 1.4 Paraphrase vs Analysis
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The single largest leap between Grade 10 and Grade 11 essay marks is the leap from paraphrase to analysis. Paraphrase tells the reader what the text says. Analysis explains what the text does and why it matters. Both have a place — a sharp paraphrase can launch an argument — but a paragraph that ends in paraphrase has not earned its conclusion. A paragraph that ends in analysis has.

The simplest diagnostic: read your paragraph and ask, "Could a careful summary write-up have produced this?" If yes, you are paraphrasing. If your paragraph names a specific choice the writer made, attributes an effect to it, and links the effect to a larger claim about the text, you are analysing. Analysis is interpretation backed by quoted evidence and reasoning.

The PEEL paragraph structure exists to keep analysis honest. Point (a topic sentence that makes a debatable claim about the text); Evidence (a short, embedded quotation with citation); Explain (your interpretation of how the evidence supports the point — naming the device or technique and saying why it produces this effect); Link (a closing sentence that ties this paragraph's idea back to the thesis or forward to the next). If any of the four steps is missing, the paragraph is incomplete; if the third step is weak, the paragraph is paraphrase masquerading as analysis.

One useful trick for testing the strength of your analytical sentence: try replacing the final line of your paragraph with "And therefore…" If the rest of the paragraph supports the sentence that follows, the paragraph is doing analysis. If you cannot finish "And therefore…" without restating what you already said, your final sentence is paraphrase. Strong Grade 11 paragraphs end with a sentence the reader could disagree with, because that sentence is the paragraph's actual contribution. "This shows that Macbeth is conflicted" is too obvious to disagree with; "Macbeth's conflict is therefore not regret but calculation — he is rehearsing the murder, not reconsidering it" is a claim that could be tested against the rest of the play. Practice writing the contestable closing sentence and your paragraphs will sharpen quickly.

  • Paraphrase — restating the text in different words.
  • Analysis — explaining how and why a textual choice produces its effect.
  • PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explain, Link.
  • Topic sentence — the analytical claim that opens a paragraph.
  • Embedded quotation — a quoted phrase grammatically integrated into your sentence.
  • Signal phrase — words that introduce a quotation and attribute it.
  • Warrant — the reasoning that connects evidence to claim (Toulmin's term).
  • Contestable claim — a thesis a reasonable reader could disagree with — and that you can defend.

Worked Example — Same Evidence, Three Levels

The evidence: Macbeth, in his first soliloquy, says, "If it were done when 'tis done, then 'twere well / It were done quickly" (1.7.1–2).

Plot summary: "Macbeth is thinking about killing Duncan." — Adds nothing the reader did not have.

Paraphrase: "Macbeth says that if killing Duncan ends with the act itself, it would be best to do it quickly." — Slightly more precise, still restatement.

Analysis: "The triple repetition of 'done' in two lines — once as past participle, twice as the noun-like 'tis done / 'twere done — shows Macbeth trying to argue himself into seeing the murder as a single contained event. The conditional 'if' carries the whole sentence; Macbeth has not committed to the act, but he has committed to imagining it as completed. The very effort to compress the murder into one syllable ('done') is what exposes Shakespeare's point: an act this large will never be 'done' in the sense the murderer needs it to be." — This is analysis: it names a specific technique (repetition, conditional syntax), explains the effect (the murderer's effort to compress the crime), and links to a larger claim about the play (the impossibility of containment).

  • Summarising plot instead of analysing technique.
  • Dropping a quotation into a paragraph without integrating it grammatically.
  • Ending the paragraph at "this shows" without saying what it shows.
  • Confusing the writer with the speaker or narrator.
  • Writing the topic sentence as a fact rather than as a claim ("Macbeth has soliloquies" is not a claim).

Curriculum expectation: Strand B, Expectation B1.6 — "analyse texts in terms of the information, ideas, issues, or themes they explore… and explain how various aspects of the texts contribute to their meaning."
