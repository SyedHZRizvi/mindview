📚 5.1 Choosing a Topic and Building a Research Question
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The Independent Study Unit (ISU) is the largest piece of work in the ENG3U course and the closest thing you have done to a university-style research paper. The hinge of the whole project is topic selection. A topic that is too broad ("Canadian literature") will overwhelm you; a topic that is too narrow ("the second sentence of one short story") will starve you of material. The Grade 11 sweet spot is a topic specific enough to argue but broad enough to research — something like "How three Canadian short-story writers treat childhood under economic pressure," or "What changes when Macbeth is staged for film vs. for live theatre."

A topic becomes a project when you turn it into a research question. A question is sharper than a topic because it predicts the kind of evidence the essay needs. "How three Canadian short-story writers treat childhood" is a topic; "Do Munro, Atwood, and Boyden treat childhood as an irreversible event or as a recoverable state?" is a research question. Notice what the question forces: a comparative structure (three writers), an analytical axis (irreversible vs. recoverable), and a defendable answer (yes / no / sometimes). A good ISU question can be answered three or four different ways, each of them defensible from the texts — that is what makes the project worth doing.

The research question is usually drafted before the research begins and revised after the first week of reading. Do not expect your final question to be the question you started with. Reading reshapes the question, and that reshaping is part of the work. Students who lock in a question on day one and refuse to revise it usually write essays that try to force evidence into a shape that no longer fits. Treat the question as a moving target until you have done your first complete pass through the sources.

Practical advice: pick a topic you can sustain interest in for six to eight weeks. ISU projects fail more often from fatigue than from incompetence. If you cannot imagine yourself wanting to read about your chosen topic in week seven, when the essay is due in week eight and you are tired, the topic is wrong. Sit with two or three candidates for a few days before you commit; the topic you keep returning to is usually the right one. Approval from your teacher is a real step, not a formality. Bring two or three candidate questions to the meeting and an honest sense of which you find most interesting; come out of the meeting with a single question and a small reading list to begin.

  • Topic — the area you will study.
  • Research question — a focused, answerable question that drives the project.
  • Scope — the manageable size of a project given the time and word count.
  • Thesis — the answer to the research question, refined through research.
  • Working bibliography — the running list of sources you are gathering.
  • Approval — the teacher's sign-off on topic and question.
  • Project timeline — the calendar that distributes work across weeks.
  • Pivot — revising the question in response to what the research reveals.

Worked Example — Sharpening a Question Across Three Drafts

A student wants to write on social media's effect on teenage mental health.

Draft 1: "Is social media bad for teenagers?" — Too broad and answer-shaped before research begins.
Draft 2: "How does Instagram affect teen self-image?" — Better, but still one platform and one outcome, and the answer is partly already in the public discourse.
Draft 3: "When recent quasi-experimental studies disagree about whether social-media use causes increases in teen depression rates, which methodological choices account for the disagreement, and what does this tell us about how to read media-effects research?" — This question is specific, comparative, has multiple defensible answers, and predicts the evidence the essay will need (the studies themselves, plus secondary commentary on methodology).

Notice the progression: Draft 1 is a topic disguised as a question, Draft 2 is closer but still answerable in one sentence, Draft 3 is a research question that requires reading and synthesis to answer. Grade 11 ISUs that land on a Draft 3 question outperform projects with Draft 1 or 2 questions, regardless of how hard the student works on the writing.

  • Locking in a thesis before research begins.
  • Choosing a topic for ease rather than for sustained interest.
  • Confusing topic with question — the question is what the project answers.
  • Refusing to revise the question once research has reshaped it.
  • Skipping teacher approval and discovering, three weeks in, that the topic is unworkable.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.2 — "generate, expand, explore, and focus ideas for potential writing tasks, using a variety of strategies and print, electronic, and other resources."

🔎 5.2 Evaluating Sources
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Source evaluation is the difference between a research paper and a collage. A research paper uses sources to build an argument; a collage strings sources together and hopes meaning emerges. The Grade 11 ISU is the moment when you stop trusting that "I found it online" is sufficient justification and start asking, of every source: who wrote this, where was it published, when, with what evidence, and why does it appear in my search results?

The simplest practical framework is the SIFT method (developed by Mike Caulfield): Stop before you read — notice your reaction and ask whether you already trust or distrust this source for the wrong reasons; Investigate the source — open a new tab and find out who runs the site and what their reputation is; Find better coverage — search for the same claim on reputable outlets; Trace claims back to their original context. SIFT replaces a checklist of "is this site trustworthy?" with the move that actually works: leave the site and ask the wider web what it thinks of the site.

Source quality matters more than source quantity. A short ISU bibliography of four strong sources — peer-reviewed articles via library databases (JSTOR, Project MUSE, Academic Search Premier), books from university presses, reputable newspapers and journals — beats a long list of blog posts and AI summaries. SparkNotes, CliffsNotes, LitCharts, and AI chatbots are study aids, not citable sources; using them as authority in an ISU undermines your credibility and, more importantly, removes you from the actual scholarly conversation. Your provincial public library card, in most Ontario municipalities, gives you free access to scholarly databases — use it.

An annotated bibliography is the bridge from research to writing. For each source: full MLA citation; a two-to-three-sentence summary of the source's argument; and a one-sentence statement of how the source will function in your essay (support, context, counter-position?). Drafting the annotation forces you to take a stance toward each source rather than merely collect it. The annotation also makes the drafting stage faster, because the structure of your evidence is already organised before you start writing the essay itself. Many Grade 11 students discover, in the middle of a draft, that their best source is unfindable because they did not log it when they found it; the annotated bibliography is the discipline that prevents this loss.

  • Primary source — original document or first-hand account.
  • Secondary source — scholarly writing about primary material.
  • Peer review — vetting by other experts before publication.
  • Database — JSTOR, Project MUSE, Academic Search Premier, MLA International Bibliography.
  • SIFT method — Stop, Investigate, Find better coverage, Trace claims (Caulfield).
  • Lateral reading — checking the source by leaving the source and searching elsewhere.
  • Annotated bibliography — list of sources with summary and stance.
  • Plagiarism — presenting another's words or ideas without attribution.

Worked Example — Two Annotated Entries

Strong:

"Twenge, Jean M., et al. 'Increases in Depressive Symptoms, Suicide-Related Outcomes, and Suicide Rates Among U.S. Adolescents After 2010 and Links to Increased New Media Screen Time.' Clinical Psychological Science, vol. 6, no. 1, 2018, pp. 3–17. Twenge argues that an inflection point in U.S. adolescent depression coincides with the mass adoption of smartphones, and that the trend is concentrated among heavy social-media users. I will use Twenge to anchor the case for a causal mechanism, but I disagree with her treatment of correlation as sufficient evidence; my essay engages later work (Orben & Przybylski 2019) that shows the effect size is smaller than her framing implies."

This annotation does three things: summarises the source accurately, names how it will be used, and identifies a point of productive disagreement. That is what a graduate-style annotation looks like, and it is what Grade 11 essays should aspire toward.

Weak:

"LitCharts. Macbeth Study Guide. LitCharts.com. This website has scene summaries and character analyses. It will help me write my essay."

This is not a citable source. It has no named author, no peer review, and is designed to substitute for reading. If you find a useful idea on LitCharts, treat it as a prompt to consult a real source — open the play, then open a library database — not as a citation in itself.

  • Citing SparkNotes, CliffsNotes, LitCharts, or AI chatbots as authoritative.
  • Copying quotations from another critic without going back to the primary source.
  • Collecting sources without taking a stance toward them.
  • Annotated entries that paraphrase rather than evaluate.
  • Using Wikipedia citations directly — go to Wikipedia's footnotes and follow them to the actual scholarly source.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.4 — "identify, sort, and order main ideas and supporting details for writing tasks, using a variety of strategies and selecting the organizational pattern best suited to the content and the purpose for writing."

📝 5.3 MLA Citation and Academic Integrity
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Citation is not the polite manners of academic writing; it is part of its argument. A properly attributed quotation tells the reader where authority comes from and frees you from the suspicion that you are smuggling someone else's ideas as your own. MLA 9th edition is the standard for English studies in Ontario universities and the citation style ENG3U uses for the ISU and most literary work.

MLA in-text citation has one job: it points the reader to the exact place in the source where the cited claim can be found. The format varies by source type. For drama: act, scene, and line numbers, separated by periods — (Macbeth 1.7.1–2). For poetry: line numbers — (Frost 13–14). For prose: page number — (Munro 17). For web sources without page numbers: nothing in the citation, but the author or article title should appear in your signal phrase so the reader can find the source in the Works Cited list. The Works Cited list itself appears on a new page, alphabetical by first element, and includes every source you have cited.

Embedding quotations grammatically into your own sentences is a skill worth practising. A free-standing quotation ("She says: 'Quote.'") is journalistic and weak. An integrated quotation reads as if your sentence and the quoted phrase were written together: "Macbeth describes ambition as a horse that 'o'erleaps itself / And falls on th' other' (1.7.27–28), an image that already contains the catastrophe." Note the use of a forward slash to mark the line break inside the verse quotation, and the citation outside the closing quotation mark. These small conventions add up to a writing style a university grader will recognise as competent on the first page.

Academic integrity is not just about avoiding penalty; it is about being honest in a profession (scholarship) that depends on honesty. Plagiarism is the presentation of another's words or ideas as your own without attribution. The most-common Grade 11 violations are not deliberate fraud; they are sloppy notes (you forgot which words you copied from where), paraphrase without citation (paraphrased ideas still require citation), and AI-generated text used without disclosure. AI writing tools complicate the question further: an Ontario university will treat unattributed AI-generated text the same way it treats unattributed human text — as plagiarism — and your high school is increasingly aligning with that policy. Build the habit now: when in doubt, cite. Citing too generously is a smaller error than citing too sparingly.

  • MLA 9th edition — the citation style for ENG3U literary work.
  • In-text citation — brief parenthetical pointing to the Works Cited entry.
  • Works Cited — full bibliographic list at the end of the essay.
  • Block quotation — quotations of four+ lines of prose, or three+ lines of verse, indented without quotation marks.
  • Embedded quotation — short quote integrated into your sentence's syntax.
  • Signal phrase — language that introduces a quotation and attributes it.
  • Plagiarism — presenting another's words or ideas without attribution.
  • Paraphrase (cited) — restating a source's idea in your own words; still requires citation.

Worked Example — Repairing an MLA Mistake

Before:

"Macbeth says I have done the deed (Shakespeare). This shows he killed Duncan."

This sentence does several things wrong: missing quotation marks around the borrowed words, missing line citation, weak analytical move ("this shows" + paraphrase), and a citation that names the author but not the location.

After:

"Macbeth's terse re-entry from Duncan's chamber — 'I have done the deed' (2.2.15) — uses one of the shortest sentences he speaks in the play; the four-word declarative, all monosyllables, performs the closure he wants the murder to have, and the play spends the next three acts disproving the closure."

The repaired version uses correct MLA drama citation (act.scene.line), embeds the quotation grammatically, supplies analysis (monosyllables, declarative form) rather than paraphrase, and links the local reading to a larger claim about the play. The conventions and the analysis improve together — strong citation habits are part of strong analytical writing, not a separate cosmetic concern.

  • Comma splices, particularly when two clauses sound related.
  • Quoting without a signal phrase, leaving the quotation orphaned.
  • Using page numbers for plays (use act.scene.line) or for poems (use line numbers).
  • Citing the page where you read a quotation in a secondary source instead of the primary source.
  • Forgetting that paraphrased ideas still require citation — paraphrase without attribution is plagiarism.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.5 — "produce pieces of published work that follow the conventions of the chosen form, including credit sources accurately to avoid plagiarism."

🎤 5.4 Presenting Findings
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The ISU usually concludes with an oral presentation as well as a written essay or paper. The presentation is not the essay read aloud; it is a different form of the same argument, condensed for a listening audience and supported by visuals. The Grade 11 ISU presentation is your first sustained experience of academic public speaking; it is also the form that will repeat at university (seminar presentations), in workplaces (briefings), and across public life (PSAs, talks). Treat it as a transferable skill, not a one-time task.

Three planning moves separate a strong presentation from an average one. First, identify the one claim you most want the audience to remember. A talk that tries to convey six claims usually conveys none; a talk that lands a single sharp claim with three supporting reasons is memorable. Decide what your single claim is before you build slides. Second, structure for listening, not reading: short sentences, signposted transitions ("My second reason is…"), repetition of the central claim. The listener cannot rewind; structure does the rewinding for them. Third, rehearse with a clock. Strong presenters know to within thirty seconds how long their talk runs. Weak presenters guess and either run short (sounds underprepared) or run long (gets cut off mid-conclusion).

Slides, when used, must obey two rules. First, they support the talk, not replace it. A slide is a visual cue for the listener and the speaker, not a script. Six words per slide is a reasonable upper bound; one image, one short quotation, or one piece of data reads better than a bulleted paragraph. Second, the design should be invisible — high contrast, consistent typography, no distracting transitions. The audience should leave remembering your argument, not your animations. Print speaker notes as a paper backup in case technology fails; a calm student who continues from paper when the projector dies wins composure points immediately and rarely loses anything else.

The Q&A is part of the presentation, and many students under-prepare for it. After your rehearsals, write down the five hardest questions an attentive listener could ask, and practise answering each. "What is the strongest objection to your thesis?" is almost always one of them. The format for a good Q&A answer is the same as for a good analytical paragraph: paraphrase the question (so the audience knows you understood it), concede the strongest part of it (so the audience trusts you), then refute or extend (so you advance the argument). A student who can do that consistently for ten minutes of Q&A is doing the work that Grade 11 oral communication is meant to develop.

  • Oral presentation — spoken argument delivered to a listening audience.
  • Thesis recap — restating the central claim at the start of the talk.
  • Signposting — explicit transitions that orient the listener ("My second reason…").
  • Pacing — rate of speech and use of pauses for emphasis.
  • Visual aid principles — high contrast, low text, one idea per slide.
  • Rehearsal with timing — running through the talk with a clock multiple times.
  • Q&A protocol — paraphrase, concede, refute or extend.
  • Composure — pause rather than fill silence with filler words.

Worked Example — Handling a Hard Question

A panelist asks:

"You argued that Macbeth shows ambition as contagious, but isn't it possible Lady Macbeth is just a stronger character who pushes a weaker one? Doesn't that make her, not ambition itself, the explanation?"

A weak response: defensive denial ("No, you're misreading"). A strong response: paraphrase, concede, refute, redirect.

"That's a fair question — you're asking whether the catalyst is the character (Lady Macbeth) rather than the idea (ambition). I'll concede that Lady Macbeth's force is decisive in Act 1; the play makes that case strongly. But notice that the imagery of ambition moves in both directions across the play: by Act 3 it is Macbeth who is sustaining the murders, and Lady Macbeth who pulls back ('Things without all remedy / Should be without regard,' 3.2.13–14). If Lady Macbeth were the source of ambition, her hesitation in Act 3 would be inexplicable. My argument is that what we see is the same impulse passing between them — which is the play's specific way of staging contagion."

This response paraphrases, concedes the strongest part of the question, refutes with specific evidence (the Act 3 line, and the bidirectionality of the imagery), and returns to the larger claim. That structure is the Q&A move; it works across topics.

  • Reading slides verbatim instead of speaking from them.
  • Defending against questions instead of engaging them.
  • Filling pauses with "um/like/so" rather than thinking visibly.
  • Refusing to concede any point — concession is strength, not weakness.
  • Going over time — rehearse with a clock and cut ruthlessly.

Curriculum expectation: Strand A, Expectation A2.2 — "communicate in a clear, coherent manner, using a structure and style appropriate to the purpose, the subject matter, and the intended audience."
