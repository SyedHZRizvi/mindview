🧭 2.1 The Writing Process & Thesis Development
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The writing process is recursive, not linear. The textbook diagram — pre-write, draft, revise, edit, publish — is useful as long as you remember that real writers loop back constantly: a draft sentence exposes a flaw in the outline, a revision step reveals that the thesis needs sharpening, an editing pass uncovers a logical gap that requires fresh thinking. Grade 11 is the year to stop treating a first draft as the final draft and to start treating drafting as thinking on paper.

The most consequential moment in the whole process is the thesis. A thesis is not a topic ("This essay is about Macbeth"), not a question ("Was Macbeth a victim?"), and not an obvious truth ("Macbeth is a tragedy"). A thesis is an arguable, specific, defensible claim that names what you will argue and gestures at how you will argue it. "Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth not as the source of her husband's ambition but as the voice that names what he has already imagined, so that her famous 'unsex me here' speech is a confession on his behalf rather than a temptation toward him" — that is a thesis. It can be disagreed with; it commits to specific evidence; it predicts the essay's shape.

Thesis-writing is a skill that improves through specific drafting moves. Move 1: name the topic. Move 2: name a claim about the topic. Move 3: add the so-what — what does this claim let us see about the text? A thesis that stops at Move 1 is a topic statement; one that stops at Move 2 is on the right track but is often too general; one that reaches Move 3 is doing the work the assignment asks for. Strong Grade 11 writers usually write their thesis last, after they have drafted a body section or two and learned what they actually think; weak Grade 11 writers lock in a vague thesis early and never revise it. Treat the thesis as a moving target until late in the process.

Revision and editing are different operations. Revision works on argument, structure, and evidence — large-scale moves like cutting a paragraph, reordering body paragraphs, or sharpening the thesis. Editing works on sentence, syntax, and convention — fragments, comma splices, agreement. Revise first; edit second. Editing a paragraph you are about to delete is a tax on yourself.

  • Pre-writing — brainstorming, mind-mapping, outlining, free-writing.
  • Thesis statement — the central arguable claim of the essay.
  • Outline — the architecture of paragraphs and evidence before drafting.
  • Drafting — committing arguments to prose, knowing they will change.
  • Revision — large-scale changes to argument, structure, and evidence.
  • Editing / proofreading — sentence-level corrections of grammar and convention.
  • Recursive process — looping between stages rather than marching through them.
  • So-what — the analytical payoff that distinguishes a thesis from a topic.

Worked Example — Three Drafts of the Same Thesis

A student writing on Macbeth drafts three versions:

Draft 1: "Macbeth is about ambition."
Draft 2: "Macbeth shows that too much ambition is bad."
Draft 3: "Shakespeare presents ambition in Macbeth not as a character flaw but as a contagious idea — one that infects Lady Macbeth and Macbeth alternately, depending on which of them is currently doing the imagining, so that the play tracks not one ambitious person but the way ambition moves between people."

Draft 1 names a topic. Draft 2 names a value claim too obvious to defend. Draft 3 names a mechanism (ambition as contagious), specifies the textual evidence that will appear (the back-and-forth between the Macbeths), and gives the essay's intellectual stakes (ambition as social rather than individual). Only Draft 3 will produce an essay that earns a Level 4 in Grade 11.

  • Treating the writing process as linear and never revising the thesis.
  • Writing the introduction first and locking in a vague claim.
  • Confusing topic with thesis.
  • Editing for commas before fixing the argument.
  • Skipping the outline because "the ideas are in my head" — they aren't until they are on paper.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C1.3 — "locate and select information to effectively support ideas for writing, using a variety of strategies and a range of print and electronic sources."

📐 2.2 Paragraph Structure (PEEL)
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

The body paragraph is the workhorse of every essay. If the paragraphs are strong, the essay holds together even when the introduction and conclusion are weak; if the paragraphs are weak, no amount of polish at the bookends can rescue it. Grade 11 is the year to make body-paragraph structure automatic, so that your effort can shift to the harder work of choosing the right evidence and developing the right interpretation.

The PEEL scaffold names the four moves every analytical body paragraph must make. Point: a topic sentence that asserts a single, specific, debatable claim about the text. Evidence: a short, embedded quotation with proper citation. Explain: your interpretation of how the evidence supports the point — name the device or technique and say why it produces this effect. Link: a closing sentence that ties this paragraph back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph. Each step has a function; skip any one and the paragraph fails as analysis.

A common Grade 11 mistake is to inflate the paragraph by stacking evidence — two quotations, then three quotations — without growing the explanation in proportion. The rule of thumb: every quotation needs at least two sentences of your own commentary. If a paragraph contains three quotations and four sentences of analysis, two of those quotations are decorative. Cut to the strongest evidence and let your explanation breathe.

The closing Link sentence is harder than it looks. A weak Link restates the Point ("This shows that Macbeth feels guilty") and adds nothing. A strong Link moves the argument one step forward, either by tying the paragraph's specific reading to the larger thesis ("Macbeth's guilt is therefore not the product of his crime but its precondition — he is already half-mourning a king he has not yet killed"), or by setting up the next paragraph's claim ("If Macbeth carries this rehearsal of guilt into Act 2, his wife will need to override it for him"). The Link is where a Level 3 paragraph becomes a Level 4 paragraph; train it deliberately.

  • PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explain, Link.
  • Topic sentence — the analytical claim that opens a body paragraph.
  • Embedded quotation — a short quote integrated into your sentence's syntax.
  • Signal phrase — language that introduces and attributes a quotation.
  • Citation — parenthetical reference to the source (act.scene.line for drama, line for poetry, page for prose).
  • Transition — a phrase that connects one paragraph or sentence to the next.
  • Unity — every sentence in a paragraph supports the same claim.
  • Coherence — the paragraph's logic is visible to the reader.

Worked Example — A PEEL Paragraph in Action

Thesis: In Macbeth Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth's initial confidence as a performance she cannot sustain.

P: Lady Macbeth's famous "unsex me here" speech is delivered with such force that it is easy to mistake it for steadiness, but its imagery is already that of a woman who needs help to do what she is about to attempt. E: She prays for spirits to "fill me from the crown to the toe top-full / Of direst cruelty" (1.5.42–3). Explain: The verb "fill" is the giveaway: she does not claim to be full of cruelty already, only to pray that something else fill her, and the metaphor of a body as an empty vessel admits that the speaker is currently hollow. Shakespeare lets her ferocity and her need to be made ferocious occupy the same sentence. Link: Lady Macbeth's collapse in Act 5 is therefore not a sudden reversal but the playing-out of a borrowed strength that was never her own — the play has been warning us of it from the first line of her soliloquy.

Notice: a single short quotation (eight words), three sentences of explanation that name the verb, the metaphor, and the structural admission, and a Link that connects the paragraph to a larger arc of the play (Act 1 to Act 5). That is the shape of a Level 4 Grade 11 paragraph.

  • Topic sentences that state a fact instead of a claim ("There are soliloquies in Macbeth").
  • Stacking quotations without proportional commentary.
  • Citing without a signal phrase, so the quotation reads as orphaned.
  • Link sentences that restate the Point instead of advancing the argument.
  • Mixing topics inside a single paragraph — one paragraph, one claim.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "write for different purposes and audiences using a variety of literary, informational, and graphic forms."

📑 2.3 Essay Forms: Expository, Persuasive, Personal
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

"Essay" is a family of forms, not a single form. Choosing the right family member is half the assignment. Three forms anchor Grade 11 work: the expository essay, which explains; the persuasive essay, which argues an opinion; and the personal essay, which reflects on lived experience. Later, in literary work, you will write the literary-analysis essay (covered in Unit 3 on Macbeth), which is a kind of argumentative essay applied to a text. The four overlap, but they ask for different burdens of proof.

An expository essay informs and explains. Its thesis is a claim about how something works, what something means, or why something matters. "Photosynthesis converts sunlight to chemical energy in three stages" is an expository thesis: the essay's job is to explain those three stages clearly, in an order the reader can follow. Expository essays prize organisation and clarity above persuasion; the writer's voice is steady and instructional, and emotional appeals are rare. The burden of proof is accuracy.

A persuasive essay wants the reader to do or believe something. "Schools should drop standardised testing because it narrows the curriculum and discriminates against students from lower-income backgrounds" is a persuasive thesis. Persuasive essays use the classical rhetorical appeals — logos (logic), ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion) — in combination. The writer engages a counterargument, takes it seriously, and refutes it. The burden of proof is to move a sceptical reader, ethically, by reasons strong enough that a fair person could change her mind.

A personal essay reflects on an experience or idea from the writer's own life and arrives at an insight a reader can share. "What working a cash register taught me about politeness" is the seed of a personal essay; the form is older than it sounds (Michel de Montaigne invented it in the 1580s), and its conventions are still loose, but its discipline is real. A personal essay is not a diary entry; it shapes private experience into something that earns a stranger's attention. The burden of proof is honesty paired with craft — the experience must be specific, the reflection must be earned, and the writing must not collapse into sentimentality or self-importance. The strongest Grade 11 personal essays sit with the smallest possible incident and write outward from it — a five-minute encounter at a bus stop, a single sentence overheard at home, a moment of failure that lasted ninety seconds — because the small, concrete starting point keeps the writer honest while the reflection does the lifting. Grade 11 is also the right year to begin practising the personal essay because it is the form most often required on university application supplements.

  • Expository — explains; thesis is informative; voice is steady.
  • Persuasive — argues an opinion; uses logos, ethos, pathos; engages counterargument.
  • Personal — reflects on lived experience; arrives at a shareable insight.
  • Literary-analysis — argues an interpretation of a text using textual evidence (Unit 3).
  • Logos / ethos / pathos — appeals to logic, credibility, and emotion.
  • Counterargument — the strongest version of the opposing view.
  • Refutation / concession — answering the opposing view or granting limited ground.
  • Anecdote — a brief specific story used as evidence in personal or persuasive writing.

Worked Example — Same Topic, Three Forms

Topic: cellphones in classrooms.

Expository thesis: "Ontario schools handle classroom cellphone use through three main approaches — full bans during instruction, teacher-discretion models, and structured use for educational tasks — each with documented effects on student attention."
Persuasive thesis: "Although classroom cellphone bans are politically popular, the available research shows that targeted training in self-regulation produces a larger gain in student focus than a blanket ban, so Ontario should invest in self-regulation programs before reaching for prohibition."
Personal thesis: "The first time I put my phone in the box at the front of the classroom, I noticed how my hand kept moving toward my pocket; the surprise of that movement, more than any policy, was what taught me how much of my attention I had been quietly giving away."

Same topic, three burdens of proof. Expository must be accurate. Persuasive must persuade. Personal must honour the experience and earn the reader's interest.

  • Writing a persuasive essay when the assignment asked for expository.
  • Treating "personal" as "anything I want to say" — the form has its own discipline.
  • Hooks that grab attention but do not earn the thesis ("Picture this: a dark, stormy night…").
  • Counterargument that is one sentence ("Some people think otherwise") rather than a paragraph.
  • Conclusions that restate the introduction word for word.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C2.1 — "write for different purposes and audiences using a variety of literary, informational, and graphic forms."

🔧 2.4 Revising and Editing
📖 Lesson Notes — Ontario Curriculum

Revision and editing are different operations, and a Grade 11 essay improves much faster when you treat them as separate passes. Revision works on argument, structure, and evidence: cutting a paragraph, reordering body paragraphs, sharpening the thesis, finding stronger evidence, replacing paraphrase with analysis. Editing works on the sentence level: comma splices, fragments, subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference, word choice. The discipline of the order matters — revise first, edit second, because editing a sentence you will later cut is wasted effort.

Three convention errors disproportionately affect Grade 11 marks. The comma splice joins two independent clauses with only a comma ("Macbeth hesitates, Lady Macbeth pushes him forward") — repair with a period, a semicolon, or a coordinating conjunction. The sentence fragment presents a dependent clause as a sentence ("Because the witches plant the idea.") — repair by attaching to a main clause. Pronoun-antecedent disagreement ("Each character must guard their tongue") used to be a strict error and is now contested in casual usage; at the university level the safe move is to revise to plural ("Characters must guard their tongues") or to rephrase entirely.

Peer review is the single most underused revision technique in Grade 11 classrooms. Trade essays with a partner with two specific questions in mind, not "tell me what you think." Useful questions: "Where did you lose track of my argument?" and "Which paragraph would you cut?" The first is honest about your reader's experience; the second forces a productive disagreement. A peer who answers both questions is a more valuable reader than one who says the essay is good. Equally important: when you read someone else's essay, look for places where their argument drifts, not for typos. Editing for typos in a draft that has not yet been revised is a category error — the typos may not survive the next revision pass anyway.

Reading aloud is the most reliable editing tool for the final pass. The mouth catches what the eye skips. Sentences too long for one breath are usually too long for one thought; awkward verb-tense shifts trip the tongue before the mind notices them; missing commas register as the absence of a pause your reader will not feel. Read every page out loud at least once before submission. The technique is free, takes ten minutes for a 1,000-word essay, and reliably catches errors no other method does.

  • Revision — large-scale changes to argument, structure, evidence.
  • Editing / proofreading — sentence-level corrections.
  • Comma splice — two independent clauses joined only by a comma.
  • Sentence fragment — a dependent clause punctuated as a sentence.
  • Subject-verb agreement — the verb matches the subject in number.
  • Pronoun-antecedent agreement — the pronoun matches the noun it refers to.
  • Concision — saying what you mean in the fewest necessary words.
  • Voice / register — the appropriate level of formality for the audience.

Worked Example — A Revision in Two Passes

Original draft sentence:

"Macbeth is feeling really guilty, the dagger is showing him this, he thinks he can see it but he can't, this shows that his mind is going crazy and he is starting to lose it after killing Duncan."

Pass 1 — Revision: this sentence is doing too many jobs at once (guilt, hallucination, mental decline, consequence of the murder). Split it into two sentences with distinct claims, and replace the vague verbs ("going crazy," "losing it") with specific ones.

"The dagger Macbeth sees but cannot grasp (2.1.33–9) is the first sign that his guilt has begun to outrun his reason. Shakespeare locates the breakdown not after the murder but before it — Macbeth's mind has already split from itself by the time he reaches Duncan's chamber."

Pass 2 — Editing: check the comma in the parenthetical, verify the act.scene.line citation, confirm subject-verb agreement, read aloud for rhythm. Conventions clean; commit.

Two passes, one strong analytical sentence pair replacing one weak run-on. That is the leverage revision gives you.

  • Editing before revising — fixing commas in paragraphs you should cut.
  • Treating peer review as a request for compliments.
  • Trusting spellcheck to catch grammar (it does not).
  • Skipping the read-aloud pass to save time.
  • Leaving the introduction and conclusion as the first draft long after the body has been revised.

Curriculum expectation: Strand C, Expectation C3.1 — "use editing, proofreading, and publishing skills and strategies, and knowledge of language conventions, to correct errors, refine expression, and present their work effectively."
